 Abstract 

 Automatically generating high-performance kernels with LLM-based agents
promises substantial gains in development efficiency, but two obstacles remain.
First, while increasing multi-turn interaction with execution environments is crucial for refining generation quality, continuous context accumulation rapidly exceeds the model’s effective context window. Second, training long-horizon tasks via GRPO inherently incurs sparse rewards due to its reliance on terminal outcome evaluation, severely hindering step-level credit assignment; To address these obstacles, we propose ATRL (Ascend Triton Reinforcement Learning), a post-training method
that refines GRPO into a compaction-aware, segment-level objective for
long-horizon kernel generation. We also design a lightweight agent harness providing cascaded compilation, correctness, and profiling feedback, alongside an Ascend-specific knowledge base injected during agentic RL training and evaluation to compensate for the base model’s missing platform expertise. Evaluations on NPUKernelBench and KernelBench, both
executed on Ascend hardware, show that ATRL consistently surpasses all
baselines, including GRPO, in both correctness and speedup.

 1 Introduction 

 High-performance kernels are critically important in LLMs infrastructure, as they directly accelerate both the training and inference pipelines of LLMs. Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have opened a promising path toward automated kernel generation. A growing body of work trains models to translate high-level PyTorch reference implementations into optimized kernels in domain-specific languages (DSLs) such as Triton  Tillet et al. (2019) , whose tile-based programming model simplifies kernel development and substantially improves developer productivity  Guo et al. (2026) ; Liu et al. (2026) . To further improve generation quality, researchers have adopted an agentic paradigm: instead of producing a kernel through a single-round chat interaction, an LLM-based agent interacts with an execution environment across multiple turns–generating code, receiving compilation and correctness feedback, profiling performance, and iteratively refining its output  Dai et al. (2026) ; Du et al. (2026b) ; Lange et al. (2025) . This multi-turn, tool-augmented workflow has proven substantially more effective than single-round generation, as it enables the model to learn from execution feedback and explore a broader optimization landscape. 

 Despite this progress, two fundamental challenges remain unresolved. First, the multi-turn agentic workflow inherently causes context to grow continuously: each turn appends tool results like view files, steadily inflating the input length. As recent workload characterizations have shown, this unbounded context growth not only degrades the quality of model inference, but also risks exceeding the effective context window, terminating the optimization loop prematurely  Yuan et al. (2026) ; Li et al. (2026) . Existing kernel-generation agents  Dai et al. (2026) ; Liu et al. (2026) either ignore this problem entirely or apply ad hoc truncation that discards potentially valuable debugging history, yielding no principled solution. Second, training long-horizon kernel generation with GRPO relies on a terminal outcome reward. Because credit is assigned only after the full trajectory finishes, intermediate edits receive little supervision, the learning signal remains sparse, and step-level credit assignment becomes unreliable. Standard GRPO therefore struggles to distinguish a useful repair from a later regression across many edit–verify turns. 

 To address these challenges, we propose ATRL (Ascend Triton Reinforcement Learning), a post-training method that refines GRPO for long-horizon kernel generation. ATRL decomposes a long agent trajectory into segments at verifiable boundaries and optimizes the policy with segment-level rewards built on three ideas: (i) structured trajectory compaction , which condenses the accumulated history into a compact checkpoint once the context grows too long; (ii) mixed segment reward , which combines the terminal task success with the verifiable progress measured at each segment’s boundaries; and (iii) task-relative segment advantage , which normalizes segment advantages across rollouts of the same task and weights them segment-wise in the loss. The compaction checkpoint itself participates in training, so the model learns both to preserve state and to continue from it. Throughout, a lightweight agent harness supplies compilation, correctness, and profiling feedback, and an on-demand knowledge base provides Ascend-specific optimization guidance. 

 2 Related Work 

 2.1 LLM-Based Kernel Generation 

 Training-free agentic generation. Several frameworks employ LLM agents that iteratively refine kernels through execution feedback without modifying model weights. AdaExplore  Du et al. (2026b) introduces failure-driven adaptation, synthesizing recurring errors into reusable validity rules, combined with diversity-preserving tree search to escape local optima. The robust-kbench framework  Lange et al. (2025) proposes an evolutionary meta-generation procedure with LLM-based verifiers for correctness filtering, while also exposing exploitable benchmark loopholes. MKEvolve  Yoo et al. (2026) adopts a modular decomposition strategy, iteratively co-evolving sub-kernel decompositions and their implementations to improve interpretability and reduce token consumption. While effective at test time, these approaches do not improve the model’s intrinsic generation ability and incur substantial inference cost per task. 

 Training-based generation. DRTriton  Guo et al. (2026) proposes a synthetic data pipeline (CSP-DAG) with curriculum RL to train models for PyTorch-to-Triton conversion, achieving strong generalization despite training only on synthetic data. Dr. Kernel  Liu et al. (2026) introduces KernelGYM, a distributed GPU environment with reward-hacking detection, and identifies a biased policy-gradient issue in GRPO, proposing Turn-level Reinforce-Leave-One-Out (TRLOO) for unbiased multi-turn advantage estimation. MusaCoder  Cheng et al. (2026) presents an end-to-end alignment pipeline for Moore Threads MUSA kernels. It proceeds from SFT through diversity-preserving RFT to two-stage RL, and introduces PrimeEcho, MirrorPop, and Buffered Dynamic Retry to stabilize training. Kernel-Smith  Du et al. (2026a) combines an evolutionary agent with post-training RL, converting long-horizon trajectories into step-centric supervision, and validates cross-backend transfer between NVIDIA Triton and MetaX MACA. CUDA-Agent  Dai et al. (2026) scales agentic RL with a skill-augmented environment and automated verification/profiling, achieving state-of-the-art results on KernelBench. Compiler-R1  Pan et al. (2025) applies RL to compiler pass-sequence auto-tuning, demonstrating that RL-trained LLMs can optimize IR-level metrics. 

 Despite this rapid progress, existing work is overwhelmingly centered on NVIDIA CUDA/Triton or, more recently, other GPU backends (MetaX MACA, Moore Threads MUSA). None of these methods address Ascend kernel generation , where the Da Vinci architecture introduces fundamentally different compute and memory semantics. Moreover, most approaches treat the multi-turn execution environment as a black box for reward computation, without exploiting the transparency of intermediate verification steps for fine-grained credit assignment. Our work fills both gaps by targeting the Ascend Triton ecosystem and adopting a white-box RL formulation. 

 2.2 Agentic RL 

 Agentic Reinforcement Learning. 
Agentic RL trains language models to improve through multi-turn interaction with tools and stateful environments, rather than optimizing isolated responses.
ArCHer establishes a hierarchical actor-critic formulation that combines turn-level value learning with token-level policy optimization  ( Zhou et al., 2024 ) .
WebRL further addresses task scarcity, sparse feedback, and policy drift in web environments using a self-evolving curriculum and outcome-supervised rewards  ( Qi et al., 2025 ) .
For long-horizon digital agents, LOOP eliminates the learned critic through a leave-one-out baseline, enabling memory-efficient policy optimization directly in target environments  ( Chen et al., 2025 ) .
RAGEN instead studies the dynamics of multi-turn RL and identifies the Echo Trap, in which declining reward variance and policy entropy destabilize learning, motivating its stabilized StarPO-S algorithm  ( Wang et al., 2025 ) . 

 Recent studies address delayed credit assignment and unstable exploration through hierarchical or step-aware policy optimization.
GiGPO combines episode-level advantages with step-level comparisons among actions from equivalent states  ( Feng et al., 2025 ) .
Agent Lightning decomposes arbitrary agent executions into trainable transitions through hierarchical credit assignment  ( Luo et al., 2025 ) , while AgentGym-RL progressively increases interaction horizons to stabilize long-horizon training  ( Xi et al., 2025 ) .
However, these methods are not readily applicable to Ascend kernel generation, where code states rarely recur and rewards are inherently sparse.
In contrast, our framework leverages compiler, correctness, and performance feedback to optimize long-horizon kernel-generation trajectories. 

 3 Preliminary 

 3.1 Agentic Ascend Triton Kernel Generation 

 An Ascend Triton kernel-generation task starts from a reference PyTorch
implementation and a set of test inputs covering the required shapes and data
types. The goal is to produce a Triton kernel that matches the reference
outputs while running efficiently on the Ascend platform. Triton expresses the
kernel as a collection of parallel tile programs  Tillet et al. (2019) ,
which the Ascend backend compiles into an executable kernel. 

 Producing a correct and efficient kernel usually requires several attempts.
The agent writes or modifies the kernel, invokes a unified evaluation tool,
and revises the implementation based on the structured report it
returns—compiler diagnostics, correctness results, and performance
measurements. This consolidated feedback makes the environment white-box,
allowing training to distinguish compilation failures, numerical errors, and
performance bottlenecks rather than relying only on final success; the full
verification cascade is detailed in Section  4.1 and
Appendix  B . 

 Multi-turn optimization also creates an increasingly long interaction
history. Source-code snapshots, compiler logs, correctness reports, and
profiling results accumulate even after they are no longer useful. A long
history increases inference cost, distracts the model with stale information,
and may eventually exhaust its context window. Effective context management
is therefore necessary to sustain long-horizon kernel optimization.

assignment introduced in Section  4 . 

 4 Method 

 We formulate Ascend Triton kernel generation as a long-horizon interaction between a language-model policy and an executable Ascend environment. Starting from a PyTorch reference implementation, the policy generates a kernel implementation, invokes verification tools, and revises the implementation using compiler, correctness, and profiling feedback. Two properties of this setting shape the design of ATRL. First, trajectories are long and grow without bound: a single kernel can require many edit–verify cycles, and the accumulated tool history eventually exceeds the model’s context window. Second, the only naturally available scalar signal is the terminal outcome, which is too coarse to distinguish a useful repair from a later regression. ATRL addresses the first with a correctness-gated optimization workflow (Section  4.1 ) and structured trajectory compaction (Section  4.2 ), and the second with a compaction-aware RL objective that assigns credit to the resulting trajectory segments (Section  4.3 ). Throughout, an on-demand knowledge base supplies Ascend-specific programming and optimization guidance (Section  4.1 ).

 4.1 Agentic Kernel Optimization 

 Interaction loop. 

 The agent operates in an isolated sandbox and alternates between language-model generation and tool execution. Its tools support file inspection and editing, knowledge retrieval, verification, and final submission; the full tool suite and sandbox setup are given in Appendix  A , and the corresponding prompt templates in Appendix  E . Tool outputs are returned as observations in the next model context, allowing the policy to repair compilation errors, correct numerical mismatches, and optimize measured latency without requiring a complete kernel in a single response. 

 Correctness-gated optimization. 

 We decouple correctness establishment from performance tuning by gating access to optimization guidance. During implementation , the agent identifies the kernel semantics from the PyTorch reference, retrieves relevant API documentation, and iterates until the candidate passes verification. During optimization , the agent uses profiling signals to select transformations such as tiling, multi-core partitioning, or improved memory access, while re-running correctness tests after every change. The gate prevents the policy from trading correctness for apparent speed. Near the turn limit, the harness may inject a brief reminder to create or verify an implementation; the reminder is also written to the rollout buffer so that inference and training observe the same sequence. 

 On-demand knowledge retrieval. 

 The sandbox exposes a structured knowledge base of Ascend-specific guidance covering hardware constraints, Triton APIs and their Ascend extensions, example kernels, and optimization strategies, retrieved through a judge–retrieve–apply chain that bounds token consumption. Before writing the kernel, the policy consults hardware constraints, examples, and API documentation to ground the initial implementation; during optimization, profiling signals guide the choice among transformations. Profiling reports diagnose symptoms—for example, low vector utilization or memory-bound execution—but do not prescribe an optimization, leaving the policy to connect each observation to an appropriate transformation. The retrieval interface and corpus maintenance are described in Appendix  A.3 . 

 Cascaded verification. 

 Each verification call executes a four-stage cascade—static analysis, compilation, numerical correctness, and hardware benchmarking—and stops at the first failed stage, so invalid candidates never reach the accelerator. The ordering is itself a safeguard: submissions that bypass the Triton kernel are rejected before any execution, and benchmarking runs only after every correctness case passes. Each stage emits a structured report that doubles as the environment observation. Stage definitions, precision criteria, and integrity safeguards are given in Appendix  B .

 4.2 Structured Trajectory Compaction 

 The iterative workflow above can produce trajectories that exceed the model’s context window, making an explicit mechanism for preserving execution state necessary. Truncating old messages discards precisely the failures and constraints needed for subsequent repair. Instead, after a verification call and once context usage crosses a threshold, we ask the rollout model to replace the accumulated history with a structured checkpoint. The checkpoint records seven types of state: attempted approaches, discovered constraints, design decisions, errors and fixes, the current implementation, the latest verification result, and one immediate next action. 

 After compaction, the next context contains the unchanged system instruction and task, followed by the checkpoint and a short continuation instruction. The sandbox workspace and implementation files remain unchanged, so execution continues from the same environment state. Compaction is attempted only under conservative trigger conditions, and a checkpoint is accepted only if it is non-empty and shorter than the history it replaces. 

 Compaction partitions rollout j j into I j + 1 I_{j}+1 segments,
 τ j = { τ 1 j , … , τ I j + 1 j } \tau^{j}=\{\tau_{1}^{j},\ldots,\tau_{I_{j}+1}^{j}\} . At each boundary we store the code, verification status, and performance measurements. These snapshots allow us to measure what changed within a segment. We deliberately place the generated checkpoint in two positions: it is part of the preceding segment’s response, where RL can supervise its generation, and part of the following segment’s prompt, where it conditions continued execution. This dual role makes checkpoint quality itself optimizable while teaching the policy to preserve the state required for later kernel revisions. Trigger thresholds, prompt format, and failure-handling details appear in Appendix  C .

 4.3 Compaction-Aware Reinforcement Learning 

 Compaction changes the unit of training from one uninterrupted trajectory to several state-dependent segments. Standard trajectory-level GRPO assigns one terminal advantage to every generated token and cannot distinguish a useful repair from a later regression. We therefore combine global task success with the measured progress of each segment.

 Effect of the agent harness. 

 We compare the same multi-turn loop with versus without the harness: cascaded static analysis, compilation, numerical correctness, and benchmarking; correctness-gated access to profiling; and on-demand retrieval from the Ascend knowledge base through the judge–retrieve–apply chain.
On NPUKernelBench the largest relative gain is on Level 2 ( 2 2 to 13 13 of 31 31 Pass@1), where operators are both fused and hardware-specific; Qwen3.8-27B, a stronger starting point, still improves on Levels 0, 2, and 3, while Level 1 remains comparable.
On KernelBench, enabling the harness raises frozen Qwen3.6-35B from 49 % 49\% to 71 % 71\% Pass@1 on Level 1 and from 7 % 7\% to 34 % 34\% on Level 2, and raises Fast@1 on Level 1 from 19 % 19\% to 32 % 32\% . On Level 3, Pass@1 falls from 4 4 to 2 2 of 50 50 and Fast@1 from 1 1 to 0 0 . This multi-turn comparison is separate from the Qwen3.6-35B row in Table  1 .
This supports the claim that the harness, including retrieval from the Ascend knowledge base, supplies checks and Triton-Ascend APIs that the base model does not apply on its own. 

 6 Conclusion 

 We introduced ATRL, a compaction-aware reinforcement learning method for long-horizon Ascend Triton kernel generation. ATRL preserves execution state through structured checkpoints and incorporates checkpoint generation into policy training. By combining terminal outcomes with verifiable progress at segment boundaries, it provides finer-grained credit assignment than outcome-supervised GRPO. A lightweight agent harness and an Ascend-specific knowledge base support iterative implementation, verification, and optimization. On KernelBench, ATRL improves Pass@1 over GRPO from 66 % 66\% to 73 % 73\% on Level 1 and from 19 % 19\% to 35 % 35\% on Level 2, with additional correctness gains on NPUKernelBench. Although performance gains vary across benchmark levels, these results support jointly designing context management and reward assignment for long-horizon kernel-generation agents. Future work will investigate stronger performance-oriented credit assignment and extend the approach to additional hardware backends.
