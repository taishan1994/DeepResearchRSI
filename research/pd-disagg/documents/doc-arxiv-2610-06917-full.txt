 Abstract 

 Prefill-decode disaggregation is becoming a common architecture for LLM serving because it separates two phases with distinct execution patterns and SLO objectives. Existing systems typically combine a fixed prefill/decode worker ratio with request routing across workers. However, real-world workloads exhibit both short bursts and sustained shifts in the prefill-to-decode demand ratio. As a result, a configuration that is well provisioned at one time may quickly become mismatched, causing latency SLO violations even when idle capacity exists elsewhere. Existing autoscaling mechanisms can add capacity, but they react slowly, require spare GPUs, and do not directly address short-timescale phase imbalance. 

 We present FluidPD, a P/D-disaggregated serving system that provides SLO-aware in-place elasticity. FluidPD introduces two complementary mechanisms. FluidToken handles transient imbalance by offloading a bounded portion of prefill computation to decode workers when decode-side slack is available. FluidRole handles sustained imbalance by reassigning running workers between prefill and decode roles in place, avoiding model reload and engine restart. Both mechanisms are guided by lightweight pressure indices that expose prefill and decode-side resource pressure before they appear as SLO violations.
Across production Azure trace workloads, FluidPD improves overall SLO attainment over static SGLang by up to 94.6 percentage points, demonstrating that SLO-aware in-place P/D elasticity improves service quality without provisioning additional workers. 

 I Introduction 

 Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in interactive applications, such as chat assistant  [ 32 ] , coding agent  [ 33 , 5 , 14 ] , and tool-using workflows  [ 50 , 34 , 30 ] .
These applications have strict latency demands on both initial response time and continuous token generation.
Prefill-decode (P/D) disaggregation has become a common architecture for meeting these demands in LLM serving  [ 35 , 54 ] . By placing prefill and decode on separate worker pools, it allows each phase to be provisioned and optimized around its own execution pattern and Service Level Objectives (SLOs).
Prefill processes the input prompt and largely determines Time-To-First-Token (TTFT), whereas decode generates output tokens iteratively and determines Time-Per-Output-Token (TPOT).
This separation makes P/D disaggregation effective for high-throughput, SLO-aware LLM serving. 

 However, this separation also makes capacity difficult to move across phases.
In a typical deployment, the numbers of prefill and decode workers are chosen ahead of time, often through offline workload profiling  [ 35 , 54 ] , and each worker remains tied to its assigned role at runtime.
This static design has limited flexibility under dynamic workloads. Production LLM workloads exhibit both short-term bursts and sustained shifts in request mix  [ 42 ] . A burst can temporarily overload prefill workers when many prompts arrive within a short interval  [ 47 ] , whereas a sustained shift can change the aggregate prefill-to-decode demand ratio as the workload mix moves, for example, between prompt-heavy coding requests and longer interactive conversations  [ 54 ] .
Under a static P/D partition, routing can balance requests only among workers already assigned to the required phase  [ 54 , 38 ] .
It cannot use idle decode-side capacity to relieve prefill pressure, nor can it reassign underutilized prefill capacity when decode demand grows. Consequently, the system may violate latency SLOs even when sufficient capacity remains available elsewhere in the running deployment. 

 Autoscaling is a natural response to changing demand and is increasingly supported by production LLM serving platforms  [ 9 , 1 , 27 ] . It is effective when the running deployment lacks sufficient aggregate capacity, but is less suitable when capacity is merely distributed poorly between prefill and decode. Scale-out requires spare GPUs and incurs detection, provisioning, model loading, and runtime initialization delays. These delays are too long for short bursts, while scaling out for sustained phase imbalance adds resources even when the less-pressured pool already has available capacity. In our Dynamo  [ 9 ] experiment, a new worker becomes ready ∼ \sim 200 s after scale-out is triggered, long after the burst has ended. We call this missing capability in-place elasticity : adapting the prefill/decode capacity available within the running deployment before changing its serving world size. 

 We present FluidPD, the first P/D-disaggregated serving system to combine SLO-aware token-level work stealing for transient pressure with bidirectional in-place role switching for sustained phase imbalance. FluidPD adapts the prefill and decode capacity of a fixed set of running workers without changing the serving world size. For short-lived prefill pressure, FluidToken offloads a bounded suffix of selected prefill requests to their paired decode workers, allowing available decode-side capacity to reduce the prefill backlog without changing worker roles. When phase pressure persists, FluidRole performs lightweight, bidirectional role switching between prefill and decode, rebalancing the P/D worker split within the running deployment without dropping in-flight requests or provisioning additional workers.
Both mechanisms are guided by two predictive pressure signals derived from internal engine state.
The Prefill Pressure Index (PPI) estimates whether queued prefill requests can meet their remaining TTFT budgets, while the Decode Pressure Index (DPI) captures decode execution pressure and KV-cache admission headroom.
Unlike observed TTFT and TPOT, which reveal pressure only after it has affected request latency, these signals expose emerging prefill and decode pressure early enough to guide adaptation.
Together, FluidPD is able to respond to both bursts and sustained workload shifts using capacity already available in the deployment.
In summary, this paper makes the following contributions: 

 • 

 We show that static P/D partitions can lose SLO attainment under both short bursts and sustained demand shifts, even when aggregate capacity remains sufficient. Autoscaling is too slow for transient pressure and too coarse for phase imbalance. 

 • 

 We present FluidPD, the first P/D-disaggregated serving system to combine SLO-aware token-level work stealing with lightweight, bidirectional in-place role switching. Its decisions are guided by predictive PPI and DPI signals derived from internal engine state, allowing it to respond to transient and persistent pressure before latency violations happen. 

 • 

 We implement FluidPD on SGLang and evaluate it on production-derived Azure workloads across three LLMs. FluidPD improves SLO attainment over SGLang by up to 94.6 percentage points under the same GPU allocation, and outperforms Dynamo autoscaling by up to 88.3 percentage points while using fewer GPUs. 

 II Related Work 

 A large body of work improves LLM serving efficiency through advanced batching and scheduling  [ 11 , 3 , 52 ] , KV-cache and memory management  [ 20 , 53 , 13 , 23 ] , optimized attention and decoding kernels  [ 10 , 51 , 37 , 4 , 31 , 46 ] , CPU offloading  [ 49 , 40 ] , model parallelism and pipelining frameworks  [ 24 , 36 ] , and speculative decoding  [ 28 , 41 ] . Other systems use request ordering, admission control, and batch selection to meet latency SLOs  [ 15 , 7 , 44 , 19 , 8 ] .
These techniques improve the efficiency and scheduling of co-located prefill–decode execution. FluidPD instead targets P/D-disaggregation, where prefill and decode run on separate worker pools. 

 To better accommodate the different compute characteristics of the prefill and decode phases, recent work has proposed P/D-disaggregation, which assigns the two phases to separate GPU workers or worker pools.
Systems such as Splitwise  [ 35 ] , TetriInfer  [ 16 ] , Llumnix  [ 43 ] , and DistServe  [ 54 ] show that this separation can improve goodput and TTFT and ITL SLO attainment while enabling phase-specific execution policies.
Subsequent work introduces additional optimizations for provisioning, scheduling, and KV-cache management  [ 38 , 16 ] .
Industry studies further show that the preferred P:D partition depends on the workload and can change over time  [ 29 , 22 ] .
However, these designs primarily optimize a given P/D configuration and do not continuously adapt the running capacity split as phase demand changes. 

 Several systems adapt phase capacity by changing worker roles or serving groups. Arrow  [ 48 ] routes requests over stateless instances and reallocates instances between prefill and decode. DOPD  [ 26 ] adjusts instance allocation toward a predicted target P/D ratio, while P/D-Serve  [ 18 ] supports dynamic P/D organization at deployment scale. ThunderServe  [ 17 ] uses lightweight rescheduling to change phase assignments and request orchestration in heterogeneous cloud environments.
At a broader deployment level, NVIDIA Dynamo  [ 9 ] , AIBrix  [ 1 ] , and llm-d  [ 27 ] provide autoscaling, routing, KV-cache management, and orchestration for distributed inference. These systems adapt capacity at worker or serving-group granularity, but do not exploit token-level work stealing for short-lived phase pressure. 

 Other systems share capacity across the P/D boundary without first reconfiguring whole workers. TaiChi  [ 45 ] and WindServe  [ 12 ] support mixed prefill–decode execution within an instance, while TokenScale  [ 21 ] uses pre-provisioned convertible decode workers to absorb prefill bursts. Libra  [ 39 ] , Kairos  [ 6 ] , and PPD  [ 25 ] further move request execution across phase boundaries at token, request, or conversational-turn granularity. These systems provide either mixed execution or specialized cross-phase offloading, but do not combine SLO-bounded token-level work stealing for
transient pressure with in-place worker-role adaptation for sustained changes in the P/D demand ratio. 

 III Observations and Opportunities 

 P/D-disaggregated serving uses separate prefill and decode worker pools, but a fixed capacity split may not track changing workloads. We show that short bursts and sustained demand shifts both create phase imbalance. 

 Fig. 1 : Time-varying input/output ratios in Azure workloads. 

 III-A Sustained Demand Shifts Break SLOs under Static P/D Splits 

 Different LLM applications produce substantially different prompt and generation characteristics.
Prompt-heavy workloads generally consume relatively more prefill capacity, whereas generation-heavy workloads place greater relative demand on decode. Consequently, changes in the application mix can shift the effective P/D capacity split required by the workload over time.
Fig.  1 shows the diurnal variation using one day from the Azure trace  [ 35 ] .
We observe that coding requests are concentrated during business hours and tend to be more prefill-heavy, while conversational requests are distributed more evenly throughout the day and contain relatively more decode work. As the workload mix changes over the day, the aggregate input-to-output token ratio varies substantially, from 16.9 to 49.4. 

 A static P/D deployment cannot adapt to this shift because worker capacity is bound to phase-specific roles.
As demand moves between prefill and decode, a fixed P/D partition can become poorly matched to the workload even when aggregate capacity is sufficient.
To measure the SLO impact of this workload shift, we construct Azure (staged) , a controlled workload with three consecutive 10k-request windows that follow an Azure-conversation, Azure-code, Azure-conversation sequence; its construction is described in detail in §  V-A .
We evaluate the workload using Llama-3.1-8B at a constant 40 RPS Poisson arrival rate on an 8 × \times A100-80GB server and compare 4P4D, 5P3D, and 6P2D deployments, using TTFT and TPOT SLOs of 2000ms and 100ms.
We define the oracle P:D ratio for each window as the static ratio with the highest overall SLO attainment.
Fig.  2 shows that no single static partition performs well across both workload types: 4P4D reaches 86.93% SLO attainment on Azure-conversation but only 3.40% on Azure-code. In contrast, 6P2D reaches 99.40% on Azure-code but only 6.64% on Azure-conversation. The oracle performs well by selecting a different P/D split for each window, which shows that the SLO loss comes from P/D imbalance rather than insufficient total capacity. 

 Fig. 2 : Sustained shifts favor different static P/D ratios. 

 III-B Short Bursts Create Transient P/D Imbalance 

 Workload variation also occurs at a much shorter timescale. We define a burst as a brief interval in which arrival or token demand rises sharply. Unlike the sustained shifts in §  III-A , a burst does not necessarily change the long-term workload mix.
To measure the effect of such bursts, we construct Azure (mixed) , a 10K-request workload composed of Azure-code and Azure-conversation requests; its construction is described in detail in §  V-A . We replay it using Llama-3.1-8B on 8 × \times A100-80GB under a static 4P4D deployment, with TTFT and TPOT SLOs of 2000 ms and 100 ms, respectively. Fig.  3 shows the resulting burst-induced prefill pressure.
During the marked burst, input-token demand rises from a median of 98.0K to 109.3K tokens/s. The burst rapidly builds a prefill waiting queue, causing TTFT to exceed its SLO.
More importantly, the impact of this burst lasts longer than the burst itself.
New requests arriving during and after the burst must wait behind the accumulated prefill backlog, so TTFT remains high even after input-token demand begins to fall.
Meanwhile, TPOT remains within its SLO throughout the interval.
Requests enter decode only after completing prefill, and their output tokens are produced iteratively rather than arriving as one prompt-sized unit of computation.
This immediate burst pressure therefore accumulates primarily in the prefill queue, while already admitted decode streams continue to make progress within their per-token latency budget.
This behavior exposes another limitation of static P/D serving: a short burst can overload the prefill pool while decode workers still have slack, yet phase-specialized workers cannot use that capacity to reduce the prefill backlog. Moreover, the pressure may disappear before changing the cluster-level P/D split becomes worthwhile, which motivates a fine-grained adaptation method that temporarily exploits decode-side slack without changing worker roles. 

 Fig. 3 : Transient bursts build prefill queueing, causing TTFT violations while TPOT remains within SLO. 

 III-C Autoscaling Is Often Coarse for P/D Imbalance 

 Autoscaling is a common approach to handling changes in inference demand.
Systems such as Dynamo, AIBrix, and llm-d can add or remove inference
workers as load changes  [ 9 , 1 , 27 ] .
This capability is important when total workload demand changes over long timescales,
but it is less suitable for the two forms of phase imbalance identified above. 

 For short bursts, autoscaling incurs both detection and actuation delay.
The controller must first observe a lagging symptom, such as latency degradation or high resource utilization. By the time the scale-out condition is detected, the burst may already have produced a substantial backlog.
After the decision, the newly allocated capacity must be provisioned before it can serve requests. 

 To quantify these delays, we deploy Llama-3.1-8B with Dynamo’s SGLang backend and disaggregated planner on the same Azure (mixed) workload. Starting from a 3P3D deployment under an 8-GPU budget, Dynamo issues a scale-out decision roughly 5 s after the burst begins. However, the added worker becomes ready only about 200 s later: approximately 100 s elapses before Kubernetes creates the pod, including scheduling and resource provisioning, and a further ∼ \sim 100 s is spent initializing the inference runtime, loading the model weights, and capturing CUDA graphs before the worker can serve requests. The burst, and in fact even the synthetic workload, has long ended before the added capacity becomes available, making autoscaling unable to mitigate the resulting prefill backlog and TTFT violations. 

 Autoscaling is also unnecessarily expensive when the problem is a
phase-capacity imbalance rather than insufficient aggregate capacity. One worker pool may be overloaded while the other has substantial slack. Adding another worker increases the serving world size and consumes additional GPUs, even though sufficient resources may already exist inside the running deployment. Autoscaling is therefore well suited to aggregate-capacity shortages, but can be too slow for short bursts and too coarse when capacity is imbalanced across prefill and decode.

 IV System Design 

 FluidPD provides in-place elasticity for P/D-disaggregated LLM serving. Its goal is to adapt the effective prefill/decode capacity available to a running deployment without immediately changing the serving world size. 

 IV-A Overview 

 Fig.  5 shows the architecture of FluidPD. FluidPD builds on the standard P/D-disaggregated structure with separate prefill and decode worker pools and a router that assigns each request to a prefill/decode worker pair. FluidPD then introduces two elasticity mechanisms.
FluidToken provides request-level adaptation. When a prefill worker estimates that its queued requests are at risk of violating TTFT, it can ask the paired decode worker to execute a suffix of selected prefill requests. This token-level work stealing reduces prefill queuing delay without changing worker roles. Since the stolen work runs on a decode worker, FluidToken allows stealing only when the decode worker has sufficient slack and memory headroom, preventing TTFT gains from causing TPOT violations or decode-side backpressure. 

 FluidRole provides worker-level adaptation. When phase pressure persists, FluidPD changes the effective P/D
ratio by switching selected workers between prefill and decode roles in place. Unlike auto-scaling, FluidRole keeps model weights and runtime state, and changes the active role of an existing worker after draining in-flight work.
The two mechanisms are coordinated by pressure-guided control, i.e., Prefill Pressure Index (PPI) and Decode Pressure Index (DPI). FluidToken is used first because it can relieve transient prefill pressure without changing worker roles. FluidRole is invoked when pressure persists, indicating that the current P/D partition is no longer suitable for the workload. Together, they allow FluidPD to handle both short bursts and sustained P/D demand shifts within the running deployment, while reserving autoscaling for cases where the workload exceeds the available cluster capacity. 

 IV-B FluidToken: Pressure-Guided SLO-Aware Token Stealing

 IV-B 1 Token-Stealing Execution 

 FluidToken steals work from prefill to decode, targeting the short-term bursts in §  III-B , where prefill queues build while decode-side slack remains available. It implements this stealing as a partial-prefill handoff along the request’s existing P/D execution flow, as shown in Fig.  6 .
For a request i i with prefill length L i L_{i} , FluidToken selects a stealing ratio β i ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \beta_{i}\in[0,1] using the policy in §  IV-B3 , yielding a prefill-side prefix of length L pfx = ( 1 − β i ) ​ L i L_{\mathrm{pfx}}=(1-\beta_{i})L_{i} and a decode-side suffix of length L sfx = β i ​ L i L_{\mathrm{sfx}}=\beta_{i}L_{i} . 

 After the prefill worker computes the prefix and transfers its KV states, the decode worker executes the suffix by treating the transferred KV as prior context, as in chunked prefill  [ 3 ] . Each suffix token therefore observes the same causal mask and position ordering as it would in an unsplit prefill, making split execution mathematically equivalent to unsplit. After prefilling the suffix, the decode worker generates the first token and inserts the request into the decode batch. 

 As shown in Fig.  6 , FluidToken reuses SGLang’s async P/D KV-transfer path  [ 53 ] . During the prefix-KV transfer, both workers continue processing other requests, overlapping the handoff with concurrent prefill and decode execution. FluidToken therefore redistributes rather than reduces prompt computation. Its benefit comes from moving part of the queued prefill work to a decode worker with available slack, allowing the prefill worker to begin subsequent requests earlier. Fig.  7 illustrates this effect. FluidToken splits request R ​ 2 R2 , and schedules just the prefix R ​ 2 pfx R2_{\text{pfx}} on the prefill worker. This allows request R ​ 3 R3 to start prefill earlier and meet its TTFT SLO. 

 Fig. 6 : FluidToken execution flow for prefix KV handoff and stolen-suffix execution. 

 Executing a stolen suffix introduces two decode-side costs. First, the suffix forward pass temporarily defers the next decode iteration. Second, earlier admission of the stolen request increases decode-side KV occupancy and may reduce the worker’s headroom for subsequent requests. 

 To address this, FluidToken admits stolen work only when the paired decode worker has sufficient TPOT slack and KV-cache headroom. The pressure signals and policy that enforce these conditions and determine the maximum admissible suffix length are described next. 

 IV-B 2 Prefill-Decode Pressure Signals 

 FluidToken uses two phase-specific signals. The Prefill Pressure Index (PPI) predicts whether queued requests are at risk of missing their TTFT SLOs and therefore whether token stealing is needed. The Decode Pressure Index (DPI) captures whether the paired decode worker has sufficient resource and admission headroom to accept stolen work. 

 Fig. 7 : FluidToken splits request R ​ 2 R2 so R ​ 3 R3 can begin prefill earlier and meet its TTFT SLO. 

 Directly using observed TTFT and TPOT alone is insufficient for this decision. They are lagging indicators, and the same latency symptom can arise from different bottlenecks. In particular, elevated TTFT may result either from prefill overload or from decode-side admission backpressure. In P/D-disaggregated serving such as SGLang  [ 53 ] , the decode worker reserves KV-cache capacity before prefill execution begins. Insufficient decode-side KV-cache headroom can block a request from entering prefill, even when prefill compute remains available. FluidToken therefore uses PPI and DPI to distinguish prefill deadline pressure from decode-side pressure before an SLO violation occurs.

 IV-C FluidRole: In-Place Role Switching 

 FluidToken handles transient prefill pressure without changing worker roles. When phase imbalance persists, FluidRole changes the effective P/D worker ratio by switching an existing worker between prefill and decode roles in place. 

 IV-C 1 Role-State Expansion and Bidirectional Live-Role Migration 

 FluidRole extends the runtime with a role-switching state machine (Fig.  8 ) comprised of three states:
 Active ,
 Drain , and
 Switch .
In Active , the worker executes the scheduler and KV-transfer path of its current role. Upon receiving a switch request,
the worker transitions through three states:
 Active ( old role ) → \rightarrow Drain → \rightarrow Switch → \rightarrow Active ( new role ).
It first enters Drain , where it stops accepting new work and waits for in-flight operations to reach a safe boundary. It then enters Switch , where FluidRole activates the target role’s queues, transfer handlers, and scheduler state. 

 FluidRole changes a worker’s serving role while preserving its model placement and parallelism configuration. The worker therefore reuses its existing model-parallel communication groups throughout the transition.
When switching into the decode role, FluidRole initializes the required decode CUDA graphs within the existing worker process, without restarting the engine or reloading model weights. In the current implementation, these graphs
are captured on the first switch into decode and remain cached for subsequent switches, so only the first transition incurs the capture overhead. Capturing the graphs for both roles at startup would eliminate this first-switch cost,
at the expense of additional GPU memory for retaining both sets of CUDA graph state.
The worker finally returns to Active and resumes serving under the new role. 

 Fig. 8 : FluidRole role-switching state machine. 

 FluidRole switches roles without dropping requests or causing downtime, even when the selected worker is serving requests under its current role. Unlike restart-based reconfiguration, role switching avoids the downtime caused by process startup, model loading, and distributed initialization, but still requires the worker to drain role-specific in-flight work before the transition can complete.
The key idea is to reassign work that has not yet started while draining work that is already in progress. Queued requests can be removed from the worker and
reassigned to another worker with the same current role.
In-progress requests remain on the selected worker until they reach a safe transition boundary. Because the amount and duration of in-progress work differ between prefill and decode, the resulting drain cost is directional. 

 For a P → \rightarrow D switch, the worker stops admitting new prefill requests, reassigns queued requests that have not started execution, completes its current prefill batch, and flushes any outstanding KV transfers. Its drain time is therefore bounded primarily by the remaining prefill execution and non-overlapped KV-transfer time. For a D → \rightarrow P switch, the worker stops admitting new decode requests and allows its active requests to finish before activating the prefill role. These requests already hold local KV state and may continue generating until EOS or the configured maximum generation length. Because their remaining output lengths are unknown at switch time, the drain time depends on the longest remaining active sequence and can be substantially larger than for a P → \rightarrow D switch. We quantify these overheads in §  V-H .

If both phases exhibit persistent pressure, FluidRole does not move capacity between them, since relieving one bottleneck would worsen the other.
This condition indicates insufficient aggregate serving capacity and could instead be handled through cluster-level admission control or autoscaling. 

 FluidRole commits to a role switch only when the same switch recommendation persists for N persist N_{\mathrm{persist}} consecutive intervals. This prevents short-lived pressure fluctuations from triggering role changes.
Once this happens, FluidRole selects the worker with the smallest estimated drain cost. For D → \rightarrow P transitions, where the remaining output lengths are unknown, FluidRole uses the number of active decode requests as a lightweight proxy for drain time. The controller switches one worker at a time and favors the candidate expected to reach the safe switching boundary with the least disruption.
After a switch, the controller enforces a cooldown period T c ​ o ​ o ​ l ​ d ​ o ​ w ​ n T_{cooldown} before considering another transition, allowing the new P/D configuration to take effect.
Together, N persist N_{\mathrm{persist}} and T cooldown T_{\mathrm{cooldown}} prevent rapid or repeated role changes when workload demand fluctuates near the decision thresholds. 

 V Evaluation 

 V-A Evaluation Methodology 

 Implementation: We implement FluidPD on top of SGLang [ 53 ] , a production-grade LLM serving engine with P/D disaggregation support. FluidPD extends SGLang’s disaggregated runtime (v0.5.4) with FluidToken and FluidRole while preserving SGLang’s standard P/D execution flow, including prefill-side scheduling, decode-side KV-cache preallocation, asynchronous KV transfer, and CUDA Graph execution. A deployment with N N prefill workers and M M decode workers is denoted as N N P M M D.
Our current predictor implementation relies on offline profiling. Online recalibration from observed execution times is a natural extension for tracking runtime variation and workload drift. 

 Models and workloads: We evaluate FluidPD on three LLMs with different serving characteristics: Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-14B, and Qwen3-30B-A3B (MoE).
We evaluate FluidPD using workloads derived from the Azure LLM Inference trace  [ 42 ] , which provides separate Azure-code and Azure-conversation traces spanning one week each.
These traces contain substantial variation in request mix, arrival rate, and short-timescale arrival burstiness.
Because replaying the complete 168-hour trace for every configuration is prohibitively expensive, we construct two representative workloads. Azure (staged) contains 30K requests arranged as three 10K-request stages: Azure-conversation, Azure-code, and Azure-conversation. 

 Azure-code is more prefill-heavy because its requests tend to have longer prompts and shorter generations, whereas Azure-conversation places relatively more pressure on decode through longer output streams. The staged workload therefore captures sustained shifts in the preferred P:D ratio. Azure (mixed) contains 10K naturally interleaved requests from a bursty trace window,
capturing the short-timescale variation described in §  III-B .
We replay both workloads at controlled request rates.

 (b) TTFT and TPOT contribution on Llama-3.1-8B for Azure (mixed). 

 (c) FluidPD-static versus Dynamo-autoscaling on Azure workloads. 

 Fig. 9 : Main results on Azure workloads across Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-14B, and Qwen3-30B-A3B MoE. 

 Baselines and configurations: We compare FluidPD against SGLang v0.5.4, vLLM v0.26.0, and Dynamo v1.3.0. The SGLang and vLLM baselines use static 4P4D deployments, while FluidPD starts from the same 4P4D allocation and adapts its effective P:D capacity in place. We evaluate Dynamo both as a static 4P4D deployment under the same 8-GPU budget and, in a separate autoscaling comparison, as a 6-GPU deployment that can grow to 8 GPUs. All evaluated configurations use round-robin request routing. We do not directly compare with TaiChi, TokenScale, or Libra  [ 39 ] (DynaServe in its preprint version) because no public implementations were available at the time of evaluation. 

 Metrics: We use overall SLO attainment as the primary metric, following   [ 54 , 35 , 48 ] . TTFT and TPOT SLOs define upper bounds for the corresponding latency metrics, and attainment rate is the percentage of requests satisfying each bound.
Overall SLO attainment is the percentage of requests that satisfy both bounds. In the main results, TPOT SLO is 100 ms for all models, while TTFT SLO is 1000 ms for Qwen3-30B-A3B, 2000 ms for Llama-3.1-8B, and 3000 ms for Qwen3-14B. 

 Hardware testbed: All experiments run on an AWS server with 8 × \times NVIDIA A100-80GB GPUs interconnected via NVLink, using one GPU per serving instance. 

 V-B Main Results 

 Fig.  9(a) evaluates whether in-place P/D elasticity improves SLO attainment over static 8-GPU deployments as the offered request rate increases. Across both Azure workloads, FluidPD improves overall SLO attainment under pressure. Compared with static SGLang, FluidPD improves overall SLO attainment by up to 94.6 percentage points on Azure (staged) and by up to 74.3 percentage points on Azure (mixed).
The largest gains appear at low and moderate offered loads, where the fixed baselines become mismatched to workload phase demand even though the deployment still has usable aggregate capacity. FluidPD can exploit this headroom by reassigning workers for sustained shifts and temporarily borrowing decode-side slack for shorter bursts. At higher offered loads, the deployment approaches saturation, leaving less spare capacity for either mechanism. 

 Fig.  9(b) breaks down this effect for Llama-3.1-8B on Azure (mixed).
In SGLang’s P/D-disaggregated execution, both sustained phase shifts and transient bursts first create admission-side pressure at the prefill workers, increasing TTFT violation. As a result, FluidPD’s overall SLO improvement primarily comes from higher TTFT attainment, while TPOT attainment remains close to the baselines through most of the sweep.
At the highest offered load, FluidPD’s TPOT attainment decreases slightly. This happens because partial-prefill execution on decode workers consumes decode-side slack; once the system is saturated, stolen prefill work can interfere with decode iterations enough to create TPOT violations. 

 We next compare FluidPD with autoscaling to determine whether adding workers can address the same P/D imbalance that FluidPD resolves within the running deployment. We run Llama-3.1-8B on Azure (mixed) and Azure (staged). Both systems start from the same 6-GPU 3P3D configuration. FluidPD remains within these six GPUs, whereas Dynamo may scale the deployment to eight GPUs.
Fig.  9(c) shows that autoscaling reacts slowly to the imbalance timescales in both workloads. On Azure (mixed), the dominant event is a short prefill-heavy burst. At 20 RPS, FluidPD achieves 97.6% overall SLO attainment, compared with 9.3% for Dynamo. Dynamo initiates scale-out after the burst begins, but the new worker becomes available only hundreds of seconds later, after the burst has already caused substantial TTFT violations. In contrast, FluidPD responds within the existing deployment: FluidToken borrows available decode-side slack to execute bounded prefill work and reduce queueing without waiting for additional workers.
Azure (staged) contains a longer-lasting demand shift and is therefore more favorable to autoscaling. Nevertheless, at 20 RPS, FluidPD achieves 92.6% overall SLO attainment, whereas Dynamo achieves 5.2%. Under Dynamo’s active planner, the deployment repeatedly changes its P/D allocation, including removing workers and later adding them back, and does not converge quickly to a configuration that matches the workload. FluidPD instead adjusts the P/D split among already-running workers through FluidRole and uses FluidToken to absorb shorter pressure intervals. 

 V-C Why FluidPD Helps: Time-Series Breakdown 

 We examine the time-series behavior of FluidPD on Azure (staged). The initial 4P4D allocation is a good fit for Azure-conversation and keeps prefill queueing low. When the workload shifts to Azure-code,
input-token demand increases and the same allocation becomes prefill-constrained.
FluidPD responds to this sustained imbalance through FluidRole, which performs the observed 4P4D → \rightarrow 5P3D transition.
The additional prefill worker bounds queue growth, allowing p95 TTFT to recover below the SLO after the workload returns to Azure-conversation. In contrast, SGLang cannot adjust its P/D allocation: its prefill queue grows throughout the Azure-code stage and remains elevated through the end of the trace. During shorter pressure intervals that do not justify a role switch, FluidToken can instead borrow decode-side slack for bounded prefill work without changing the worker allocation.
SGLang cancels requests that wait more than 60 s for their first token. These requests therefore leave the queue as failures, producing the saw-tooth pattern in the static TTFT timeline rather than a continuously increasing curve.

 V-F Hyperparameter Sensitivity 

 FluidPD exposes several control parameters that determine how aggressively it reuses capacity across phases. For FluidToken, one important parameter is the budget factor η \eta , which scales the TPOT-derived suffix-execution bound. A larger η \eta relaxes this bound, allowing the solver to admit larger stolen suffixes to reduce prefill queueing but introduces decode interference. In our runs, we find η = 1.25 \eta=1.25 to balance this trade-off.
For FluidRole, the key parameters are the PPI and DPI thresholds that control when the controller changes the P:D allocation. These thresholds are important because they directly control reaction time: overly conservative values delay adaptation, while overly aggressive values can cause churn. We find PPI-low = 0.6 =0.6 , PPI-high = 1.0 =1.0 , DPI-load = 0.6 =0.6 , and DPI-admission = 0.2 =0.2 to be a good combination. 

 V-G Predictor Model Accuracy 

 FluidPD uses lightweight latency predictors to estimate prefill execution, stolen-suffix execution, and decode-iteration latency. We calibrate these predictors offline for each model and hardware configuration evaluated in this work. The controller does not require a universally valid analytical model. It only requires sufficiently accurate estimates over the operating range of the deployed configuration. Fig.  13 compares predicted and measured execution times over the profiled range. The predictions closely track the measurements, with relative error within 2–10% for most profiled points. This accuracy is sufficient for online control because FluidPD uses the predictions to enforce conservative stealing and role-switching constraints, rather than to estimate exact end-to-end request latency. 

 Fig. 13 : Accuracy of the latency models used by FluidPD. MRE denotes mean relative error. 

 V-H Control and Switching Overheads 

 We also measure the runtime overhead of FluidPD’s online control logic. Computing PPI, DPI, and suffix-stealing budgets uses scheduler metadata already maintained by SGLang, including queue state, request lengths, decode batch size, context length, and KV-cache occupancy. The resulting CPU-side overhead is less than 0.50 ms per control iteration. SGLang’s overlap scheduler pipelines CPU-side scheduling with GPU execution, so this overhead is largely hidden from the prefill and decode critical paths. FluidToken introduces additional GPU work only when a stolen suffix executes on a decode worker. This cost is included in the end-to-end latency and SLO results.
FluidRole additionally incurs the time required for the selected worker to reach a safe transition boundary. A P → \rightarrow D transition takes approximately 1 s, dominated by completing the current prefill and flushing outstanding KV transfers. A D → \rightarrow P transition takes up to 12 s, depending on the active decode requests that must finish before the worker can change roles. These transitions preserve admitted requests and avoid process restart and model reloading. 

 VI Conclusion 

 In this work, we present FluidPD, a P/D-disaggregated LLM serving system that adapts capacity in place under both transient bursts and sustained shifts in phase demand. FluidToken uses SLO-aware token-level work stealing to exploit decode-side slack, while FluidRole rebalances the running P/D worker split through in-place role switching. Guided by predictive pressure signals, FluidPD improves overall SLO attainment by up to 94.6 percentage points on Azure trace workloads while largely preserving TPOT attainment outside overloaded ranges. These results show that P/D imbalance can often be addressed using capacity already present in the deployment, before resorting to additional GPUs.
