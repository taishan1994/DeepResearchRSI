 Abstract. 

 With growing emphasis on (text-only) LLM serving,
disaggregating the two stages: Prefill and Decode onto separate GPU pools
is now a standard optimization.
However, multimodal LLMs (MLLMs), which add a third phase, Encode , pose new
challenges for optimizing resource allocation.
 Encode turns images, video, or audio into embeddings that the language model can consume,
yielding a three-stage Encode–Prefill–Decode (EPD) pipeline, which requires new research for
optimizing resource allocation and utilization. Existing frameworks offer only partial answers:
text-only PD systems lack Encode , while EPD frameworks expose it as a separate service
without regulating downstream request flow.
New challenges arise because the three stage pipeline carries a structural resource imbalance:
every request enters through Encode before downstream work can begin,
yet per-request execution leaves the encode GPU severely underutilized even at high loads,
and this idle capacity starves the downstream Prefill and Decode workers. 

 Addressing this challenge,
we reposition Encode as the control point of the EPD pipeline,
exposing three tightly coupled dimensions for optimization:
when work enters downstream, where prefill executes, and how the GPU is shared.
We instantiate this in EAServe across two co-designed layers.
Its runtime manages load-adaptive micro-batching, rate-controlled partial offload to a co-resident prefill worker,
and dynamic SM partitioning for predictable co-location.
The configuration layer, Hybrid Auto Selection (HAS), then navigates the joint space of GPU allocation,
encode batch size, and offload ratio by pruning unbalanced allocations
with per-stage capacity profiling and refining the remainder through TPE-based Bayesian optimization.
In our extensive evaluation on three MLLM architectures spanning image, video, and audio, EAServe delivers up to 4.3 × \times and 1.7 × \times higher goodput than NVIDIA Dynamo and vLLM, respectively, under identical SLO constraints,
sustains substantially more balanced and higher GPU utilization across the EPD pipeline,
while reaching near-optimal disaggregated configurations faster than baseline search methods. 

 Keywords:  Multimodal large language models, Disaggregated serving, GPU resource allocation, SM partitioning, Request scheduling

 1. Introduction 

 Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) are rapidly moving
from research prototypes to deployed services,
as they now power interactive assistants, visual question answering,
document understanding, video understanding, and speech-based applications  ( Man et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2023 ; Bai et al., 2023 ; Li et al., 2024 ; Fixie AI, 2024 ) .
As deployment grows, the systems challenge is shifting from supporting MLLMs at all
to serving them efficiently at scale under latency objectives and fluctuating demand  ( Dong et al., 2025 ) . 

 Serving an MLLM efficiently is more challenging than serving a text-only LLM
because the pipeline is more heterogeneous.
Text-only inference splits into a compute-bound Prefill 
and a memory-bandwidth-bound Decode , or PD,
and this clean split is what makes disaggregating the two onto independently scaled
GPU pools ( PD separation ) effective in practice  ( Zhong et al., 2024 ; Patel et al., 2024 ) .
MLLMs add a third stage in front of this pipeline. Specifically,
a modality encoder , typically a transformer, turns images, video,
or audio into embeddings that the language model can then consume.
The result is the
encode-prefill-decode (EPD) pipeline, which is widely used in modern MLLM
deployments  ( NVIDIA, 2025 ) .
The new Encode stage’s
compute and bandwidth demands vary with modality, input size, and arrival pattern.
This extra axis of heterogeneity makes EPD harder to schedule than PD. 

 Despite being on the critical path, Encode has received less attention
from recent work than the two stages following it.
Text-only PD frameworks  ( Zhong et al., 2024 ; Patel et al., 2024 ) 
expose only a token-level interface and coordinate Prefill with Decode .
EPD frameworks such as NVIDIA Dynamo  ( NVIDIA, 2025 ) 
factor Encode out as a separate service but operate it as a pass-through:
one request at a time, with operator-set GPU allocation and no mechanism for regulating
how work enters the rest of the pipeline.
The cost of this passivity is that
a single encoder forward pass is too small to saturate a modern GPU,
yet batching is constrained by the latency cost of waiting for a larger batch to form.
The stage that gates pipeline admission is therefore also the one that leaves the most capacity unused:
under sustained load, the encode GPU stays largely idle
while downstream workers wait on a growing encode queue and tail latency inflates  ( NVIDIA, 2025 ) . 

 We argue that Encode should instead be repositioned as the pipeline’s control point .
As the pipeline’s entry, it naturally controls three coupled dimensions:
 when work enters downstream, where prefill executes,
and how the GPU is shared under co-location.
Co-optimization is needed as tuning any of them in isolation breaks the others,
and yet challenging, as joint space is too large to search by hand.
With this motivation, this paper presents EAServe , a layered serving architecture built around this control point.
At the top, Hybrid Auto Selection (HAS) performs offline configuration search over the
joint space of GPU allocation, encode batch bound, and prefill offload ratio.
At the bottom, three encode-aware runtime mechanisms expose the parameters that HAS
searches and enforce the latency guarantees that make the search results reliable.
Different mechanisms dominate on different modalities,
motivating both the runtime co-design and the joint search. 

 In all,
this paper makes the following contributions. 

 • 

 We identify Encode as the natural control point
of disaggregated MLLM serving: both the entry point of the pipeline
and a major source of unused GPU capacity. 

 • 

 We design Hybrid Auto Selection (HAS), an offline framework that
automatically picks the deployment configuration of the EPD pipeline for
a given serving workload. 

 • 

 We design three encode-aware runtime mechanisms that turn HAS’s chosen configuration
into a working serving stack and adapt to load online with no per-deployment tuning:
load-adaptive micro-batching with a Poisson-gap dispatch rule,
rate-controlled partial offload to a local prefill worker,
and dynamic per-micro-batch Streaming Multiprocessor (SM) partitioning. 

 • 

 We implement EAServe and show significant speedups
over the state-of-the-art production-level baselines. 

 We evaluate EAServe on three representative MLLMs covering the main input
modalities: LLaVA-v1.6-34B   ( Liu et al., 2023 ) for image, Qwen2.5-VL-32B   ( Bai et al., 2025 ) for video,
and Ultravox-v0.6-27B   ( Fixie AI, 2024 ) for audio.
Experiments run on an 8-GPU server under a range of TTFT/TPOT latency targets,
and we compare against the state-of-the-art baselines:
NVIDIA Dynamo  ( NVIDIA, 2025 ) and vLLM  ( Kwon et al., 2023 ) .
Across these workloads, EAServe improves goodput (SLO-compliant request rate)
by up to 4.3 × 4.3\times over Dynamo and 1.7 × 1.7\times over vLLM,
and yields more balanced GPU utilization across the EPD pipeline. 

 2. Background and Motivation 

 2.1. EPD Pipeline And Metrics 

 An MLLM extends a text-only LLM with non-text inputs such as images, video, or audio.
Broadly, MLLMs include
a modality encoder (e.g., CLIP ViT  ( Radford et al., 2021 ) for images, Whisper  ( Radford et al., 2023 ) for audio)
that maps the raw input into embedding vectors,
a lightweight connector that projects them into the LLM’s input space,
and the LLM backbone that consumes them alongside text tokens to generate text autoregressively.
Disaggregated MLLM serving thus has three stages
that scale on independent GPU pools (Figure  1 ):
 Encode runs the encoder and connector to produce modality embeddings ;
 Prefill executes the backbone on the concatenation of modality embeddings and text prompt,
populates the key–value (KV) cache, and emits the first output token;
 Decode generates the remaining tokens autoregressively from the context.

 This decomposition results in stages with heterogeneous resource profiles:
 Encode performs short forward passes;
 Prefill is a single attention-heavy pass whose cost grows with context length;
and Decode runs a long, memory-bandwidth-bound autoregressive loop.
Independent scaling lets each stage be provisioned independently  ( NVIDIA, 2025 ) ,
at the cost of carrying intermediate state (modality embeddings and KV cache) across workers.
Within a stage, when the model exceeds one GPU,
weights are usually sharded with tensor parallelism (TP), the standard intra-replica strategy in latency-sensitive serving; we denote TP = k \mathrm{TP}{=}k for k k -way sharding.
In all, we denote the resulting GPU allocation across the three stages by ( E , P , D ) (E,P,D) ,
which, together with the choice to enable co-located workers,
specifies the deployment configuration for a given (model, workload, cluster) combination. 

 A configuration’s quality cannot be judged by throughput alone,
since the EPD pipeline is expected to meet user-facing latency targets at every stage.
Following prior LLM-serving work  ( Zhong et al., 2024 ; Patel et al., 2024 ; Kwon et al., 2023 ) ,
we therefore use latency and SLO-aware metrics.
 Time-to-first-token (TTFT) is the elapsed time from request arrival to the first output token;
in an EPD pipeline it covers encode, embedding transfer, prefill, and any queueing along the path.
 Time-per-output-token (TPOT) is the mean interval between consecutive output tokens during Decode .
We report both at the mean and the 99th-percentile tail (P99).
Following DistServe  ( Zhong et al., 2024 ) ,
we summarize end-to-end performance with goodput , the rate of requests that satisfy the SLO: 

 (1) |

We adopt goodput as the primary metric in our evaluation (§ 4 ),

 2.2. Why Encode Becomes a System Bottleneck 

 As the entry point of the pipeline, Encode gates the downstream stages;
yet a single encode pass rarely places enough work on the GPU to keep it busy.
We illustrate the resulting inefficiency with two measurements taken on a standard EPD deployment
(Figure  2 ). Full hardware, model, and workload details appear in § 4.1 . 

 Observation 1. Encode gates the pipeline yet leaves substantial GPU capacity unused. 
Encode batching is constrained by request arrivals and the latency cost of waiting for a larger batch to form,
so the system cannot grow the batch size at will.
Individual encoder passes are consequently too small to saturate the GPU: both compute and high-bandwidth memory (HBM) bandwidth utilization remain low (as shown in Figure  2 , Baseline).
At sustained arrival rates the per-request encoder throughput falls below the offered load,
so downstream Prefill and Decode workers spend most of their time waiting on a growing encode queue
rather than processing embeddings. 

 Observation 2. Micro-batching helps but does not close the gap. 
Adding a simple micro-batcher ( B = 8 B{=}8 ) raises throughput and lowers TTFT (Table  1 ),
but mean TPOT rises sharply because the accelerated encode floods the downstream Prefill 
and Decode workers.
The encode GPU itself remains far from saturated,
alternating between brief bursts and long idle gaps (Figure  2 , B = 8 B{=}8 ).
Micro-batching captures part of the available gain but does not resolve the underlying imbalance.

 These two observations suggest three design choices for an encode-aware system.
First, a dispatch policy must be decided that adapts the encode batch bound to the
current arrival rate, so the dispatcher tracks load rather than waits on a static timeout.
Second, an offload mechanism is needed that channels the encode-GPU’s idle capacity
into a co-located prefill worker without slowing encode itself.
Third, a resource-sharing primitive must keep the two co-located workers
isolated as the batch size changes.
The three need to be co-optimized: the batch bound shapes the SM split that
protects latency, the SM split shifts how much offload the local worker can
accept, and any reallocation across ( E , P , D ) (E,P,D) rebalances all three.
The resulting joint configuration is too large to tune by hand for each new
(model, workload, cluster), which is the challenge our work addresses.

 2.3. SM Partitioning 

 A modern GPU offers hundreds of TFLOPS of compute and terabytes per second of HBM bandwidth,
and this has motivated co-locating two workloads on one GPU for full utilization.
Existing primitives form a hierarchy of increasing isolation strength, with implications
for how workloads can interfere with one another.
The default CUDA scheduler simply time-slices the full SM array,
so only one process runs at a time and concurrency is lost.
CUDA Multi-Process Service (MPS)  ( NVIDIA, 2024 ) merges kernels from multiple processes into a single CUDA context
to enable concurrent execution, optionally with per-client compute-percentage hints.
However,
the hints are advisory, and a long-running kernel from one client
can still delay latency-sensitive work in another,
so this solution raises throughput without protecting tail latency.
The strongest option is spatial partitioning ,
which assigns disjoint physical resources to each process.
NVIDIA Multi-Instance GPU (MIG) does this in hardware on A100/H100-class GPUs
and partitions both SMs and HBM bandwidth,
but its reconfiguration is heavyweight and it is unavailable on the consumer
and workstation GPUs widely used for MLLM serving.
Software-level spatial SM partitioning via libsmctrl   ( Bakita and Anderson, 2023 ) fills this gap
by writing per-process Texture Processing Cluster (TPC) masks (Figure  3 ):
a process executes only on its allotted SMs, and the partition can be reconfigured in under a microsecond.
What it cannot isolate, however, is HBM bandwidth, since the SM subsets still share a single memory bus,
and whichever process issues more traffic displaces the other’s. 

 Software SM partitioning is a natural fit for the encode-side underutilization identified above.
 Encode and Prefill are both compute-bound transformer forward passes,
so co-locating them under a TPC-mask split divides compute proportionally and protects encode latency,
while sub-microsecond reconfiguration lets the split track the encode batch size as it changes online.
The same primitive, however, also imposes two constraints that shape the rest of our design.
First, the HBM-bandwidth limitation rules out placing Decode on the encode GPU,
since it streams the KV cache token by token and would saturate the shared bus regardless of the SM split.
 Decode , therefore, remains on a dedicated GPU in our design.
Second, the right partition ratio depends on the current encode batch bound, which varies online,
making static split ineffective.
Specifically, the resulting split also has to absorb the local prefill load determined by the offload ratio,
which couples the SM-split decision to the dispatch and offload mechanisms developed next.

 3. System Design 

 Motivated by the discussion in previous section,
 EAServe is an encode-aware EPD serving framework organized in two co-designed layers. As shown in Figure  4 , at the top, Hybrid Auto Selection (HAS) searches the joint
configuration P ​ C = ( alloc , B , s ) PC=(\mathrm{alloc},B,s) offline,
jointly choosing the GPU allocation, encode batch bound, and prefill offload ratio.
At the bottom, three encode-aware runtime mechanisms realize the chosen
 P ​ C PC online by controlling when encode dispatches,
 where each request’s prefill runs,
and how the encode GPU is shared with a co-resident local prefill worker.
We describe each layer in detail in the following subsections, and
Table  2 lists the symbols used in the rest of this section. 

 3.1. Hybrid Auto Selection 

 This critical layer, abbreviated as
HAS, automates the selection of the configuration (or P ​ C PC ) for each deployment target,
replacing the manual configuration that operators would otherwise perform.
Given the GPU budget (or count) G G , the model ℳ \mathcal{M} , the workload 𝒟 \mathcal{D} ,
and the target arrival rate Λ \Lambda ,
HAS returns the deployment configuration that maximizes throughput: 

 (2) | 
 | 
 P ​ C ⋆ = arg ⁡ max P ​ C ⁡ Throughput ⁡ ( P ​ C ) , PC^{\star}\;=\;\arg\max_{PC}\;\mathrm{Throughput}(PC), | 
 | 

 with ties broken by mean TTFT, then mean TPOT.
The search runs once offline, for each combination of GPU budget, model, workload, and target-rate,
and its output is then used for configuring the system at the runtime. 

 Existing serving stacks automate only parts of this search:
text-only PD systems such as DistServe  ( Zhong et al., 2024 ) 
and Splitwise  ( Patel et al., 2024 ) 
search stage allocation but have no Encode to tune,
while EPD frameworks such as Dynamo  ( NVIDIA, 2025 ) run Encode 
as a pass-through with B B and s s fixed to 1 rather than exposed as tunable parameters.
Searching for the configuration manually is impractical here, because the three components of P ​ C PC are tightly coupled:
the SM split that protects encode latency depends on the encode batch bound B B ;
the SM partitioner enforces this split at runtime.
The SM split, in turn, shifts the offload ratio s s that keeps the pipeline balanced,
since the local prefill worker’s compute capacity depends on its SM share;
and any change to alloc \mathrm{alloc} rebalances all three simultaneously.
Even on our 8-GPU testbed, the resulting space contains hundreds of valid configurations,
and the count grows exponentially with the GPU budget.
Exhaustive search is prohibitive because every candidate
requires a full deployment trial of several minutes on our 8-GPU testbed,
adding up to hours or longer for a single workload.
Purely analytical modeling can avoid this cost, but it is very challenging
to capture model and hardware characteristics fully. 

 Our key insight, and the central design contribution of HAS,
is that the configuration space has an internal asymmetry
that can be exploited to combine analytical reasoning and empirical search effectively.
The first-order effect of alloc \mathrm{alloc} is monotonic
and predictable from per-stage capacity alone:
if any stage is starved, the pipeline has a bottleneck there
regardless of how B B and s s are chosen,
so such allocations can be ruled out without ever being deployed.
The interaction between B B and s s , by contrast, is non-monotonic
within the set of feasible allocations
and is best located by direct end-to-end measurement.
HAS therefore splits the work between two stages (Algorithm  1 ):
Stage 1 (lines 1–12) profiles each stage’s per-worker capacity in parallel on disjoint GPU subsets,
discards allocations whose dominant stage already exceeds capacity at the target rate,
and retains the top- K K candidates by a bottleneck-balance score.
Stage 2 (lines 13–20) seeds a Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE)
study around each survivor’s analytical optimum and
refines it through end-to-end trials, stopping once the top configurations stabilize.
This split is what lets HAS span the full coupled ( alloc , B , s ) (\mathrm{alloc},B,s) space
in tens of trials rather than hundreds of full-system deployments,
ranking configurations by throughput, with mean TTFT and mean TPOT as tie-breakers
when configurations achieve comparable throughput.

 Stage 1: Profiling and Pruning. 
The role of Stage 1 is to analytically answer the question “is this allocation worth considering?” .
The enabling observation is that per-stage capacity is a property
of the combination of model, hardware, and workload, and is independent of how the GPUs are partitioned.
Thus,
each stage can be profiled once on a small GPU subset
and the resulting capacities reused to score every candidate allocation.
Encode profiling adaptively doubles B B until throughput saturates,
producing a saturation capacity C E C_{E} that captures the batching response.
Prefill and Decode profiling each run a stage-targeted stress workload
on an isolated worker, yielding per-worker capacities C P C_{P} and C D C_{D} .
The three profiles run in parallel on disjoint GPU subsets
and together cost less than a single end-to-end trial.

 3.2. Load-Adaptive Micro-Batching 

 HAS sets the encode batch bound B B offline;
the micro-batcher’s runtime task is to decide
 when to dispatch a partial batch that has not yet reached B B .
Each forward pass of a vision encoder at small B B is dominated by kernel-launch
and scheduling overhead, so batching is necessary for achieving high throughput.
At the same time, every additional request the dispatcher waits for adds latency to the critical path.
An effective dispatch policy needs to identify this trade-off point automatically across
different arrival rates. 

 We want a threshold that tightens under high load and relaxes under low load,
without per-workload retuning.
Standard dispatch policies  ( Crankshaw et al., 2017 ; NVIDIA, 2025 ) fall short here.
A fixed timeout grows mistuned whenever load shifts.
Queue-length triggers cannot tell a transient lull from the end of a burst,
holding batches open too long at low load.
Continuous batching  ( Yu et al., 2022 ) sidesteps the question entirely,
since it applies only to autoregressive decoding. 

 Poisson-gap dispatch rule. 
Our key insight is that the dispatch decision is fundamentally a property
of the arrival process itself,
so we derive the dispatch threshold directly from that process
rather than treating it as a free parameter.
Let λ = Λ / n E \lambda=\Lambda/n_{E} be the per-encode-worker arrival rate,
and let p ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) p\in(0,1) be the target probability of filling a batch before dispatch.
Modeling arrivals as Poisson
(§ 4.5 reports sensitivity under non-Poisson arrivals),
inter-arrival times are exponentially distributed:
 Pr ⁡ ( X ≤ g ) = 1 − e − λ ​ g \Pr(X\leq g)=1-e^{-\lambda g} .
Starting from the first enqueued request,
let X 1 , … , X B − 1 X_{1},\ldots,X_{B-1} denote the gaps between successive arrivals;
we require each of these gaps to be at most g g for the batch to fill: 

 | 
 Pr ⁡ ( max ⁡ { X 1 , … , X B − 1 } ≤ g ) = ( 1 − e − λ ​ g ) B − 1 . \Pr\bigl(\max\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{B-1}\}\leq g\bigr)=\bigl(1-e^{-\lambda g}\bigr)^{B-1}. | 
 | 

 Setting this equal to p p and solving: 

 (5) |

 The result has the desired property g ∝ 1 / λ g\propto 1/\lambda :
the threshold tightens automatically under high load,
when batches fill quickly.
It also relaxes under low load,
when latency pressure is lower,
with no per-workload retuning.
We assume λ \lambda is known from the deployment’s target arrival rate;
in production where λ \lambda drifts, κ \kappa can be recomputed periodically
from a windowed arrival-rate estimate.
The single tuning constant p p trades batch fullness against tail latency;
we use p = 0.6 p{=}0.6 throughout, which lies at the knee of this trade-off,
and performance is insensitive to the choice within [ 0.5 , 0.7 ] [0.5,0.7] . 

 Given g g , the batcher dispatches when any of three conditions is satisfied:
the batch reaches size B B , the idle-gap timer (reset on every arrival) exceeds g g ,
or the batch’s age from its first request exceeds a hard cap A = 1000 A=1000  ms.
The hard cap is not intended for the common case;
it bounds the worst-case TTFT under pathological arrival patterns
in which each request arrives just before g g expires
and would otherwise keep a batch open for g ⁡ ( B − 1 ) g(B{-}1) seconds.
The complete dispatch condition is

 where B cur B_{\text{cur}} is the set of requests currently in the micro-batch.

and groups arrivals into variable-sized batches without manual tuning;
the batcher adds O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) work per arrival and incurs sub-microsecond timer overhead. 

 3.3. Rate-Controlled Partial Offload 

 Once the micro-batcher has decided when an encode batch dispatches,
the next question is where each request’s prefill should run.
Partial offload realizes this routing decision through encode’s spatial tuning parameter s ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] s\in[0,1] ,
the target fraction of requests sent to a remote Prefill worker
rather than to a co-resident local prefill worker on the encode GPU.
We co-locate prefill, rather than decode, with encode for the reason established in § 2.3 ;
remote Prefill and all Decode workers therefore run on dedicated GPUs.

 The router’s role is to make s s meaningful at runtime.
If the actual routing pattern drifts from the configured ratio,
or if requests reach one path in bursts,
the latency observed by HAS reflects routing noise rather than the intended setting.
Two natural baselines both fail this test:
randomized routing (each request goes remote with probability s s )
is noisy at short horizons and can produce long runs of consecutive remote decisions
even when s s is moderate;
windowed routing (send exactly ⌊ s ​ W ⌋ \lfloor sW\rfloor of every W W requests remote)
trades randomness for a new tuning parameter W W 
whose appropriate value is workload-dependent.
We therefore adopt a parameter-free scheme whose worst-case routing deviation is bounded analytically. 

 Deficit-controlled routing. 
Our solution is a counter-based scheme inspired by Deficit Round Robin  ( Shreedhar and Varghese, 1996 ) 
that converges exactly to s s in the long run
and bounds the worst-case run length per direction,
without introducing any new tuning parameter.
Where DRR maintains per-flow deficit counters to schedule packets among competing queues,
we collapse the same idea into a single scalar that splits a request stream
into a continuous-valued ratio between two paths.
The resulting bounded-spacing property
is what makes the offload ratio s s chosen by HAS observable as latency at runtime,
the property a black-box search like Stage 2 needs.
The router maintains a real-valued accumulator d d , initialized to 0 0 ;
for each request it adds s s to d d ,
sends the request remotely if d ≥ 1 d\geq 1 (and decrements d d by 1 1 ),
and locally otherwise (Figure  6 ).
Two properties follow.
First, after n n requests the cumulative remote count is ⌊ n ​ s + d 0 ⌋ \lfloor ns+d_{0}\rfloor 
for some d 0 ∈ [ 0 , 1 ) d_{0}\in[0,1) ,
so it stays within ± 1 \pm 1 of n ​ s ns for all n n and converges exactly to fraction s s .
Second, the counter never produces more than ⌈ 1 / ( 1 − s ) ⌉ \lceil 1/(1{-}s)\rceil consecutive remote decisions
or ⌈ 1 / s ⌉ \lceil 1/s\rceil consecutive local decisions,
which keeps the downstream queues smoother than randomized routing.
The router adds O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) work per request. 

 Envelope-based embedding transfer. 
After encode, each request’s vision embedding has to be delivered to its assigned prefill worker.
Sending one embedding at a time would pay the transfer setup cost on every request.
Instead, we group all embeddings for the same prefill worker into one contiguous tensor
and ship them as a single NIXL  ( NVIDIA Corporation, 2025 ) transfer per destination,
so a micro-batch of N N requests issues at most one transfer per prefill worker,
rather than N N separate ones.
A small concurrency cap on in-flight sends provides backpressure
and bounds memory use without stalling the pipeline.

 Once the router has decided where each request’s prefill runs,
the encode GPU may host both an encode worker and a co-resident local prefill worker.
We still need to decide how the GPU is split between them.
This split involves the following tradeoff:
encode is on the critical path and should not be slowed below its latency target,
while any compute encode does not use is wasted unless given to local prefill.
This balance is workload-dependent,
so the split has to be set per micro-batch rather than statically. 

 Our key insight is that the encode latency-versus-SM curve saturates:
encode latency drops sharply as it receives more SMs,
then flattens once it has enough to meet its target (Figure  7 ).
This means there is a well-defined smallest safe SM share for each batch size,
and any SM above that point can be released to local prefill at no cost to encode quality of service (QoS).
We therefore re-split the SMs at every micro-batch:
look up the smallest safe share for the current batch,
give it to encode, and hand the remainder to local prefill.
The split adapts to batch size with no new tuning parameter. 

 We materialize the smallest safe share as a lookup table indexed by SM-allocation level,
built once at startup.
Building it amounts to profiling encode latency
at a small set of SM-allocation levels (5 in our deployment) across a range of batch sizes,
and recording, for each SM level, the largest batch size
whose latency stays close to the exclusive-access baseline.
The cost is modest: each profiling point is a single encode-only forward pass,
so the full sweep runs in minutes on one GPU and produces only a handful of table entries.
The table depends only on the encode model, GPU, and workload,
so it is reused across deployment rates and offload ratios,
and its storage and lookup cost at runtime are negligible. 

 At runtime, the encode worker selects the smallest SM share whose supported batch size covers the current micro-batch size,
and the local prefill worker takes the complementary fraction
(e.g., 70% for encode and 30% for prefill).
Both processes install the split through TPC masks via libsmctrl   ( Bakita and Anderson, 2023 ) ,
which reconfigures in sub-microsecond and requires no kernel restart.
Small batches therefore release more SMs to local prefill,
while large batches reclaim them for encode (Figure  8 ).
On workloads where HAS selects s = 1 s{=}1 (e.g., video in our evaluation),
local prefill is not co-located and dynamic SM partitioning collapses to a
single-tenant encode allocation, so it incurs no overhead when its benefit is small.

 4.1. Experimental Setup 

 Models and workloads. 
We evaluate three MLLM architectures spanning image, video, and audio,
with different encoders and LLM backbones (Table  3 ).
For image and video, we use the standard random multimodal workload generator
from the vLLM benchmarking suite  ( Kwon et al., 2023 ) ,
which produces synthetic inputs at realistic shapes
paired with random text prompts of typical length.
For audio, we draw real speech samples from LibriSpeech  ( Panayotov et al., 2015 ) 
with random text prompts.
This mix follows established serving-benchmark practice  ( Zhong et al., 2024 ; Patel et al., 2024 ; Dong et al., 2025 ) 
and isolates serving-system performance from dataset-specific content effects. 

 Testbed and baselines. 
Our primary testbed is a server
with two AMD EPYC 9254 CPUs (48 cores, 96 threads),
1 TB DRAM, and eight NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada GPUs (48 GB each, PCIe, CUDA 13.0),
and Ubuntu 22.04 LTS.
Each encode worker occupies one GPU at TP = 1 {=}1 ,
while Prefill and Decode workers span 2 or 4 GPUs (TP = 2 {=}2 or TP = 4 {=}4 ),
since the 27B–34B backbones require TP ≥ 2 {\geq}2 to fit on a single 48 GB GPU.
Together with the choice of encode batch bound B B and prefill offload ratio s s ,
the resulting joint configuration space contains hundreds of valid points even at 8 GPUs.
Both Dynamo’s manual sweep and HAS’s automated search draw from the same space.
This setup captures the encode-prefill-decode imbalance our design targets. Multi-node scaling and cross-rack KV transfer will be topics for future efforts.
When local prefill is enabled, it shares the encode GPUs through SM partitioning
following the dynamic split rule of § 3.4 .
We compare four system configurations under the same 8-GPU budget:
 vLLM  (v0.14.1) and Dynamo  (v0.9.0);
 Ours ( s = 1 s{=}1 ) , our system with s s fixed to 1 (no local prefill); and
 Ours (HAS-tuned) , our system with HAS-tuned s s (local prefill enabled with dynamic SM partitioning when s < 1 s{<}1 ).
All baselines use their best-performing configurations on this testbed.
We further evaluate EAServe against two EPD-serving systems,
EPDServe  ( Singh et al., 2025 ) and HydraInfer  ( Dong et al., 2025 ) ,
on two eight-GPU platforms: RTX 6000 Ada and A100 SXM4
(Table  5 ). 

 Metrics and SLOs. 
Following prior LLM-serving work  ( Zhong et al., 2024 ; Hong et al., 2025b ) ,
we report goodput (Eq.  1 ), throughput,
mean and P99 time-to-first-token (TTFT), and mean and P99 time-per-output-token (TPOT).
Five SLO tiers per (model, workload) pair (Table  4 )
range from strict to relaxed,
and goodput is computed independently at each tier.
Following standard serving-benchmark practice  ( Zhong et al., 2024 ; Patel et al., 2024 ; Dong et al., 2025 ) ,
each run uses Poisson arrivals at a controlled rate.
For the main end-to-end comparison, we vary the arrival rate
from 1 to 5 req/s to cover under-, near-, and over-saturated regimes
(the saturation point for our three models lies in the 2–4 req/s range).
Each run processes 500 requests;
reported numbers are medians of three independent runs.
For Dynamo, “best manually tuned” means sweeping the feasible allocations

 4.3. HAS Efficiency 

 We evaluate HAS as the configuration layer that selects
 ( allocation , B , s ) (\text{allocation},\,B,\,s) for each deployment.
This search is non-trivial because the joint ( alloc , B , s ) (\mathrm{alloc},B,s) space
contains hundreds of configurations even at 8 GPUs,
its dimensions interact non-monotonically,
and the best operating point varies with modality and load.
We therefore compare HAS against two search baselines under the same budget of
30 trials. Each trial deploys one configuration and runs 100 requests at
 Λ = 5 \Lambda{=}5  req/s; we report results on the image ( LLaVA-34B ), video ( Qwen-32B ), and audio ( Ultravox-27B ) workloads.
All methods search the same joint space of feasible allocations, micro-batch
sizes, and offload ratios: Random samples uniformly from this space,
whereas Coordinate performs coordinate descent over the three dimensions.
For all three methods, the selected configuration is the one with the highest
throughput observed within the 30-trial budget, with mean TTFT and mean TPOT as tie-breakers,
and the reported throughput and latency metrics correspond to that configuration.
Figure  12 reports the wall-clock search cost of each method. 

 Stage 1 pruning. 
Run at the same target rate as Stage 2 ( Λ = 5 \Lambda{=}5  req/s, chosen above saturation
to expose method differences),
Stage 1’s bottleneck-balance score (Eq.  4 )
prunes the allocation dimension of the search space,
keeping 3 of the 7 feasible allocations under the 1.2 × 1.2\times threshold.
The four pruned allocations all have a bottleneck stage at least 20% more loaded
than the best-balanced candidate at this rate,
so that stage would dominate end-to-end latency regardless of how B B and s s are tuned.
This 7 → 3 7{\to}3 reduction lets Stage 2 concentrate its 30-trial budget
on balanced candidates, improving per-trial reliability. 

 Search quality. 
Across the three modalities, HAS consistently finds the highest-throughput configuration within the 30-trial budget.
The benefit is largest on audio, where the light Whisper encoder leaves substantial encode-GPU headroom; HAS’s directed search exploits this headroom more reliably than uninformed baselines;
moderate on image, where the CLIP encoder is heavy enough to benefit from disaggregation but still leaves enough headroom for local prefill;
and smallest on video, where the multi-frame encoder leaves little room for local prefill and narrows the useful part of the configuration space. 

 Search efficiency. 
Figure  12 reports the average wall-clock time each method takes to find a configuration reaching a given percentile of the best-observed throughput across the three modalities.
HAS reaches the 99th percentile in 1.7 h on image, 1.3 h on video, and 1.7 h on audio, while Random fails to reach the 99th percentile on any modality within its 30-trial budget, and Coordinate fails on image and video.
Even at lower percentiles HAS is consistently faster: it reaches the 90th percentile in 0.3 h on image (vs. 5.0 h for Random and 0.6 h for Coordinate) and 0.6 h on audio (where Random does not reach this percentile within its budget).
These differences also reveal each baseline’s failure mode: Random’s slow rise to high percentiles suggests it spends much of its budget in poor regions of the search space, while Coordinate descends quickly at low percentiles but stalls at high ones, consistent with descent into a local optimum.
HAS avoids both behaviors by pruning bottlenecked allocations before search, so its 30-trial budget concentrates on balanced candidates.

 4.4. SM Partitioning Ablation 

 We isolate the contribution of dynamic SM partitioning by comparing three configurations on Ultravox-27B with local prefill enabled:
(i) no SM partitioning (default CUDA time-slicing between encode and local prefill),
(ii) static 50/50 SM split via libsmctrl , and
(iii) our dynamic five-level scheme. 

 Table  6 reports the results at Λ = 10 \Lambda{=}10  req/s, a high rate that pushes all three configurations past their throughput limit so the throughput column shows what each can actually sustain.
Figure  7 explains why dynamic partitioning is effective:
encode latency decreases sharply as the encode worker receives more SMs,
but the benefit quickly saturates past the latency knee.
This diminishing-return profile implies that a fixed 50/50 split is rarely optimal;
once encode receives enough SMs to reach its sweet spot,
the remaining SMs are more valuable to local prefill.
Without SM partitioning, uncontrolled contention increases P99 TTFT by 19%
and P99 TPOT by 42% compared to our dynamic scheme, while throughput drops by 9%.
The static 50/50 split improves over the no-partition baseline
but still wastes local prefill capacity at low batch sizes
and under-provisions encode at high batch sizes,
reducing throughput by 3% and increasing P99 TTFT by 11% and P99 TPOT by 22% relative to our dynamic scheme.
Our dynamic scheme adapts per-batch,
approaching the latency of running encode alone at large batches
and giving the remaining SMs to local prefill at small batches.

 5. Related Work 

 Disaggregated LLM and MLLM serving. 
Disaggregating inference stages across GPU pools has become a standard technique for text-only LLM serving.
DistServe  ( Zhong et al., 2024 ) , Splitwise  ( Patel et al., 2024 ) ,
and Mooncake  ( Qin et al., 2025 ) separate prefill and decode to
exploit their distinct compute and memory profiles.
LoongServe  ( Wu et al., 2024 ) adds elastic sequence parallelism
that shifts resources between the two phases at runtime,
TPLA  ( Tang et al., 2026 ) co-designs tensor-parallel attention with disaggregation,
and Aegaeon  ( Xiang et al., 2025 ) pools GPUs across concurrent multi-model serving.
NVIDIA Dynamo  ( NVIDIA, 2025 ) extends disaggregation to MLLMs
with an explicit three-stage Encode / Prefill / Decode pipeline,
but executes Encode per-request and leaves GPU allocation to operators.
HydraInfer  ( Dong et al., 2025 ) also adopts EPD disaggregation but tunes only per-stage GPU allocation, with neither Encode – Prefill offload nor joint ( B , s ) (B,s) tuning.
Parallel efforts target multimodal generative serving beyond language:
MoDM  ( Xia et al., 2026 ) , TetriServe  ( Lu et al., 2026 ) , and DiffServe  ( Ahmad et al., 2025 ) 
serve diffusion and DiT workloads with their own scheduling tradeoffs orthogonal to ours.
In contrast to all of the above, our contribution is a layered architecture
in which Encode -aware mechanisms and automated configuration
are co-designed around the Encode stage as the single pipeline entry point. 

 Intra-GPU prefill–decode multiplexing. 
A recent line of work co-locates Prefill and Decode 
on the same GPU within a single LLM instance.
MuxWise  ( Chen et al., 2026 ) uses NVIDIA Green Context  ( NVIDIA Corporation, 2024 ) 
to split SMs between the two phases with low reconfiguration overhead,
and Bullet  ( Lin et al., 2026 ) uses libsmctrl   ( Bakita and Anderson, 2023 ) 
to orchestrate dynamic spatial–temporal SM partitions for LLM serving.
Semi-PD  ( Hong et al., 2025a ) adopts MPS-based allocations with helper processes,
Nexus  ( Shi et al., 2025 ) proposes intra-GPU prefill–decode disaggregation
via dynamic SM partitioning, and POD-Attention  ( Kamath et al., 2025 ) 
fuses the two phases at the kernel level.
These systems multiplex two dependent phases within a single LLM instance;
our work instead multiplexes Encode and a local Prefill worker
 across the stages of a three-stage multimodal pipeline.
 Encode co-location introduces an orthogonal offload-ratio dimension
that prefill–decode multiplexing systems do not model,
and requires joint configuration with GPU allocation and micro-batch bound,
a space navigated by our Hybrid Auto Selection . 

 GPU sharing and SM partitioning. 
CUDA MPS  ( NVIDIA, 2024 ) enables concurrent kernel execution
on shared GPUs with per-client resource limits and priority hints,
but its priorities cannot preempt running kernels,
leaving latency-sensitive workloads vulnerable to interference.
 libsmctrl   ( Bakita and Anderson, 2023 ) and NVIDIA Green Context  ( NVIDIA Corporation, 2024 ) 
provide strictly enforced SM partitioning with sub-microsecond overhead;
we adopt these primitives rather than reinvent them.
LithOS  ( Coppock et al., 2025 ) presents a transparent operating system for fine-grained ML multitenancy.
REEF  ( Han et al., 2022 ) , Abacus  ( Cui et al., 2021 ) , Orion  ( Strati et al., 2024 ) , and GPUlet  ( Choi et al., 2022 ) 
share GPUs among independent DNN workloads with interference-aware scheduling;
Paella  ( Ng et al., 2023 ) operates at the kernel level for fine-grained GPU sharing;
MuxServe  ( Duan et al., 2024 ) multiplexes multiple full LLMs on a shared GPU.
All of these co-locate independent workloads;
our Encode and local Prefill are dependent 
stages of a single MLLM request, requiring pipeline-aware QoS that
off-the-shelf GPU-sharing mechanisms do not provide. 

 Inference batching and scheduling. 
Orca  ( Yu et al., 2022 ) introduced continuous batching at decode token boundaries,
vLLM  ( Kwon et al., 2023 ) added PagedAttention for near-optimal KV-cache management,
and SGLang  ( Zheng et al., 2024 ) improves reuse with RadixAttention.
Sarathi-Serve  ( Agrawal et al., 2024b ) chunks prefill to enable stall-free batching,
FastServe  ( Wu et al., 2023 ) preempts with proactive KV management,
AlpaServe  ( Li et al., 2023 ) exploits statistical multiplexing under bursty arrivals,
and Niyama  ( Goel et al., 2025 ) introduces multi-class SLO-tiered scheduling.
SOLA  ( Hong et al., 2025b ) optimizes SLO attainment via state-aware scheduling,
and Klotski  ( Fang et al., 2025 ) overlaps MoE expert I/O with computation across batches.
These systems target Prefill or Decode scheduling in text-only pipelines;
our Poisson-gap micro-batching targets the Encode stage specifically,
exploiting the arrival process to derive an adaptive
dispatch threshold for a single-shot forward pass where continuous batching does not apply. 

 Automated configuration for LLM serving. 
Optuna  ( Akiba et al., 2019 ) provides TPE-based Bayesian optimization,
which we use as Stage 2’s backbone.
Vidur  ( Agrawal et al., 2024a ) proposes a simulation framework for LLM configuration exploration,
avoiding end-to-end deployment cost at the price of simulation fidelity.
ExeGPT  ( Oh et al., 2024 ) formulates constraint-aware resource scheduling for LLM inference
as a search assuming monotonic structure over batch and parallelism choices.
Alpa  ( Zheng et al., 2022 ) automates parallelism-strategy search for distributed training.
Our HAS differs along two axes.
First, it combines a lightweight empirical
proxy (per-stage capacity profiling with a bottleneck-balance score)
for allocation screening with end-to-end benchmark measurements
for runtime-parameter refinement, avoiding both exhaustive search cost
and simulation-fidelity risk.
Second, the coupled (allocation, B B , s s ) surface induced by our
encode-aware mechanisms is non-monotonic,
motivating TPE over the monotonic structure assumed by ExeGPT. 

 6. Conclusion 

 Disaggregated MLLM serving introduces a new Encode stage
that gates the entire pipeline,
yet existing systems run it one request at a time with operator-set GPU allocation,
which leaves substantial GPU capacity idle
and forces operators to navigate a configuration space too large to tune by hand.
To address this, we propose EAServe , a layered serving framework
that repositions Encode as the pipeline’s control point.
 EAServe combines an offline configuration search,
which jointly tunes GPU allocation, encode batch bound, and prefill offload ratio,
with three encode-aware runtime mechanisms
that expose those parameters and enforce the latency guarantees
needed for the search to be reliable.
Across image, video, and audio MLLM workloads on an 8-GPU server,
 EAServe delivers up to 4.3 × 4.3\times higher goodput than strong production baselines
while reclaiming most of the encode-side capacity that prior systems leave unused.
Looking ahead, we plan to validate the design at multi-node datacenter scale,
where cross-node embedding transfer and HAS’s search budget under thousands of candidate
configurations both warrant dedicated study,
and to extend the dispatch rule with online arrival-rate estimation for various workloads.
