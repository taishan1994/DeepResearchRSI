ManimCode: Learning Visual Self-Correction for Educational Animation
Zhengyang Liang
Lizi Liao
Abstract
Manim supports precise, programmatic video generation, but a successfully rendered educational animation can still have visual defects, missing explanations, or incorrect content. Rendering does not directly identify the code changes needed to correct these visual failures, so feedback-driven revision is needed. Existing agentic frameworks use iterative rendering and revision but typically rely on large models, often proprietary. Existing Manim datasets primarily contain final programs and often lack quality verification and the trajectories of feedback and revisions needed to learn this refinement process.
We introduce ManimData and ManimCode, a data and training framework for feedback-driven educational animation generation. We construct ManimData through a render-guided pipeline that iteratively generates, executes, reviews, and revises Manim programs with retrieved API documentation. Its 24,034 render-verified, quality-filtered records preserve intermediate programs, execution errors, visual critiques, and corrective revisions. We use role-conditioned supervised fine-tuning on these trajectories to train ManimCode, a single 4B model, to write, review, and revise animation programs. To improve correction of errors that differ from those in the collected trajectories, we then train the Reviser with reinforcement learning on self-generated states, using rendering outcomes and reference-free quality feedback.
ManimCode achieves 96.6% and 97.0% render success on ManimData-test and ManimBench, compared with 52.6% and 82.0% for the untrained 4B model under the same inference setup, while also improving video quality scores from 2.48 to 2.94 and from 3.00 to 3.44. Integrating ManimCode into two long-form educational video generation systems increases completed videos from 19/117 to 107/117 and from 53/239 to 239/239, alongside improvements in educational effectiveness and visual quality.

1  Introduction
Educational animations help explain abstract STEM concepts by showing mathematical relationships, geometric constructions, and processes that unfold over time. Creating these animations by hand, however, requires both subject knowledge and programming expertise. Recent work uses language models to write programs for animation engines such as Manim, which render them into videos ( Chen et al., 2025b ; Ku et al., 2025 ) . This approach offers precise control over formulas, text, and geometry, which are difficult to maintain with pixel-space video generation. A successful render can still produce a poor teaching video: labels may overlap, objects may fall outside the frame, or explanations may omit important steps. These defects are difficult to fix because the video does not directly identify the required code changes. Producing a usable animation therefore requires inspecting the output and revising the program based on the defects.
Agentic systems such as Code2Video ( Chen et al., 2025b ) , TheoremExplainAgent ( Ku et al., 2025 ) , and ManimAgent ( Jiang et al., 2026 ) use execution errors and, in some cases, visual feedback to revise generated programs. These systems typically rely on large models, often proprietary, and their refinement loops become much less reliable with smaller open alternatives. Specialized training is also limited by the available data. ManimBench ( Rammuni Silva et al., 2026a ) contains render-verified programs but is relatively small, while community datasets and JanusCode-800K ( Sun et al., 2026 ) lack systematic rendering and quality checks. Of the 20,976 Hugging Face community programs we collected, roughly one-third fail to render as released. Even among the renderable Hugging Face programs, only 31% pass our quality criterion after revision. These datasets retain final programs but discard the errors, feedback, and edits that led to them. ManimTrainer ( Rammuni Silva et al., 2026b ) uses reference-based rewards during training and execution feedback at inference, but does not directly supervise models to interpret and correct visual defects. Existing data therefore offers little guidance on how a model should review and revise its own animations.
To make feedback-driven refinement learnable by small open models, we build ManimData through a render-guided pipeline that records how animation programs are corrected and improved. We collect Manim programs from public datasets and generate additional requests from Wikipedia STEM topics. A strong open multimodal model then writes or revises each program, executes it, and reviews the rendered frames. Execution errors, layout checks, and visual critiques guide subsequent revisions, with relevant API documentation retrieved from a knowledge base covering Manim and eight domain plugins. The pipeline keeps the best version and admits a record only when it meets the quality criterion. The resulting 24,034 render-verified, quality-filtered records contain the final program and video, intermediate versions, errors, rendered frames, reviews, and revisions, preserving the feedback and corrective actions missing from existing Manim corpora.
We use ManimData to train ManimCode , enabling a single 4B model to write, review, and revise animation programs. We first organize the recorded trajectories into role-conditioned supervised fine-tuning (SFT) tasks. The Writer generates programs from requests and retrieved API documentation, the Reviewer assesses rendered frames and identifies visual defects, and the Reviser corrects execution failures and improves programs based on visual critiques. Training on final programs and intermediate correction steps improves initial generation and feedback-driven revision. Because the trajectories come from a stronger model, they may not fully capture errors encountered by the smaller model. We address this gap by training the Reviser with reinforcement learning (RL) on self-generated repair and critique states, using rendering outcomes and reference-free quality feedback as rewards. At inference, a single 4B model runs the complete render–review–revise loop without the larger model.
We evaluate ManimCode on two scene-level animation benchmarks and integrate it as the scene generator in two long-form systems, Code2Video and TheoremExplainAgent. It achieves 96.6% and 97.0% render success on ManimData-test and ManimBench, compared with 52.6% and 82.0% for the untrained 4B backbone under the same inference setup. With the planning and rendering budgets held constant, completed videos increase from 19/117 to 107/117 and from 53/239 to 239/239, respectively, alongside improvements in learning-gain and visual-quality metrics.

2  Related Work
2.1  Feedback-Driven Code Generation
Language models can improve programs at inference time by critiquing and revising their own outputs ( Madaan et al., 2023 ; Shinn et al., 2023 ) or by reading execution results ( Chen et al., 2024 ; Ding et al., 2024 ) , although under matched sampling budgets such self-repair often gains little over resampling ( Olausson et al., 2024 ) . Later work trains these abilities directly. RLEF ( Gehring et al., 2024 ) and SCoRe ( Kumar et al., 2025 ) optimize multi-turn correction with reinforcement learning from execution feedback or final-answer correctness, while CriticGPT ( McAleese et al., 2024 ) and CTRL ( Xie et al., 2025 ) train critics that are rewarded for the bugs they catch or the fixes they enable. When a program’s output is visual, rendering supplies the feedback. Training corpora for chart, plotting, and user-interface code are filtered or repaired through execution ( Zhao et al., 2025 ; Ni et al., 2025 ; Wu et al., 2024 ) , and reinforcement learning methods derive rewards for rendered outputs from their similarity to reference images ( Rodriguez et al., 2025 ; Chen et al., 2025a ) , from a multimodal critic ( Li et al., 2025 ) , or from verifiable layout metrics ( Pan et al., 2026 ) . Closest to our setting, WebLoop ( Meng et al., 2026 ) trains one policy to generate, critique, and refine web pages, and UI2Code N ( Yang et al., 2025 ) and VisRefiner ( Deng et al., 2026 ) train models to refine UI code from rendered screenshots. Most of these methods target static images or web pages, and most train only the generator or only the critic. In our setting, programs render videos whose defects unfold over time, usually with no reference to compare against, and one model learns to critique the rendered frames as well as to write and revise the program.
2.2  Code-Based Educational Animation
Code-based generation is now a common route to precise educational animation. Agentic systems write Manim scenes with large, mostly proprietary language models ( Samarth et al., 2025 ) . Code2Video ( Chen et al., 2025b ) and TheoremExplainAgent ( Ku et al., 2025 ) plan long-form lectures and repair each scene’s code from render errors over several renders, and ManimAgent ( Jiang et al., 2026 ) guides the repairs with visual review of the rendered frames. OmniManim ( Li et al., 2026 ) adds a learned layout prior, SGA ( Lopez et al., 2026 ) checks scene geometry symbolically before rendering, and multi-agent systems such as SciEducator ( Xu et al., 2026 ) produce multimodal educational content from scientific videos. Benchmarks score generated Manim code on executability, request alignment, and visual similarity ( Rammuni Silva et al., 2026a ; Oli, 2026 ) .
Training-based work is sparse. JanusCoder ( Sun et al., 2026 ) includes Manim programs in a large corpus of visual code. ManimTrainer ( Rammuni Silva et al., 2026b ) follows fine-tuning with reinforcement learning that rewards similarity to reference programs and videos, and at inference it re-prompts the model with render errors and retrieved documentation. SimuScene ( Wang et al., 2026 ) rewards physics animations with a vision-language model that answers verification questions about the rendered video. Each of these trains a model to write a complete program, and feedback enters only as a training reward or as error messages at inference. ManimData instead records how programs were reviewed and revised, so our model learns these steps as well.
3  Data
ManimData pairs each request with a render-verified program, its video, and the trajectory of errors, critiques, and revisions that produced the program. It draws on two sources: requests derived from Wikipedia STEM topics and public community programs (Sec. 3.1 ). A single render-guided pipeline writes, renders, checks, reviews, and revises the programs from both sources with retrieved API documentation (Sec. 3.2 , Fig. 1 ). 3.1  Data Sources and API Knowledge Base
Wikipedia requests.
We derive requests from 639,700 English Wikipedia articles. A coarse-to-fine filter with Qwen3.5-4B ( Qwen Team, 2026a ) and Qwen3.6-27B ( Qwen Team, 2026b ) keeps 15,159 topics whose central ideas can be shown graphically. Qwen3.8-27B ( Qwen Team, 2026c ) finds directly drawable content in 12,516 of these topics, assigns each of them one of 30 visualization types, and writes up to two open-ended requests per topic specifying what to draw and emphasize. Excluding requests that depend on realistic biological or medical illustration (e.g., cells) leaves 22,058 requests across 11,150 topics (Appendix A.1 ).
Community programs.
We also collect programs from seven Hugging Face datasets (HF Community), the Manim split of JanusCode-800K ( Sun et al., 2026 ) , and the ManimBench training split ( Rammuni Silva et al., 2026a ) . From HF Community we keep only the programs that render as released. After deduplication, 20,449 inputs remain, and each starts from its released code.
API knowledge base.
We compile 2,219 API cards (Appendix A.2 ) so the Writer and Reviser can look up APIs for our renderer, ManimCE v0.19.1 with eight domain plugins. The cards come from the official documentation, descriptions of plugin objects written by Qwen3.8-27B, capability recipes that map topic keywords to plugins, and the best-practice rules and example scenes of manim_skill . Cards are retrieved with Qwen3-Embedding-8B and reranked with Qwen3-Reranker-8B ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) .
3.2  Render-Guided Generation and Filtering
Every input enters the render–review–revise loop (Fig. 1 ), in which Qwen3.8-27B plays the Writer , the Reviewer , and the Reviser within a budget of N = 10 N=10 renders. Let x x be the request and c c the current program. For a Wikipedia request, the Writer writes retrieval queries for x x and drafts c c from the retrieved API cards 𝒦 \mathcal{K} ; a community input starts from its released code. The renderer ℛ \mathcal{R} either produces a video v = ℛ ⁡ ( c ) v=\mathcal{R}(c) or stops with an execution error e e . If c c renders, a layout checker reads object positions at animation pauses and reports geometric defects as layout diagnostics ℓ \ell : objects outside the frame, overlapping text, and labels too small to read.

The Reviewer then sees x x , c c , and eight frames sampled from v v . It scores request alignment, content completeness, visual clarity, and animation quality from 1 to 5 and writes a critique of specific defects, including non-geometric ones such as missing content; the scores and critique form the review f f . The Reviser rewrites the full program: it repairs a crash from e e or revises a rendered program from f f and ℓ \ell , with cards 𝒦 \mathcal{K} retrieved anew for each revision.

4  Method
ManimCode learns from both the final programs and the intermediate corrections in ManimData’s render–review–revise trajectories. Role-conditioned trajectory supervision trains a 4B model to write programs, assess rendered outputs, and correct execution and visual defects. Because a stronger model collected these trajectories, we further train the Reviser with reinforcement learning on failures generated by the supervised model. At inference, the model iteratively refines educational animations within a fixed rendering budget.

4.1  Role-Conditioned Trajectory Supervision
We use each trajectory to supervise the model’s writing, reviewing, and revision steps. Every example is a triple ( ρ , z , y ) (\rho,z,y) : role ρ \rho , context z z , and target y y . The Writer receives the request and retrieved API cards and targets the trajectory’s final program c ∗ c^{*} , so it learns from the refinements made during construction. The Reviewer receives the request, current program, and sampled frames; its targets are the four recorded scores and the critique. For the Reviser , we extract two kinds of successful transitions: (i) execution repairs , revisions that turn a crashed program into one that renders, and (ii) quality revisions , critique-driven revisions for which the mean review score exceeds that of the program being revised. The Reviser targets the complete revised program given the request, program being revised, error or critique, layout diagnostics when available, and API cards. We also supervise retrieval queries based on the request and, when available, the error or critique, so the model can retrieve API cards at inference.
We use the same role instruction and context layout at training and inference. The SFT mixture 𝒟 SFT \mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{SFT}} contains 24,034 writing, 14,587 execution-repair, 32,683 quality-revision, 46,965 reviewing, and 187,183 retrieval-query examples. 4.2  Revision RL on Self-Generated States
The Reviser receives the model’s own mistakes as input, so we train it with reinforcement learning on states generated by the supervised model. Rewards combine render outcomes with a reward model’s judgments against the request or the input program. This setup requires no reference program or target video.
Self-generated training states.
For educational requests disjoint from the evaluation sets, the supervised model retrieves API cards 𝒦 \mathcal{K} , writes a first draft, and renders it. If the draft fails to render, we define a repair state z rep = ( x , c , e , 𝒦 ) z^{\mathrm{rep}}=(x,c,e,\mathcal{K}) . If it renders, the same model reviews it. If the review does not meet the early-stopping criterion, its review f f and layout diagnostics ℓ \ell form a critique state z crit = ( x , c , f , ℓ , 𝒦 ) z^{\mathrm{crit}}=(x,c,f,\ell,\mathcal{K}) . Both states follow the Reviser’s inference input format.
Rewards.

Near-duplicate revisions receive zero reward to discourage trivial changes. Inference.
At inference, ManimCode follows the same render–review–revise loop used during data construction, with a single 4B backbone handling all three roles. Each request starts from a newly generated program, and the loop retains the best successfully rendered candidate within a budget.
5  Experiments
We evaluate ManimCode with three research questions.
RQ1: How does ManimCode perform compared with existing general-purpose and Manim-specialized models on scene-level animation generation?
RQ2: Can ManimCode improve long-form educational video generation when integrated into existing systems?
RQ3: How do ManimData, trajectory supervision, and Reviser RL contribute to generation performance? We further analyze representative revision examples and examine the agreement between automatic quality assessments and human preferences.

Benchmarks.
We evaluate scene generation on two test sets. ManimData-test consists of 500 requests selected from the ManimData request pool, 219 Wikipedia requests and 281 community requests, all excluded from training (Appendix A.1 ).
ManimBench ( Rammuni Silva et al., 2026a ) has 100 test requests with reference programs, and we follow its protocol. Of the 100 references, 85 nearly duplicate ManimCE documentation examples that our knowledge base contains, so in all our ManimBench runs the retriever excludes those examples. For long-form generation we use MMMC , the 117 lecture topics of Code2Video ( Chen et al., 2025b ) , and TheoremExplainBench (TEB), the 239 distinct theorems of TheoremExplainAgent ( Ku et al., 2025 ) .
Baselines.
The general-purpose baselines served through APIs are GPT-4o ( OpenAI et al., 2024 ) and two recent mixture-of-experts models, GLM-5.3 Flash and DeepSeek-V4 Flash, which we query through OpenRouter 1 1
1
https://openrouter.ai/ . The open general-purpose baselines that we run locally are Qwen3.5-4B (our base model), Qwen3.5-9B, and Qwen3.8-27B. The Manim-specialized baselines are JanusCoder-8B ( Sun et al., 2026 ) , whose training data contains most ManimBench test requests ( † in Table 2 ), and the released Seed-Coder-8B checkpoint of ManimTrainer ( Rammuni Silva et al., 2026b ) . Two further baselines repair their programs at inference. ManimTrainer + RITL-DOC runs ManimTrainer’s own repair loop with documentation retrieval. Qwen3.5-4B + our loop puts the untrained model in every role of our loop, with the same retrieval and budget as our system.
Metrics and judge.
For scene-level evaluation, we report render success (RS), visual similarity (VS), and video quality (VQ). RS measures the percentage of successfully rendered programs. On ManimBench, VS measures similarity to reference videos using the geometric mean of SSIM and CLIP similarities, following Rammuni Silva et al. (2026b) . VQ is evaluated by Gemini 3.8 Flash 2 2
2
https://deepmind.google/models/model-cards/gemini-3-8-flash/ on a 1–5 scale, averaging content quality (request alignment, completeness, and correctness) and presentation quality (visual clarity and animation quality). VQ is computed only over successfully rendered videos. For long-form generation, we report TeachQuiz learning gain and aesthetics on MMMC and visual quality on TEB. We use the same judge for both benchmarks and exclude narration-based dimensions from TEB. Further evaluation details are provided in Appendix C .
5.2  Main Results
Scene-level generation.
Table 2 compares ManimCode with general-purpose and Manim-specialized models. With a 4B generator, ManimCode achieves 96.6% and 97.0% render success on ManimData-test and ManimBench, respectively, outperforming the evaluated general-purpose models (at most 62.4% and 83.0%) and released Manim-specialized systems (at most 67.0% and 73.0%).
ManimCode’s VQ scores are comparable to GPT-4o and approach those of recent MoE models, particularly on ManimBench.
Contribution of each component.
We examine the contributions of the refinement loop, trajectory SFT, and Reviser RL using the 4B variants in Table 2 . Applying our loop to the untrained Qwen3.5-4B raises render success from 7.0% to 52.6% on ManimData-test and from 31.0% to 82.0% on ManimBench, with only modest changes in video quality. Under the same loop and retrieval setup, trajectory SFT further improves render success to 88.6% and 92.0%, while also increasing VQ from 2.48 to 2.89 and from 3.00 to 3.45, respectively. Reviser RL brings additional render success gains of 8.0 and 5.0 percentage points, reaching 96.6% and 97.0%, while maintaining comparable video quality (2.94 and 3.44). Trajectory supervision improves both render success and video quality, while RL primarily increases render success.

5.3  Ablation Studies
Training data and curation.
Compared with existing Manim corpora (Table 4 ), ManimData achieves the highest VQ on both benchmarks and the highest render success on ManimBench. We also train two Writers on the same 8,416 of the 8,497 community records, using either the original programs or their curated versions as supervision (Figure 4 ). The other 81 records are JanusCode-800K programs that define no construct method. On ManimData-test, curation raises render success from 57.2% to 63.2% and VQ from 2.65 to 2.86. On ManimBench, it raises render success from 69.0% to 76.0% and VQ from 2.95 to 3.31.
5.4  Analysis
Agreement with human preferences.
To assess the reliability of our automatic quality judge, we conduct a human evaluation on 98 pairs of videos generated by ManimCode and GPT-4o or Qwen3.8-27B. A blinded annotator selects the video better suited for teaching or marks a tie (Appendix C.4 ).
Human preferences show stronger agreement with the judge when quality differences are larger: for VQ gaps of at least 0.5, the annotator agrees with the judge on 79% of decided pairs, compared with only 29% for gaps below 0.25. At the system level, human preferences align with the judge’s rankings in three of four comparisons (Table 11 ), favoring ManimCode over GPT-4o overall but Qwen3.8-27B on ManimData-test. The judge appears useful for assessing video quality, especially when the VQ gap is larger.

Qualitative examples.
In Figure 5 , our Reviewer locates a layout defect in the rendered frames, the Reviser turns its critique into a small code edit, and the judge, which plays no part in the loop, also scores the revised video higher. Revisions can also fail. In one example of Appendix C.5 , the first of two consecutive revisions moves a list down to clear a progress bar but pushes it past the bottom edge, and the second moves it back up and restores the overlap. The Reviewer scores the second revision lower, so the loop keeps the first as its best candidate even though its list is still cut off. Keeping the best-scored candidate stops a regression from replacing a better version, but it cannot repair a defect that the Reviser leaves behind.
6  Conclusion
ManimData and ManimCode provide a data and training framework for feedback-driven educational animation generation. ManimData contains 24,034 render-verified, quality-filtered records that retain final programs alongside execution errors, visual critiques, and corrective revisions. ManimCode uses role-conditioned supervision and reinforcement learning on self-generated revision states to train a 4B model to write, review, and revise animation programs. It achieves 96.6% and 97.0% render success on ManimData-test and ManimBench, respectively, while maintaining competitive video quality against larger general-purpose models. When integrated into Code2Video and TheoremExplainAgent, ManimCode also improves long-form video completion and visual quality scores.

Appendix B Method
B.1  Role-Conditioned Supervision
We extract five types of examples from the 24,034 ManimData trajectories (Table 8 ). A writing example pairs the request and its retrieved API cards with the final program of the record. Community records lack a Writer step because their first version is released code. To reconstruct the Writer input, Qwen3.8-27B writes retrieval queries for the request, and the top 10 retrieved cards form the context. An execution-repair example keeps a revision that turns a crashing program into one that renders. A quality-revision example keeps a revision whose mean Reviewer score is higher than that of the program it revised. We subsample reviewing examples from the recorded reviews, with at most two per community record. Retrieval-query examples pair Qwen3.8-27B’s queries with the request and any error or critique.

Appendix C Evaluation
C.1  Baselines and Model Versions
Single-pass baselines decode at temperature 0.4 and top- p p 0.95 with at most 8,192 new tokens; thinking is disabled for models that support it. On ManimData-test they receive our Writer’s system prompt without retrieved API cards; on ManimBench they receive the benchmark’s own prompt. GPT-4o is the snapshot gpt-4o-2024-11-20 . GLM-5.3 Flash ( z-ai/glm-5.3-flash ) and DeepSeek-V4 Flash ( deepseek/deepseek-v4-flash ) are queried through OpenRouter in November 2026 with the same sampling settings, pinned to the official Z.AI endpoint (fp8) for GLM-5.3 Flash and to DeepInfra (fp8) for DeepSeek-V4 Flash, with Fireworks as the only allowed alternative; Fireworks served 15 of the 600 DeepSeek-V4 Flash requests. GLM-5.3 Flash runs at minimal reasoning effort (149 and 11 reasoning tokens per request on average on ManimData-test and ManimBench), and DeepSeek-V4 Flash with reasoning off. JanusCoder-8B is the released internlm/JanusCoder-8B . For ManimTrainer we use its authors’ released LoRA adapter for ByteDance-Seed/Seed-Coder-8B-Instruct , trained with SFT and GRPO, and run it with their chat template. In the RITL-DOC row, ManimTrainer uses its authors’ inference-time repair loop with documentation retrieval, which allows at most three feedback rounds and at most four renders. The row for the strongest ManimTrainer configuration uses Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B, trained with GRPO under RITL-DOC with three rounds, and reports the ManimBench render success and visual similarity from Table 3 of its paper. That model is not released, and the authors used ManimCE 0.19.0; we use 0.19.1.
C.2  Judge
The judge is Gemini 3.8 Flash (API model models/gemini-3.8-flash ). It sees the request and the rendered video at 2 frames per second, but not the code. One call assigns 1–5 scores for request alignment, content completeness, and semantic correctness; their mean is Q cont Q_{\mathrm{cont}} . A dedicated presentation prompt scores visual clarity and animation quality in two calls; Q pres Q_{\mathrm{pres}} is the mean of the calls’ averages. VQ is the mean of Q cont Q_{\mathrm{cont}} and Q pres Q_{\mathrm{pres}} . The scene-level scores in each table come from one batch run in October 2026, except the GLM-5.3 Flash and DeepSeek-V4 Flash rows of Table 2 , which were scored on November 1, 2026. For that later batch, we re-scored 30 ManimData-test and 30 ManimBench anchor videos from October; their mean VQ changed by + 0.03 +0.03 and − 0.02 -0.02 .
C.3  Long-Form Evaluation
For each long-form system, both runs use a lecture plan written by the untrained Qwen3.5-4B in that system’s native configuration; they differ only in the scene generator. The native run uses that model to write and repair each scene, while our run uses ManimCode. Each run gets at most 10 renders per section or scene, and sections that never render are skipped when the video is merged. We replace each benchmark’s original judge with Gemini 3.8 Flash, so absolute scores are not comparable with published numbers.
For MMMC we report the number of completed videos, the TeachQuiz learning gain ( × \times 100) over all 117 topics, and the aesthetics score (0–100) over the produced videos; a topic without a video counts as zero TeachQuiz gain. The 4B planner produces no valid plan for 10 of the 117 topics, so our 107 videos cover every topic that has a plan. TheoremExplainBench (TEB) has 239 distinct theorems; its list of 240 contains The Ideal Gas Law twice. Its visual score is the geometric mean (1–5) of three visual dimensions (visual relevance, element layout, and visual consistency) over the produced videos, judged with the audio removed; an all-black video scores 1 on the two frame-based dimensions. We omit the two narration-based dimensions of TEB because they score the shared plan. For 10 theorems, the native run never saved the implementation plans of some scenes (23 of the 1,371), and our run re-planned these scenes with the same untrained 4B model.
C.4  Human Evaluation Protocol
We sampled at random (with a fixed seed) 98 requests that both our system and its opponent rendered: 48 against GPT-4o (31 from ManimData-test and 17 from ManimBench) and 50 against Qwen3.8-27B (36 and 14); no request was used for both opponents. The videos are the files scored in Table 2 , so each human choice can be compared with the judge’s scores for the same two videos.
Each page of the annotation interface shows the full request and the two silent videos side by side, labeled A and B in random order, without system names or scores. Both videos loop, and an answer is accepted only after each video has played for at least 90% of its length; dragging the scrubber to the end does not count. The annotator chooses the video they would rather show to students to explain the request (A, about the same, or B). They weigh whether the content is correct and covers the request, and whether everything is readable, inside the frame, and free of overlaps; the instructions state that a longer video is not better by itself. Pairs are shown in random order, and two of them reappear later as a consistency check; the repeats are excluded from all counts.
One member of our team, who did not know which system produced which video, judged all 98 pairs, taking a median of 24 seconds per pair. Among pairs in which both the annotator and the judge picked a side, the annotator agreed with the judge’s content ordering in 69% and with its presentation ordering in 65%.
