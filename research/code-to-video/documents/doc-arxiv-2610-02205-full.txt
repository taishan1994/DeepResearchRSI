Abstract
Programmable world models separate executable dynamics from visual generation, offering a promising foundation for next-generation game engines. However, their visual adherence to explicit rules and interactions remains insufficiently evaluated. Existing benchmarks assess visual quality, controllability, and instruction or physical adherence, but rarely test fidelity to fine-grained, program-specified world events. We introduce PROWBench , comprising 170 programmatically constructed episodes and 600 proxy videos covering diverse scenes and interactions. PROWBench logs entity states and timestamped events, including those outside the camera’s field of view, as replayable world records, from which it renders synchronized views and proxy representations. This enables generated videos to be checked against the observable consequences of program execution. An extensible framework constructs scenes, controls behaviors, and can render each camera view in different representations, such as coarse 3D, and bounding boxes. The benchmark covers first- and third-person perspectives, with synchronized multi-view observations available for a subset of episodes. Grounded in these records, PROWBench evaluates entity control, long-horizon memory, and, with two VLM-based metrics, Logic-Render Alignment and Interaction Success Rate , adherence to the prescribed timeline and the visual realization of timestamped engine-recorded events.
Figure 1 : Overview of our extensible framework and benchmark.
Our extensible framework pairs visual proxies with ground-truth world records, enabling reliable evaluation of how faithfully generated videos realize prescribed states, rules, and interactions. This assessment would otherwise be difficult. Here, we compare the CLIP score with our proposed interaction metric. CLIP assigns a higher score to the clearly inferior case, whereas one of our metric correctly reflects whether the prescribed interaction is faithfully executed.
1 Introduction
Recent advances in video world models have enabled increasingly realistic, open-ended interactive environments. However, maintaining persistent world states and enforcing user-defined rules remain challenging when entities, attributes, and interaction outcomes are represented implicitly through visual histories or latent context. Programmable World Model and Code World Model address these limitations by separating world-state evolution from visual generation ( Huang et al., 2026a ; Chen et al., 2026b ) : coding agents and executable programs maintain states and interaction rules, while video models generate observations conditioned on structured representations of the world. This separation combines explicit state control with visual generation, but correct program execution does not guarantee faithful visual realization. A generated video may appear plausible while violating a rule or failing to depict a specified interaction. This raises a central evaluation question: can video models faithfully render the scene structure, rules, and interactions of a programmable world?
Existing benchmarks evaluate video quality ( Huang et al., 2024 ) , controllability and dynamics ( Duan et al., 2025 ) , instruction following and physical adherence ( Li et al., 2025a ) , and interactive control and consistency ( Ye et al., 2026 ; Shang et al., 2026 ; Wu et al., 2026 ; Xu et al., 2026 ; Ying et al., 2026 ) . However, as summarized in Table 6.1 , they rarely test fidelity to fine-grained, program-specified world events: without replayable records of entity states and timestamped events, a generated video can be judged for plausibility but not against what the program executed. None of them renders the same recorded scene in paired proxy representations, and synchronized views are rare, although both are needed to isolate the effects of representation and viewpoint while holding scene content and actions fixed.
To address these gaps, we introduce PROWBench , a benchmark for evaluating the visual realization of programmable worlds. Figure 1 provides an overview of our extensible framework and benchmark. PROWBench combines a programmatically constructed dataset with complementary metrics for logic adherence and interaction realization. The data engine is available for extension. It logs entity states and timestamped events, including those outside the camera’s field of view, as replayable world records and renders them into synchronized views and proxy representations, so that generated videos can be checked against the observable consequences of program execution.
PROWBench contains 170 dynamic scene episodes covering diverse environments and interactions. Each episode currently provides three synchronized representations (coarse 3D, semantic proxy, and colored oriented bounding boxes) from shared state, action, and interaction records, enabling controlled comparisons across representations. Some episodes additionally provide synchronized multi-view observations for cross-view comparisons. Grounded in these records, PROWBench evaluates entity control and the long-horizon memory of entities that leave and re-enter the view, and introduces two VLM-based metrics: Logic-Render Alignment assesses adherence to the prescribed timeline, while Interaction Success Rate checks whether recorded events are visually realized at the corresponding times using engine logs as ground truth. An extensible programmatic framework separates scene construction, behavior control, state recording, and rendering, allowing researchers to expand scene layouts, actions, and representations while generating aligned observations from shared state records.
We evaluate state-of-the-art models with the inputs produced by this data engine: coarse 3D, semantic-proxy, and colored-OBB renders of the recorded states, together with text prompts obtained by converting the engine’s recorded events. Our experiments assess their logic adherence and interaction realization across viewpoints and representation settings. Surprisingly, with appropriate prompts, existing general-purpose video generation models such as MiniMax-H3 ( MiniMax-AI, 2026 ) , without task-specific training, follow the prescribed entity trajectories and event logic well, and perform on par with camera-conditioned world models in camera following without explicit camera injection. Additional task- or domain-specific training can still improve several aspects, such as entity placement and the realization of domain-specific events. The proxy itself also matters: outputs sometimes follow the input geometry too closely and inherit the coarse shapes of low-poly proxies, while box proxies leave open which described appearance belongs to which entity, so designing a good proxy remains an open problem. Long-video generation remains challenging in terms of both camera following and entity persistence, while direct multi-view generation still yields unsatisfactory results.
Our contributions are threefold:
•
Benchmark.
We introduce PROWBench, comprising 170 episodes and 600 synchronized proxy videos covering multiple perspectives, visual representations, and diverse interactions, with shared world-state and action records.
•
Metrics.
We evaluate entity control and long-horizon memory against the recorded world states, and propose Logic-Render Alignment and Interaction Success Rate to assess whether generated videos follow the prescribed timeline and visually realize engine-recorded interaction events.
•
Extensible framework.
We provide a reusable programmatic generation framework that supports extensions to scene layouts, actions, and visual representations, enabling aligned multi-view and multi-representation data generation from shared state records.
2 Related Work
2.1 Video World Models and Programmable World States
Video world models predict future observations from visual context and actions. Genie ( Bruce et al., 2024 ) learns controllable environments from unlabeled videos, while GameNGen ( Valevski et al., 2025 ) enables real-time action-conditioned game generation. GameFactory ( Yu et al., 2025 ) , Matrix-Game ( Zhang et al., 2025 ) , Yume1.5 ( Mao et al., 2026a ) , and LingBot-World ( Robbyant Team et al., 2026 ) advance generalization, interaction, and long-horizon consistency. Memory-enhanced WorldPlay ( Sun et al., 2025 ) , Matrix-Game 3.0 ( Wang et al., 2026 ) , and AlayaWorld ( Team et al., 2026 ) improve persistence through spatial-context retrieval or reconstruction.
While these models infer states from visual history and memory, programmable world models externalize state evolution. Code World Model ( Chen et al., 2026b ) uses a coding agent to maintain executable states and interaction logic, while Programmable World Model ( Huang et al., 2026a ) feeds persistent structured states to a generative renderer. Other approaches similarly separate state or dynamics from appearance ( Meng et al., 2026 ; Lin et al., 2026b ; Cai et al., 2026a ) . PROWBench evaluates whether generated observations faithfully realize these explicit states and interactions.
2.2 Structured Representations for Visual Generation
Structured conditions offer direct spatial control through depth, trajectories, bounding boxes, masks, and camera motion ( Esser et al., 2023 ; Wang et al., 2024a ; Li et al., 2025b ; Wang et al., 2024d ; Luo et al., 2025 ; Jain et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2025c ) . Geometry-aware methods incorporate 3D boxes, bird’s-eye-view layouts, world volumes, and structured traffic scenes ( Gao et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2024 ; Wen et al., 2024 ; Lu et al., 2024 ; Wang et al., 2024b ; Wang et al., 2024c ; Wang et al., 2025 ) .
Generative rendering uses explicit geometry or renderer-derived signals: Coarse-to-Real ( Gomez-Nogales et al., 2026 ) and VideoFrom3D ( Kim et al., 2025 ) use coarse 3D scenes, while Diffusion Renderer ( Liang et al., 2025 ) , AlayaRenderer ( Huang et al., 2026b ) , its real-time extension ( Lin et al., 2026a ) , and LiVER ( Cai et al., 2026b ) use denser rendering cues. These approaches span sparse object controls to dense geometric representations.
Comparisons across representations are often confounded by differences in models, datasets, and tasks. PROWBench derives paired proxy representations from the same recorded world-state trajectory and camera configuration, isolating representation effects on visual realization under fixed scene evolution.
2.3 World Model Benchmarks and Evaluation
VBench ( Huang et al., 2024 ) and EvalCrafter ( Liu et al., 2024 ) assess video quality, temporal consistency, motion, and semantic alignment. WorldSimBench ( Qin et al., 2025 ) , WorldModelBench ( Li et al., 2025a ) , and WorldScore ( Duan et al., 2025 ) extend evaluation to physical plausibility, controllability, and world dynamics. MIND ( Ye et al., 2026 ) , WorldMark ( Xu et al., 2026 ) , Omni-WorldBench ( Wu et al., 2026 ) , WorldArena ( Shang et al., 2026 ) , and iWorld-Bench ( Fang et al., 2026 ) further target interactive behavior, memory, and long-horizon consistency.
WBench ( Ying et al., 2026 ) evaluates multi-turn navigation, subject actions, event editing, and perspective switching for instruction adherence, consistency, and physical plausibility. These benchmarks generally assess complete pipelines, coupling errors in action understanding, state prediction, memory, dynamics, and rendering.
PROWBench isolates rendering fidelity using engine-recorded frame-level states, rules, and timestamped interactions, enabling comparisons across synchronized perspectives and proxy representations under the same trajectory. Logic-Renderer Alignment measures whether specified rules are visibly realized, while Interaction measures whether recorded events appear at the corresponding times.

3.1 World Generation Pipeline
We introduce a pipeline for constructing programmable worlds and producing structured conditioning inputs and evaluation annotations for generative video models. The pipeline provides three complementary capabilities: world synthesis , control compilation , and final process . Together, these capabilities produce replayable world trajectories, synchronized conditioning inputs, and structured evaluation records.
World Synthesis.
The pipeline constructs dynamic worlds from configurations specifying the environment, entity layout, participating actors, task controllers, random seeds, and execution duration. Procedural routines generate terrain, buildings, roads, interiors, and interaction objects, together with collision surfaces, collision constraints, and interaction anchors. Entities have persistent episode-level identifiers, semantic categories, component hierarchies, and task-relevant attributes. Random seeds control variations in scene layouts, motion paths, speeds, and animation phases.
Specialized controllers coordinate articulated poses, object trajectories, and participant interactions through procedural animation and motion models. They support three broad categories of behavior. Interaction controllers organize tasks into explicit phases: for example, carrying proceeds through approaching, grasping, lifting, transporting, and releasing an object, with corresponding updates to hand targets and object ownership. Activity and locomotion controllers support animal behaviors and simplified flight and swimming dynamics. Driving controllers provide closed-loop control of vehicle motion. During execution, these controllers report action phases, participating entities, and relationship changes for recording and validation. External agents can query entities, submit supported actions or plans, advance the world by fixed ticks, and inspect execution feedback.
Each execution produces a structured episode record containing shared scene geometry, entity definitions and hierarchies, initial conditions, and temporal records of entity transforms, articulated poses, visibility, and appearance changes. Controller events and available input traces accompany these records, with explicit temporal mappings linking world execution, saved states, and video frames. Each saved state can be reconstructed independently from the shared scene description and its corresponding dynamic record. Configurations, random seeds, and source information are retained for provenance tracking.
Control Compilation.
The pipeline compiles recorded world states and camera trajectories into synchronized visual conditioning inputs for generative video models.
Third-person cameras follow task-dependent trajectories, while first-person cameras are derived from recorded head positions, character or vehicle orientations, and task-specific gaze information. The observing character’s display geometry is hidden in its first-person view, while its entity and articulated state remain in the shared world record. Final camera trajectories include intrinsics, camera-to-world and world-to-camera matrices, projection parameters, and clipping planes. Additional cameras can be associated with different participants to provide multiple perspectives of the same episode.
Rendering adapters map shared world records to conditioning representations. Our current implementation provides three representations. Coarse 3D representation (Coarse 3D) uses neutral materials to preserve scene shape, object silhouettes, and articulated motion. Semantic proxy (CWM proxy) combines semantically colored environments with simplified entity proxies, retaining global trajectories, orientations, and task-relevant posture while reducing local articulation and shape detail. Colored oriented bounding boxes (Colored-OBB) represent selected entities through spatial envelopes, stable instance colors, and directional cues, with a semi-transparent white face marking the front of each entity, while retaining geometry for environmental structures and simple objects. OBB bounds are updated over time, while instance colors remain consistent across frames and views.
Rendering adapters specify entity selection, proxy templates, transparency, occlusion, and clipping behavior. Given shared scene geometry G G , recorded world state S i S_{i} , camera parameters C i v C_{i}^{v} for view v v , and representation r r , a conditioning frame is generated as I i v , r = ℛ r ​ ( G , S i , C i v ) I_{i}^{v,r}=\mathcal{R}_{r}(G,S_{i},C_{i}^{v}) ,
where ℛ r \mathcal{R}_{r} is the representation-specific rendering adapter. Different views observe the same saved state, and representations within each view share camera parameters. Additional camera policies and rendering adapters can reuse recorded episodes without rerunning behavior controllers, provided the required information is available in the world records, whereas new behaviors produce new recorded executions. This separation preserves temporal alignment and enables controlled comparisons across representations and viewpoints while keeping the underlying world evolution fixed.
Final Process.
The pipeline exports three categories of outputs: scene and motion records, rendered observations, and text and interaction labels. Scene and motion records support replay and include scene geometry, entity identities and semantic categories, spatial and articulated states, joint poses, bounding volumes, and entity and camera trajectories with associated camera parameters. Rendered observations comprise synchronized proxy videos across viewpoints and representations, accompanied by metric depth interpreted with respect to its recorded geometry. Text and interaction labels describe action phases, participant relationships, and timestamped events, together with accompanying textual descriptions and contact targets and residuals.
Validation comprises four complementary components. Task and timeline checks assess scenario-specific phases, terminal conditions or state validity, and recording coverage. They also verify task-level interaction labels against the applicable annotation policy and required participant categories. Geometric checks evaluate penetration, support, and contact consistency at saved states using canonical geometry. Replay and alignment checks assess state reconstruction, camera alignment, and consistency with the selected representation. They further establish correspondence among videos, depth, and state labels using source records, frame indices, camera information, and timestamps. Visual and media quality checks combine first-frame visibility measurements based on canonical-geometry pixel counts and view-frustum tests with documented visual inspection of first frames and selected temporal samples. These inspections assess subject visibility and observation readability, while media decoding and hash checks assess output integrity. Visibility measurements and visual reviews are interpreted within the scope of the recorded frames and geometry.
To preserve provenance and revision history, failed attempts and their available diagnostics are retained separately from accepted episodes. For each batch-specific revision, affected outputs are backed up, changes to world records, camera trajectories, or representation policies are documented, and the corresponding media and metadata are updated together. Hash checks verify the integrity of unchanged source records where applicable. Sample metadata and source hashes link released observations to their source records, rendering configurations, and verification reports.
3.2 Evaluation Suite
We evaluate five complementary capabilities: controllability, visual and temporal quality, logic and state alignment, multi-view consistency, and long-horizon memory. Engine-recorded trajectories, events, and states provide references for evaluating adherence to the prescribed world evolution, while established perceptual metrics assess video quality.
Controllability.
We evaluate whether entities and the camera follow their prescribed trajectories. For entity control, we consider dynamic and interaction-relevant entities visible in the reference view, excluding terrain, effects, and scene geometry. Generated entities are localized with SAM 3 ( Carion et al., 2026 ) from semantic-label prompts and matched to engine-projected references by label-compatible Hungarian matching. BBox IoU measures spatial alignment over the visible trajectory, with missed detections assigned zero IoU, while Center Error measures normalized center displacement conditional on detection. We report clip-level averages over evaluable entities; full visibility, matching, and aggregation details are provided in Appendix 7 .
Interaction Success Rate.
Global similarity alone does not determine whether a prescribed interaction is successfully executed. We therefore evaluate registered engine events, each defined by its participants, temporal window, action, and expected end state. For each event, Qwen3.6-27B ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) is provided with eight frames sampled around the event window, including both full-frame context and an engine-localized crop, and independently judges the prescribed action, expected end state, and participant presence. An event is considered successful only when the response is valid and both the action and end state are judged present.

Logic-Render Alignment.
Logic-Render Alignment measures whether each segment of the action timeline in the engine-derived prompt is visibly depicted within its time window.
For each annotated timeline segment, the judge receives twelve frames sampled from the corresponding temporal window, together with the expected event description and neighboring descriptions as context. The metric evaluates whether the action prescribed by the engine logic is visibly realized within the corresponding segment. Figure 8.4 illustrates the distinction between the two behavioral metrics. ISR evaluates whether a registered interaction is successfully completed, requiring both the prescribed action and expected end state, whereas LRA evaluates whether the generated video realizes the engine-defined event logic by depicting each prescribed action in its corresponding temporal segment.
Memory over Long Horizons.
For the 30-second evaluation, we test whether entities that leave the field of view reappear at the location and in the state prescribed by the engine. We define an absence event when an entity becomes invisible and later re-enters the view. Reappearance IoU measures spatial recovery after re-entry by comparing the reference box with label-compatible detections, with missed detections assigned zero IoU. State Persistence measures whether a returning person preserves the prescribed locomotion state, classified as walking or standing and judged from post-return frames by Qwen3.6-27B ( Qwen Team, 2026 ) . Both metrics are averaged first within scenes and then across scenes with valid events.
Additional metric definitions, implementation details, and evaluation protocols are provided in Appendix 7 .
3.3 Benchmark Tracks
PROWBench organizes its evaluation cases into two tracks with four settings. Each setting uses its own evaluation set, and the scenes of the two main-track settings are disjoint.
Main Track. The main track evaluates controlled generation of five-second clips in two settings that differ in the appearance reference and use disjoint scenes. When the agent already has an appearance reference, such as a concept image or a prescribed character design, it can supply it as the RGB first frame. The Verified-FF setting evaluates this case with 40 scene-camera pairs (31 third-person follow, 9 first-person; top of Table 1 ). Its RGB first frame is part of the benchmark: it is generated with MiniMax-H3 from the colored-OBB proxy and the scene description and checked against the recorded person count, positions, facing and layout (Appendix 6.2 ), and every method that accepts a first frame receives this identical image. In procedural game-world construction, the agent usually has no ready-made RGB appearance reference. The Unverified-FF setting therefore prescribes only the world state, the text, and the control signals, and asks whether models can jointly solve entity control, appearance generation, and identity binding. It contains 90 pairs (78 third-person follow, 12 first-person; bottom of Table 1 ), and appearance must be determined by each model from the text and the control signal. The curation of this set used MiniMax-H3 outputs, so results involving MiniMax-H3 should be read with this potential bias in mind.
Challenge Track. The challenge track tests whether short-clip control extends to two harder settings. Control in short clips does not guarantee that a model maintains one world state over longer horizons, in which entities leave the view, re-enter later and keep their state and motion across generation windows. The long-horizon setting (Table 2 ) contains ten 30-second recordings, one camera each. In a multi-player game, each player observes the same world through their own camera. Even when each view is generated independently, the views must agree on where each participant is, who they are, what they look like, and the scene structure. The multi-view setting (Table 3 ) contains ten four-player scenes, each with four synchronized third-person follow views, one per player, generated independently.
4 Experiments
Our experiments address a central question: can current video generation and video world models faithfully render a prescribed dynamic world, rather than merely produce visually plausible videos? For game worlds, visual quality alone is insufficient. A successful model must follow prescribed entity trajectories, camera motion, and event timelines, while maintaining consistency over long horizons and across synchronized views. We therefore organize the evaluation into a main track of five-second clips and a challenge track of long-horizon and multi-view settings. Sections 4.3 and 4.4 report the results, and Section 4.5 compares the method classes across both tracks and draws the main takeaways. The experimental setup is detailed in Section 4.2 .
4.1 Evaluated Methods
We evaluate 11 models, grouped into three families by the control information they receive.
(a) Camera-conditioned world models.
EchoWM ( Zhang et al., 2026 ) , SANA-WM ( Zhu et al., 2026 ) , LingBot-World 2.0 ( Robbyant Team et al., 2026 ) and AlayaWorld v1.1 ( Team et al., 2026 ) receive an RGB first frame, the text prompt, and the recorded camera trajectory. They obtain explicit camera control but no entity trajectories.
(b) Proxy-video-to-RGB models.
These models translate an engine-rendered control representation into a realistic RGB video. Cosmos-Transfer2.5 ( Ali et al., 2025 ) receives engine depth and class-level segmentation; C2R ( Gomez-Nogales et al., 2026 ) receives the coarse 3D proxy and the text; CWM ( Chen et al., 2026b ) (fine-tuned from MiniMax-H3) receives the CWM proxy, depth, the RGB first frame, and the text;
LynnReal-Omni ( Mao et al., 2026b ) , which builds directly on MiniMax-H3 and extends it with native support for heterogeneous visual controls, including editable 3D renders and game recordings;
and PWM ( Huang et al., 2026a ) receives colored-OBB proxy, the RGB first frame, and the text prompt.
Because PWM is trained only on gunfight scenes, it is evaluated only on the gunfight set of Section 4 .
(c) Video foundation models.
MiniMax-H3 ( MiniMax-AI, 2026 ) , Seedance 2.5 ( ByteDance Seed Team, 2026 ) are used without any adaptation to our proxy representations. They take the colored-OBB proxy video as a reference video together with the text prompt, and RGB references as specified in each table.
4.2 Experimental Setup
All evaluation samples come from recorded game-world states and are scored against the engine state; each sample is one scene observed by one camera. Every recorded state is provided in three proxy representations, Coarse 3D, CWM proxy and Colored-OBB, together with engine depth, the camera trajectory, a scene description and the state of every object, and each method receives the control representation of its native interface. Text prompts are generated automatically from the recorded scene, entities, and action timeline. All models are evaluated in their native output formats against the same engine records; the engine renders of the proxies are scored as diagnostic references. The tracks and settings are defined in Section 3.3 . Methods whose native interface requires an RGB image (the world models and CWM) receive frame 0 of the MiniMax-H3 output for the same scene and camera; this frame is not a prescribed reference, but it can transmit MiniMax-H3’s appearance assignments, and analyses of appearance identity account separately for effects inherited from it.

4.5 Discussion and Takeaways
We now compare the three method classes of Section 4.1 : camera-conditioned world models, task-specific renderers that translate a proxy into RGB video (Cosmos-Transfer2.5, C2R, CWM, LynnReal-Omni and, on the gunfight set, PWM), and general-purpose video foundation models that receive the proxy video as a reference (MiniMax-H3, Seedance 2.5). Figure 3 summarizes the Main-track results by class; unless stated otherwise, comparisons are paired over scenes with a sign test.
Method rankings are closely aligned across the Verified-FF and Unverified-FF settings. The Spearman rank correlations between the per-method means in the two parts of Table 1 are 0.98 0.98 for BBox IoU, 0.98 0.98 for translation error, 0.99 0.99 for CamMC, and 0.93 0.93 for Logic-Render Alignment. We organize the following discussion around seven takeaways.
Figure 3 : Method classes at a glance. Main-track results per method class in Verified-FF (light) and Unverified-FF (dark). Bars show class means, dots the individual methods. Camera-conditioned: EchoWM, SANA-WM, LingBot 2.0, AlayaWorld v1.1; task-specific renderers: Cosmos-Transfer2.5, C2R, CWM, LynnReal-Omni; general-purpose video generation: MiniMax-H3, Seedance 2.5.
T1   Rendering from a proxy beats camera conditioning in following entity trajectories and event timelines.
Both proxy-conditioned classes place entities far better than the camera-conditioned world model: the class-mean BBox IoU is higher in 38 of 40 Verified-FF scenes and 87 of 89 Unverified-FF scenes (median + 0.14 +0.14 , p < 10 − 8 p<10^{-8} ), with class means of 0.450 0.450 (task-specific) and 0.464 0.464 (general-purpose) against 0.322 0.322 in Verified-FF, and 0.376 0.376 and 0.373 0.373 against 0.235 0.235 in Unverified-FF. In Figure 8.3 , the proxy-conditioned models keep both cars in their reference boxes while one of them passes the walking protagonist (per-entity IoU 0.62 0.62 – 0.86 0.86 ); EchoWM keeps the protagonist ( 0.63 0.63 ) but does not bring the oncoming car past the protagonist and lets the car ahead fall back toward the camera instead of keeping its distance ( 0.27 0.27 and 0.29 0.29 ). Camera-conditioned world models do control entities implicitly, through the text prompt, the first frame and, for the protagonist, a follow camera that keeps it in view. This implicit control places the protagonist about as well as the proxy (protagonist BBox IoU 0.43 0.43 and 0.42 0.42 against 0.46 0.46 – 0.54 0.54 for the proxy-conditioned classes, over third-person views) but not the other entities ( 0.26 0.26 and 0.20 0.20 against 0.38 0.38 – 0.44 0.44 ). Events follow the same pattern when the proxy is paired with a strong video prior. Interaction Success checks whether each engine-recorded event reaches both its prescribed action and end state, and Logic-Render Alignment whether each segment of the prescribed timeline is visibly depicted (Figure 8.4 ). General-purpose models exceed the camera-conditioned world models in Logic-Render Alignment in both settings (26 of 32 and 57 of 77 non-tied scenes, p < 10 − 3 p<10^{-3} ) and in Interaction Success in Unverified-FF (42 of 61, p = 0.004 p=0.004 ), and the renderers fine-tuned from MiniMax-H3 are also strong (LynnReal-Omni has the highest Logic-Render Alignment in both settings, jointly with Seedance 2.5 at the displayed precision in Verified-FF). The task-specific class as a whole, however, is not better than the camera-conditioned world models (Interaction Success in 15 of 27 and 29 of 66 scenes), because Cosmos-Transfer2.5 has the lowest Interaction Success and Logic-Render Alignment in both settings. LingBot achieves the highest Interaction Success in Verified-FF, but correct actions and end states do not guarantee correct interaction locations, so this result should be interpreted alongside entity control results.
T2   On camera trajectories, the two are on par: the proxy already encodes the camera.

The proxy video is rendered from the prescribed camera, so it carries the camera motion implicitly. General-purpose models, which receive no camera trajectory, tie with EchoWM, SANA-WM and LingBot 2.0 in CamMC: the class mean is lower in 19 of 40 Verified-FF and 45 of 90 Unverified-FF scenes ( p = 0.87 p=0.87 and p = 1.0 p=1.0 , respectively). Per sample, MiniMax-H3 does not differ significantly from EchoWM, SANA-WM and LingBot 2.0 in CamMC in Verified-FF (sign test, p > 0.2 p>0.2 ) and has a lower translation error than EchoWM and SANA-WM in 74% of the Unverified-FF pairs ( p < 10 − 5 p<10^{-5} ), whereas LingBot 2.0 has lower camera errors than Seedance 2.5 in most pairs of both settings. MiniMax-H3, Seedance 2.5 and LynnReal-Omni all outperform AlayaWorld v1.1 in 87–98% of the pairs in translation error and CamMC. Figure 5 compares each sample with LingBot 2.0, the camera-conditioned model with the lowest CamMC in Unverified-FF: MiniMax-H3 has the lower camera error in 52% of the samples, Seedance 2.5 in 36%, and LynnReal-Omni in 78%.
The composition of the proxy also matters for camera control. Figure 5 examines how proxy components affect camera control across 20 scene-camera pairs. Starting from entity boxes, adding ground reduces CamMC from 0.243 0.243 to 0.213 0.213 , while adding sky provides no further improvement ( 0.223 0.223 ). Incorporating 50% of the background objects produces larger gains, reducing rotation error from 6.89 ∘ 6.89^{\circ} to 3.31 ∘ 3.31^{\circ} , translation error from 0.135 0.135 to 0.105 0.105 , and CamMC from 0.243 0.243 to 0.147 0.147 relative to boxes-only proxies. The illustrated scene exhibits the same pattern: mean rotation error remains near 16 ∘ 16^{\circ} after adding ground and sky, but falls to 4.89 ∘ 4.89^{\circ} when background objects are included. These results suggest that explicit background structure supplies useful spatial cues for reproducing prescribed camera motion. The full-background reference achieves still lower errors, with a rotation error of 1.91 ∘ 1.91^{\circ} and CamMC of 0.109 0.109 . However, this setting also differs in prompts, first-frame inputs, and some entity-box sets, so its additional gains cannot be attributed solely to increased background coverage.
T3   General-purpose video generation models already render short clips well.
On the short, single-camera clips of the main track, general-purpose models are competitive with task-specific renderers across several metrics. They achieve comparable BBox IoU (22 of 40 and 43 of 89 scenes), lower camera errors (CamMC in 29 of 40 and 76 of 90 scenes, p ≤ 0.007 p\leq 0.007 ), and higher Interaction Success and Logic-Render Alignment in Unverified-FF (53 of 67 and 61 of 79 scenes, p ≈ 10 − 6 p\approx 10^{-6} ). They also achieve the lowest warping error, with class means of 0.38 0.38 and 0.33 0.33 , compared with 0.72 0.72 and 0.83 0.83 for task-specific renderers.

Fine-tuning does not, however, yield uniform improvements. CWM and LynnReal-Omni both build on MiniMax-H3, enabling paired comparisons with their general-purpose base. CWM has lower BBox IoU in 71% of the Unverified-FF pairs ( p = 10 − 4 p=10^{-4} ) and lower imaging quality in approximately 90% of the pairs in both settings, but lower warping error in approximately 80%. LynnReal-Omni achieves higher BBox IoU in 62% of the Unverified-FF pairs ( p = 0.03 p=0.03 ) and higher Logic-Render Alignment in 80% of the non-tied Verified-FF pairs ( p = 0.035 p=0.035 ), but higher warping error in every pair in both settings. Because lower warping error can also reflect reduced motion, it should not be interpreted as an unqualified improvement in temporal consistency. These comparisons show that the benefits of fine-tuning depend on the model and the control objective.
Despite their strong performance on short clips, general-purpose models can still struggle to execute prescribed events reliably. We illustrate this limitation on a dedicated evaluation set of 20 gunfight scenes in which one person is shot and collapses according to a prescribed schedule (Table 4 ). T5   The next step is finding the best proxy.
The proxy decides what the renderer is told, but its form matters little for placement. Table 5 compares three proxies on ten Verified-FF pairs (nine follow, one first-person): the coarse-3D-proxy, CWM-proxy and colored-OBB rows are generated with the same first frames, seed and generation settings. All metrics follow the protocol of Table 1 but cover only this subset. The layout barely changes with the proxy (BBox IoU 0.503 0.503 – 0.514 0.514 ), and camera and quality metrics differ little. The event metrics differ more, but only through a few scenes: the colored-OBB proxy has the highest Interaction Success ( 0.733 0.733 against 0.625 0.625 – 0.683 0.683 ; higher than the coarse 3D proxy in three scenes and lower in none, and higher than the CWM proxy in two scenes and lower in one), and the coarse 3D proxy has the highest Logic–Render Alignment ( 0.802 0.802 ; higher than the colored-OBB proxy in four scenes and lower in one); with ten pairs, none of these differences is significant (sign test, p ≥ 0.25 p\geq 0.25 ).
From our visualizations, we observe that, compared with coarse 3D and CWM proxies, colored-OBB proxies generate more natural human motions, with richer joint articulation and more refined strides. Therefore, we use the colored-OBB proxy as the representation input to MiniMax-H3 in our evaluation.
However, the proxy leaves a key ambiguity unresolved: which appearance belongs to which entity. A box proxy fixes where each entity is and how it moves, but not what it looks like; which described appearance belongs to which box must be inferred from the text. Without a visual reference, the model must infer from text which appearance description belongs to which person. As a result, even when the overall scene and actions appear plausible, identity–appearance associations can be incorrect. In the supermarket example in Figure 8.10 a (Appendix 8.2 ), which contains a courier, a student, and a tourist, MiniMax-H3 swaps the student’s and tourist’s outfits throughout the clip. Seedance 2.5 assigns the courier’s outfit to the student’s position while leaving the courier’s box empty, whereas LynnReal-Omni preserves the intended appearance assignment for all three characters. In a manual audit of 62 multi-person scenes, MiniMax-H3 swaps the appearances of two people in 12 cases. These examples suggest that visually plausible multi-person generation can still exhibit errors in maintaining entity identity and associated appearance attributes.
The same ambiguity affects orientation. Without an image that fixes how each entity looks from the camera, models sometimes render an entity facing the opposite way. In the horseback scene of Figure 8.10 b, the horse on the right faces away from the camera: LynnReal-Omni and MiniMax-H3 show its rear, whereas Seedance 2.5 renders it facing the camera. The entity is placed in its box, but its front–back direction is not controlled reliably. The mixed representation in Figure 8.5 (Appendix 8.2 ) likewise omits limbs, whereas the coarse 3D proxy keeps them. A proxy that encodes identity, facing direction and articulation while remaining cheap to render is therefore a direct lever for faithful rendering, and the benchmark can compare candidate proxies under a fixed generator as in Table 5 .

T7   The benchmark reveals what existing metrics miss, but has limitations.
The task metrics capture what placement and perceptual metrics miss. Interaction Success and Logic-Render Alignment are largely independent of entity placement. Within a scene, the Spearman correlation between BBox IoU and Interaction Success across methods averages 0.05 0.05 in Verified-FF and 0.06 0.06 in Unverified-FF (and 0.14 0.14 and 0.12 0.12 for Logic-Render Alignment). Interaction Success and Logic-Render Alignment also differ from each other: in Figure 8.4 , LingBot 2.0 depicts all three prescribed actions (Logic-Render Alignment 1.00 1.00 ) but misses the end state of the first event (Interaction Success 0.67 0.67 ). Logic-Render Alignment responds to the prescription rather than to the video alone: when the judge receives a mismatched timeline, the per-method mean drops from 0.46 0.46 – 0.85 0.85 to 0.07 0.07 – 0.20 0.20 , and the matched timeline scores higher in 68–97% of the samples.
Across methods, the mean CLIP-Score varies by only 4.5 4.5 – 5.7 % 5.7\% of its average and motion smoothness by approximately 1.6 % 1.6\% in both settings, compared with 53 53 – 79 % 79\% for BBox IoU and 38 38 – 58 % 58\% for Interaction Success and Logic-Render Alignment. In Unverified-FF, LynnReal-Omni has the lowest CLIP-Score ( 24.2 24.2 ) while leading all entity, camera and logic metrics. The multi-view setting shows the same for geometric metrics: The results reveal a clear gap between geometric agreement and participant consistency across independently generated views. Thus, a model can generate views that are geometrically coherent while failing to preserve the same participant appearance across viewpoints. Multi-view evaluation should therefore measure both geometric and identity/appearance consistency, since geometric agreement alone is not a reliable proxy for participant consistency. The benchmark also has limitations. Interaction Success, Logic-Render Alignment and the appearance metrics rely on a VLM judge that has not yet been calibrated against human annotation; camera metrics inherit the error of the pose estimator, which reaches 1.1 1.1 – 5.4 ∘ 5.4^{\circ} ( 1.1 1.1 – 2.7 ∘ 2.7^{\circ} in Verified-FF) even on the engine renders; the Unverified-FF set was curated with MiniMax-H3 outputs and all first frames are generated by MiniMax-H3, which may favor it; and the challenge-track sets are small (ten recordings or scenes each).
5 Conclusion
We introduced PROWBench , a benchmark and extensible framework that grounds the evaluation of programmable world models in replayable world states and interaction records. Synchronized visual proxies enable controlled comparisons across representations, viewpoints, and time, while Logic-Render Alignment and Interaction Success Rate complement geometric and perceptual metrics by measuring whether engine-defined rules and events are visibly realized.
Our experiments demonstrate the value of explicit entity-level control: proxy-conditioned models adhere to prescribed trajectories more closely than models receiving camera control alone, and can generate coherent scenes even without an RGB first-frame reference. At the same time, visual plausibility does not guarantee faithful world realization. Appearance assignment, identity binding, and facing direction remain unreliable without a visual reference; entity control and reappearance degrade over longer horizons; and geometric agreement across independently generated views does not ensure consistent participant identity or appearance.
These findings position programmable-world rendering as a problem of preserving executable state across entities, events, time, and viewpoints, rather than optimizing visual quality alone. PROWBench provides a reproducible foundation for measuring progress toward this goal. Reliable generative world renderers will require stronger entity–appearance binding, event-aware generation, long-horizon state memory, and joint multi-view consistency.

PROWBench comprises 170 evaluation cases identified by unique episode IDs, 200 selected camera views, and 600 proxy input videos across four collections: diverse scenarios, multi-participant trajectories, event-driven state transitions, and long-horizon trajectories. Each selected view provides three synchronized representations: coarse 3D, CWM proxy, and Colored-OBB. All source proxy inputs are rendered at 30 FPS: 570 videos are five seconds long with 150 frames each, and 30 videos are 30 seconds long with 900 frames each. There are 540 videos at 1280 × 720 1280\times 720 and 60 event-driven videos at 832 × 480 832\times 480 . This yields 37,500 view-specific camera-frame observations and 112,500 proxy video frames, totaling 62.5 minutes of aggregate proxy-video duration across the three representations.
The diverse-scenario collection contains 130 episodes, divided between the 40-case Verified-FF and 90-case Unverified-FF settings of the main track. Each episode contributes one selected view and three proxy videos, yielding 390 videos. This collection spans seven scene domains and, under the common interaction policy below, nine non-exclusive interaction categories with 227 episode–tag assignments.
The multi-participant collection, Multi4, contains ten episodes with four participants following distinct scripted trajectories each. It covers six trajectory patterns: staggered following, opposing movement, crossing, convergence with turning, overtaking, and parallel turns. Four synchronized third-person following views are selected per episode, yielding 40 views and 120 proxy videos.
The event-driven collection, Gunfight, contains 20 cases derived from ten source recordings. Each case provides one third-person view and three proxy videos, contributing 20 views and 60 videos. These cases vary camera and actor motion around a recorded actor-death event. Episode IDs therefore identify benchmark cases and need not correspond to independent source recordings.
The long-horizon collection contains ten continuous 30-second episodes covering traversal, landmark revisitation, camera look-around, combat, and racing. One view is selected per episode: six first-person and four third-person views, yielding ten views and 30 proxy videos. These cases support the long-horizon evaluation in Section 4.4 and are included in the dataset totals above.
Scene-domain labels combine 130 native annotations with 40 source-reviewed annotations. The resulting counts are city (64), indoor (38), nature (31), racing (12), underwater (10), fantasy (8), and sky (7); city includes airport aprons and industrial outdoor settings. We review interaction labels across all 170 episodes under a common task-level policy, using source descriptions, entity roles, recorded events and trajectories, and sampled proxy frames. Labels cover the authored focal task and its participants, including explicit relative group motion; incidental background co-occurrence is excluded. Weapons, handcarts and furniture count as objects; fixed architectural access counts as human–building interaction; landmark observation and revisitation count as environment exploration. Original labels and per-tag evidence are retained. This AI-assisted review yields 293 episode–tag assignments across nine non-exclusive categories. These are source-defined task labels, including description-supported weapon use in Gunfight, and do not imply independently verified physical contact or hit causality.
Across the four collections, the annotations cover 30 environment labels and 47 semantic action labels for all 170 episodes, including source-supported combat labels for the 20 Gunfight cases. Seven requested visual styles cover all 200 views; the 40 Multi4 views are labeled realistic from explicit conditioning prompts. These labels describe requested styles rather than measured output appearance. The selected cameras comprise 173 third-person views (86.5%) and 27 first-person views (13.5%). Measured over each full recorded clip, the median camera path length is 6.11 m, and the median maximum angular deviation from the initial orientation is 6.60 ∘ 6.60^{\circ} . 6.2 First-Frame Construction
The Verified-FF setting (Section 4.3 ) models the case in which an agent already has access to a concept image or character design. To provide all first-frame-conditioned methods with a common appearance reference while remaining consistent with the prescribed world state, we construct one RGB first frame for each selected view from its colored-OBB proxy and scene description. Because this image becomes part of the benchmark input, it must satisfy both appearance and geometric constraints, including the recorded person count, spatial layout, subject positions, and facing directions. We evaluated several image-generation models and selected MiniMax-H3 because it showed the strongest alignment with the proxy geometry, particularly in person count and layout. The final Verified-FF set contains 40 first frames (17 realistic and 23 stylized), all of which pass the automatic verification described below. Every method that accepts a first frame receives the same RGB image.
Generation. Before generation, the appearance of every entity is fixed in text: each person, vehicle, and animal is assigned a concrete appearance from a reference library matched to the scene and target style, preventing the generator from implicitly choosing identities or appearances. The generation prompt combines three components: (i) a frame-0 layout contract specifying the screen position, extent, and facing direction of every visible entity according to the engine record; (ii) the proxy-control block used by the text prompts (Appendix 6.3 ), which binds geometry to the proxy and appearance to text; and (iii) the scene appearance and style description. MiniMax-H3 receives the first 22 frames of the colored-OBB proxy together with this prompt, without an RGB image reference, and generates a short video. We use frame 0 of the generated clip, at 1280 × 720 1280{\times}720 , as the candidate first frame. For each view-specific instance, we generate three candidates using different random seeds.
Automatic Verification. Each candidate is subjected to hard checks against the frame-0 engine record:
•
Person count : the number of persons detected by SAM 3 [ 7 ] , using the same detector as in evaluation, must equal the number of visible persons recorded by the engine.
•
Box alignment : the mean IoU between detected person boxes and their corresponding engine boxes must be at least 0.30 0.30 ; each visible animal must individually achieve an IoU of at least 0.30 0.30 .
•
Image defects : Qwen3.6-27B [ 41 ] is given the candidate image together with the scene’s style, world, and character descriptions, and must verify that the target style is respected and that the image contains none of the following artifacts: copied proxy geometry (e.g., wireframes, flat-colored mannequins, or boxes), large unlit black surfaces inherited from the proxy, structural defects such as detached doors, or placeholder geometry such as disc-shaped tree crowns or box-shaped props.
•
Heading : for vehicles and mounts with an unambiguous front direction, the visible heading (toward, away from, left of, or right of the camera) must agree with the orientation of the corresponding engine box.
Candidates that fail any check are regenerated with a new seed and additional constraint sentences targeted to the failed criteria, e.g., explicitly requiring every proxy primitive to be replaced by a fully rendered object. Across regeneration rounds, we retain the best candidate that satisfies all hard checks.
6.3 Text Prompts
Each method receives a text prompt for every rendered view of a scene. The underlying engine provides structured metadata for each view, including scene attributes, entity annotations, temporal events, and view-dependent observations. We first construct a unified base prompt from this metadata. Except for the appearance description, all prompt content is generated deterministically from the engine records; the appearance block is the only component produced by a vision–language model. The base prompt is then converted, again deterministically, into the input format required by each method. As a result, prompt construction is reproducible for a fixed set of engine records, and differences across methods arise from their input interfaces and proxy representations. We describe below the source fields, base-prompt construction, the system prompt, representation-specific wording, and the differences between the two main-track settings and across methods. Prompts C.1 and C.2 at the end of this section show the base prompt and a converted prompt for one view.
Source Fields. For each rendered view, the base prompt is constructed from the following records:
•
Scene description : an authored textual description consisting of a target rendering-style line, an environment paragraph, and a protagonist paragraph. The style line is copied verbatim into every prompt. When no first frame is provided, the environment and protagonist descriptions also serve as the source of appearance information.
•
Entity annotations : all dynamic entities and interaction objects, together with their engine identifiers, semantic classes, and role descriptions (e.g., “the receiving companion”). For each entity, we record whether it is visible at frame 0, based on the engine visibility flags and its projected bounding box. If an entity enters the view later, its first entry time is also recorded. In the colored-OBB representation, each entity is additionally assigned a unique identity color.
•
Action timeline : phase boundaries are recomputed from the recorded frames, while retaining the engine-authored sentence associated with each phase. We augment these descriptions with measured facts derived from the recorded trajectories, including the path length of each moving entity (e.g., “moves about 10 metres along its route”), inter-entity relations, final resting locations of moving objects, and occlusion events. These descriptions do not use camera-relative direction terms.
•
Appearance source : in Verified-FF, appearance is derived from the verified first frame; in Unverified-FF, it is taken from the authored scene description.
Camera motion is deliberately excluded from the text prompt and is provided only through the method’s control input, i.e., the proxy video or camera trajectory.
Base-Prompt Construction. Except for the appearance block, the base prompt is generated entirely by a deterministic script, so identical engine records yield identical text. The prompt contains five components. (1) A fixed control block specifies the roles of the two references: the proxy video provides the exact position, extent, orientation, and timing of every subject, but never its target appearance; the RGB first frame, when available, determines subject identity, appearance, and rendering style, but does not prescribe subsequent motion. The control block also defines the precedence of information sources in case of disagreement: geometry, camera, and motion follow the proxy; style and appearance follow the first frame; articulated actions follow the timestamped text. (2) Subject definitions assign each subject a unique name and explicitly bind it to its proxy representation. (3) A one-sentence task summary states the generation objective and shot duration. (4) A retention analysis specifies, for each subject, which properties must be preserved from each reference. (5) A detailed description specifies the rendering style, environment, appearance of every subject, and a contiguous action timeline covering the full five-second clip. The timeline is expressed as consecutive intervals from 00:00.000 to 00:05.000, for example: “From 00:00.000 to 00:04.667, the protagonist walks across the road while the approaching vehicles brake and yield; the protagonist moves about 10 metres along its route; …”.
The appearance block is produced by Qwen3.6-27B [ 41 ] with greedy decoding. Its input consists of two images: the RGB first frame and frame 0 of the CWM proxy render. The latter is used solely as a layout reference for matching people and objects in the first frame to engine entities; it is not treated as an appearance reference. The model outputs a concise description of the opening frame and environment, the appearance and initial pose of each entity, and any discrepancy between the first frame and the recorded layout. To prevent the proxy representation from being mistaken for target appearance, proxy-specific terms such as mannequin, wireframe, and bounding box are disallowed.
Every composed prompt is checked by an automatic validator. The validator requires that (i) every subject is defined, preserved, and described; (ii) only the allowed references are mentioned; (iii) the timeline is contiguous and covers exactly 0–5 s; (iv) prompts without a first frame contain no first-frame references; and (v) the required representation-specific wording is present. If validation fails, the validation errors are returned to the VLM for one regeneration attempt.
System Prompt. The control block plays the role of a system prompt, but how it reaches each model depends on the model’s text interface. CWM is the only method whose interface has a separate system role. Its text encoder applies the Qwen chat template with two turns, a fixed system prompt followed by a user turn containing the <Picture 1> and <Video 1> references and the caption, and encodes the formatted sequence in a single pass.
The release ships two such system prompts, one for the first generation window and one for continuation windows. Our control block is adapted from this system prompt, with three changes for the box proxy: the proxy gives each subject’s exact extent and must be followed precisely (CWM’s prompt treats its proxy as a coarse reference), the white face of a box marks the entity’s front, and the hexadecimal colour tags are identity codes. MiniMax-H3 and LynnReal-Omni use no chat template and no system role: their text encoder receives one token sequence consisting of a label and the visual tokens of each reference (<Picture 1> for the first frame, <Video 1> for the proxy video), followed by the prompt verbatim, so the control block enters as the first part of the prompt (Prompt C.1). The Seedance 2.5 API likewise accepts a single prompt. In Unverified-FF, Seedance 2.5 receives the base prompt with the control block at its start, as MiniMax-H3 does. In Verified-FF, it receives a deterministic conversion of the same base prompt into its reference syntax, as C2R and CWM receive conversions into theirs: the conversion keeps the style line, the appearance of every subject, the binding of each subject to its box by colour name and legend colour, the proxy semantics, and the timestamped timeline, but states the proxy semantics as ordinary sentences instead of a control block (Table 6.3 ). C2R, Cosmos-Transfer2.5, and the camera-conditioned world models receive no system prompt, and their converted prompts contain no proxy terminology (Prompt C.2).
Representation-Specific Wording. Only two parts of the base prompt depend on the proxy representation: the sentence defining the semantics of the proxy video, and the rule used to bind each subject to its proxy.
•
Coarse 3D proxy: the full scene is represented as an untextured light-grey low-poly proxy. People are articulated grey block figures whose recorded limb motion must be preserved. All grey surfaces encode geometry only and must not be interpreted as target colors or materials.
•
CWM proxy : people are represented as flat blue upright mannequins with a fixed pose; they translate and rotate but carry no limb-motion information. Interaction objects are shown as red boxes. The environment is rendered using flat semantic colors that act only as proxy codes and do not correspond to target materials or colors.
•
Colored-OBB: people, vehicles, animals, and interaction objects are represented as semi-transparent solid boxes, with one unique identity color per entity. The color serves only to identify which box corresponds to which subject and must not be interpreted as a target color or material. The white face marks the front of the entity; hence, if the white face is not visible, the entity is facing away from the camera. The white face itself is likewise only a proxy marker and must not appear in the generated result.
For colored-OBBs, each subject definition explicitly identifies its proxy using the hexadecimal legend color. For example: “<Subject 3> is the passenger car: in <Picture 1> a classic red sedan with a white license plate, parked on the right side of the street facing away from the camera; in <Video 1> the object proxy tagged #f79e36, whose white face is its front.” Subjects that are not visible at frame 0 are additionally annotated with their first entry time. In the main track, the methods that receive the box proxy (MiniMax-H3, LynnReal-Omni and Seedance 2.5) use the colored-OBB prompt; C2R, which receives the coarse 3D proxy, and CWM, which receives the CWM proxy, use converted prompts without proxy wording (Table 6.3 ). The coarse 3D and CWM proxy base prompts are used for MiniMax-H3 only in the representation study (Table 5 ).

They differ only in the source of appearance information and in the role of the first frame.

7 Metric Details
Entity control.
We evaluate entities marked as dynamic or interaction candidates, excluding terrain, effects, and scene geometry. Entities that are not visible in the reference view are excluded from evaluation, with additional filtering for first-person views to remove the camera wearer and other entities that dominate the field of view.
Generated entities are localized using SAM 3 [ 7 ] from text prompts derived from their semantic labels.
Generated tracks are matched to reference entities by label-compatible Hungarian matching using track-level mean IoU.

Visual and temporal quality.
Imaging Quality (MUSIQ [ 21 ] ) and Aesthetic Quality (the LAION aesthetic predictor) follow VBench [ 19 ] . Warp Error [ 23 , 33 ] estimates RAFT optical flow between consecutive frames at 672 × 384 672\times 384 , warps the next frame back to the current one, masks occlusions by forward–backward consistency and reports the masked mean squared RGB error ( × 10 − 3 \times 10^{-3} ). Motion Smoothness is the VBench score that reconstructs frames with a video frame-interpolation model [ 28 ] . CLIP-Score [ 42 ] is the mean cosine similarity ( × 100 \times 100 ) between CLIP ViT-B/32 embeddings of the 40 grid frames and the episode’s action title. These metrics are computed without reference to the engine state and therefore measure plausibility, not adherence.
Appearance Compliance and Consistency.
For each intended participant in the multi-view setting, Appearance Compliance ( Comp. ) asks whether the person at the engine-projected location matches the authored appearance description shared by all methods, judged per view at 0.5, 2.5, and 4.5 s from the full frame and a target crop. Cross-view Appearance Consistency ( Cons. ) asks whether the participant looks the same across views: the judge compares every pair of synchronized views without the description, ignoring changes in pose, viewpoint, lighting, and front/back visibility. A participant can thus be consistent yet non-compliant. Both metrics use Qwen3.6-27B with greedy decoding on five attributes (clothing color, clothing style, hair, accessories, and build) and skip attributes that are undescribed or not visible. Geometric multi-view consistency.
We also report MEt3R [ 2 ] , which compares image features after aligning views through geometry predicted from the generated frames. It measures mutual consistency of the generated views, not agreement with the engine geometry, and complements the person-level appearance judgments rather than measuring adherence to an appearance description. We evaluate all six pairs of the four views at nine synchronized times per scene, average both comparison directions, and average scenes equally; lower is better.
