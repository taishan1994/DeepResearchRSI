 Abstract

 
 Mixed-precision quantization (MPQ) assigns a different bitwidth to each linear layer of a large language model (LLM) to minimize the quantization-induced quality loss under a fixed budget, but Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models contain these layers in every expert of every MoE block, so the allocation space grows far larger than in a dense model.
Existing methods either allocate within each block under a uniform per-block budget, or allocate across blocks through an additive proxy, and neither directly optimizes a model-level objective over the choices that couple the blocks.
We propose Q-Strata , a bi-level allocator that ranks within-block assignments with a cheap proxy and allocates across blocks with a model-level objective evaluated on the assembled quantized model.
Its inner stage caches a Pareto frontier of candidates per block over finely spaced budgets, leaving the outer stage to set one budget per block instead of a bitwidth for every linear layer.
With the search reduced to one budget per block, the outer stage optimizes this model-level objective directly, capturing the inter-block coupling that additive proxies miss.
On Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct, Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B, and DeepSeek-V2-Lite, Q-Strata consistently achieves lower WikiText2 perplexity than uniform-bitwidth GPTQ and the state-of-the-art MoE MPQ methods MxMoE and GEMQ in the low-bit regime.
The code is available at https://github.com/snu-mllab/Q-Strata/tree/main .

The inner stage uses the cheap per-block proxy score to cache, for each MoE block, a Pareto frontier of candidate assignments over the budget grid, one assignment per budget level. The outer stage selects one cached candidate per block, that is, one budget β l \beta_{l} per block, to minimize the model-level objective ℒ \mathcal{L} directly under the average-bitwidth budget τ \tau , then assembles them into the global assignment. This reduces the search from a bitwidth per linear layer to one budget per block. 
 
 
 Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models such as Mixtral, DeepSeek-V3, and Qwen3 have become a leading approach for scaling large language models (LLMs) across diverse tasks  ( Jiang et al., 2024 ; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025 ; Yang et al., 2025 ) .
They route each token to a small subset of experts, which enables them to scale up model capacity without compromising compute efficiency.
The trade-off of this design is a large memory footprint, since all experts must be stored even though only a few are active per token, so reducing this footprint is central to real-world deployment and calls for weight compression that exploits the MoE structure.
In particular, we focus on weight-only post-training quantization (PTQ), which compresses the weights of a trained model with only a small calibration dataset and no retraining, reflecting practical deployment constraints  ( Frantar et al., 2023 ; Hubara et al., 2021 ) .

 
 Mixed-precision quantization (MPQ) assigns different bitwidths to the linear layers of a model to minimize the quality lost under a fixed budget, pushing the size-quality frontier of dense models beyond uniform quantization  ( Lee and Song, 2025 ) .
The task is naturally cast as minimizing this quality loss over a high-dimensional discrete space of bitwidth assignments.
Existing methods take one of two routes.
One approximates this loss as a linear sum of layer-wise proxy terms, typically from a Hessian approximation, and solves the allocation with integer linear programming  ( Dong et al., 2020 ; Chen et al., 2021 ) .
The other optimizes a model-level objective, evaluated on the assembled quantized model, in a black-box manner with model-based evolutionary search or reinforcement learning  ( Lee et al., 2025 ; Wang et al., 2020 ; Wang et al., 2019 ) .
The proxy route is suboptimal because it optimizes an approximate objective, whereas the black-box route works with the model-level objective directly and reaches higher quality at the cost of repeated model evaluations.

 
 The problem becomes considerably harder for MoE LLMs.
Each of the L L MoE blocks holds E E experts, and each expert contains three linear layers (gate, up, and down), so the MoE blocks alone hold 3 ​ L ​ E 3LE linear layers, inducing a search space of | 𝒬 | 3 ​ L ​ E |\mathcal{Q}|^{3LE} assignments over the | 𝒬 | |\mathcal{Q}| candidate quantizers, far more than in a dense model.
For instance, within the Qwen3 family, the dense Qwen3-32B has 7 × 64 = 448 7\times 64=448 linear layers, whereas the similarly sized Qwen3-30B-A3B has 3 × 48 × 128 = 18,432 3\times 48\times 128=18{,}432 linear layers in its MoE blocks alone, more than forty times as many. 1 1 
 1 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 The dense count includes all seven linear layers per block, whereas the MoE count includes only the three projections (up, gate, down) of each of the 128 128 experts across the 48 48 blocks, excluding attention and routing. 
Under this high dimensionality, black-box search cannot be applied directly.
MxMoE and MC-MoE instead use proxy scores to capture the differing contributions of the experts and linear layers within each MoE block, which improves over uniform quantization, but they keep every block’s budget uniform and do not allocate across blocks  ( Duanmu et al., 2025 ; Huang et al., 2025 ) .
GEMQ does allocate different budgets across blocks through a global assignment, but it still relies on an additive proxy for model quality, so it misses the dependencies that quantization creates between blocks  ( Deng et al., 2026 ) .
No existing quantization method for MoE LLMs directly optimizes a model-level objective over the choices that couple the blocks.

 
 Our central observation is that bitwidth allocation comprises two distinct strata.
Within a block, a low-cost proxy ranks candidate assignments reliably, as prior MoE methods demonstrate, whereas across blocks the assignments are coupled, so their joint effect shows up only in a model-level objective evaluated on the assembled quantized model.
To optimize this model-level objective directly without searching the 3 ​ L ​ E 3LE -dimensional assignment space, we propose Q-Strata , a bi-level allocator that splits the allocation along these two strata ( Figure   1 ).
Its inner stage caches a Pareto frontier of candidates per block over finely spaced budgets, and its outer stage selects one cached candidate per block to minimize the model-level objective under the global budget, operating over only L L per-block budgets rather than 3 ​ L ​ E 3LE individual bitwidths.
We solve this outer selection with a greedy descent that starts from the most expensive budgets and repeatedly lowers the budget of the block whose reduction raises the objective least, made affordable by a lazy evaluation scheme that recomputes only a few marginals per step  ( Minoux, 1978 ) .
Across Mixtral-8 × \times 7B-Instruct  ( Jiang et al., 2024 ) , Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B  ( Qwen Team, 2024 ) , DeepSeek-V2-Lite  ( DeepSeek-AI et al., 2024 ) , and Qwen3-30B-A3B  ( Yang et al., 2025 ) , Q-Strata achieves the lowest WikiText2 perplexity among the compared methods in the low-bit regime, and its outer search also serves as a standalone allocator on dense models.

 
 2 Preliminaries

 
 2.1 Problem formulation

 
 We study weight-only mixed-precision quantization (MPQ) of a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model with L L MoE blocks indexed by l ∈ [ L ] l\in[L] , where [ n ] = { 1 , … , n } [n]=\{1,\dots,n\} . Each MoE block contains E E experts indexed by e ∈ [ E ] e\in[E] , and each expert holds three linear layers, the up, gate, and down projections of its feed-forward network, indexed by i ∈ ℐ = { up , gate , down } i\in\mathcal{I}=\{\mathrm{up},\mathrm{gate},\mathrm{down}\} . A shared expert, when present, is counted as one of the E E experts. We write W l , e , i W_{l,e,i} for the full-precision weight of the linear layer ( l , e , i ) (l,e,i) , projection i i of expert e e in MoE block l l , and | W l , e , i | |W_{l,e,i}| for its number of parameters. These expert linear layers, N = 3 ​ L ​ E N=3LE in total, are the atomic units of bit allocation. The attention projections, the routing gate, and the embeddings hold a small fraction of the parameters and are kept at a fixed precision, so we allocate bits only over the expert linear layers ( Duanmu et al., 2025 ) .

 
 Given a set of candidate quantizers 𝒬 \mathcal{Q} , an assignment selects a quantizer x l , e , i ∈ 𝒬 x_{l,e,i}\in\mathcal{Q} for each layer, replacing its weight W l , e , i W_{l,e,i} with the quantized weight x l , e , i ​ ( W l , e , i ) x_{l,e,i}(W_{l,e,i}) . We gather these choices into the local assignment 𝐱 l = ( x l , e , i ) e ∈ [ E ] , i ∈ ℐ ∈ 𝒳 l = 𝒬 E × 3 \mathbf{x}_{l}=(x_{l,e,i})_{e\in[E],\,i\in\mathcal{I}}\in\mathcal{X}_{l}=\mathcal{Q}^{E\times 3} of block l l and the global assignment 𝐱 = ( 𝐱 1 , … , 𝐱 L ) ∈ 𝒳 = 𝒬 L × E × 3 \mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{L})\in\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Q}^{L\times E\times 3} .

 ℒ ⁡ ( 𝐱 ) \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}) is a model-level objective that scores the quantized model on the calibration data.
Following the protocol of Lee et al. (2025) , we use the Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) between the next-token distributions of the quantized and full-precision models, where a lower value indicates higher quality.
The full-precision distributions are precomputed once, so evaluating ℒ \mathcal{L} takes a single end-to-end forward pass of the quantized model on the calibration data.

 
 This optimization is especially hard for MoE models, since the dimension of the assignment space, N = 3 ​ L ​ E N=3LE , scales with the number of experts rather than with model size or compute.

 
 Rather than optimize over 𝒳 \mathcal{X} monolithically, we exploit the two-level structure of the MoE model.
The allocation inside a block, over its experts and projections, is captured well by a cheap block-local proxy, an estimate that needs no end-to-end forward of the quantized model, whereas the allocation across blocks couples the blocks in a way that only the model-level objective ℒ \mathcal{L} reflects.
This contrast lets us split this search into two tractable stages, an inner stage that collects, for each block, a set of bit-proxy trade-off solutions, and an outer stage that optimizes the per-block selection from these sets to minimize ℒ \mathcal{L} , the bi-level design we develop in Section   3 .

 
 2.2 Block reconstruction proxy

 
 For the allocation inside a single MoE block, prior MoE quantization methods rely on the block-output reconstruction error  ( Duanmu et al., 2025 ; Huang et al., 2025 ) .
Let h l h_{l} be the output of MoE block l l on the calibration inputs and h ^ l ​ ( 𝐱 l ) \hat{h}_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{l}) its output after quantizing the block with 𝐱 l \mathbf{x}_{l} .
The reconstruction error ∥ h ^ l ​ ( 𝐱 l ) − h l ∥ 2 \lVert\hat{h}_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{l})-h_{l}\rVert^{2} depends only on the local assignment 𝐱 l \mathbf{x}_{l} , but the gating nonlinearity and routing couple the 3 ​ E 3E linear layers, so it is not additive across them.
To make it decomposable, these methods approximate it by a proxy that sums the block-output distortion each linear layer induces in isolation,

 

 
 
 𝒟 l ​ ( 𝐱 l ) = ∑ e ∈ [ E ] ∑ i ∈ ℐ ∥ h ^ l ( e , i ) ​ ( x l , e , i ) − h l ∥ 2 , \mathcal{D}_{l}(\mathbf{x}_{l})=\sum_{e\in[E]}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\big\lVert\hat{h}_{l}^{(e,i)}(x_{l,e,i})-h_{l}\big\rVert^{2}, 
 
 
 
 where h ^ l ( e , i ) ​ ( q ) \hat{h}_{l}^{(e,i)}(q) is the block output when only linear layer ( l , e , i ) (l,e,i) is quantized with quantizer q q and the rest of the block is kept at full precision.
Each distortion is precomputed once per layer and quantizer from a single block forward, and since it is nonzero only on the tokens routed to expert e e , frequently used experts contribute more, matching their larger effect on the block output.
Two properties of this proxy shape our design.
It is block-separable, since 𝒟 l \mathcal{D}_{l} depends only on 𝐱 l \mathbf{x}_{l} while ℒ \mathcal{L} couples all blocks, and it is cheap, since each term needs only a forward of the single block.
We therefore adopt it for the allocation inside each block and reserve ℒ \mathcal{L} for the allocation across blocks.

 
 3 Method

 
 Q-Strata decomposes Equation   1 into two stages that follow the two-level structure of an MoE model ( Figure   1 ).
The inner stage uses the cheap proxy 𝒟 l \mathcal{D}_{l} to precompute, for each MoE block, a small set of Pareto-optimal candidate assignments across budgets, and caches them.
The outer stage optimizes the model-level objective ℒ \mathcal{L} directly by choosing one cached candidate per block.
The inner stage decides how to allocate quantizers to linear layers within each block for each budget, and the outer stage decides how to allocate budget across blocks.

 3.3 Solving the outer problem

 
 The outer problem is a black-box combinatorial optimization over the lattice ℬ L \mathcal{B}^{L} under a budget constraint on the sum of per-block budgets, where each evaluation of ℒ \mathcal{L} is a forward pass of the assembled model.

 
 Exhaustive search over ℬ L \mathcal{B}^{L} is infeasible, and we optimize it with a greedy descent.
Starting from the most expensive corner with β l = β ( K ) \beta_{l}=\beta^{(K)} for all l l , we repeatedly lower one block by a single level until the average bitwidth meets τ \tau .
For a block l l above the bottom level, let 𝜷 − l \boldsymbol{\beta}^{-l} lower β l \beta_{l} by one grid level, giving its marginal loss increase δ l ​ ( 𝜷 ) = ℒ ⁡ ( 𝐱 ⁡ ( 𝜷 − l ) ) − ℒ ⁡ ( 𝐱 ⁡ ( 𝜷 ) ) \delta_{l}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{-l}))-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}(\boldsymbol{\beta})) .
A budgeted greedy would weigh each move’s δ l \delta_{l} against the budget it saves  ( Khuller et al., 1999 ) , but here every one-level move saves the same budget, so we lower the block with the smallest δ l \delta_{l} .
Each step evaluates ℒ \mathcal{L} on an assembled assignment, so the descent captures the coupling between blocks, but a direct implementation recomputes every δ l \delta_{l} at each of the 𝒪 ⁡ ( L ​ K ) \mathcal{O}(LK) steps, 𝒪 ⁡ ( L 2 ​ K ) \mathcal{O}(L^{2}K) evaluations of ℒ \mathcal{L} .

 
 Lazy acceleration.

 
 Our implementation runs this descent with lazy evaluation  ( Minoux, 1978 ) .
We store each block’s last loss increase together with the step at which it was computed, keep them in a min-heap, and at each step pop the smallest, recompute it if stale, and apply the move once the popped value is current.
 Algorithm   2 gives the full procedure.

 
 This procedure reproduces the eager descent as long as a stale loss increase only underestimates its current value, so that the min-heap never hides the least harmful move. Our ℒ \mathcal{L} carries no guarantee of this condition, so we use lazy evaluation as a heuristic, justified empirically by the near-monotone growth of the marginals along the descent ( Figure   2 ). Definition   3.1 states the diminishing-returns property under which the condition holds exactly.

 Remark 3.2 . 

 
 If G = − ℒ G=-\mathcal{L} is DR-submodular ( Definition   3.1 ), this lazy descent commits the same moves as the eager descent that recomputes all L L marginals each step, while evaluating ℒ \mathcal{L} fewer times ( Minoux, 1978 ; Soma and Yoshida, 2018 ) .

 
 Empirically, ℒ \mathcal{L} is close to this property. A more compressed model is more fragile, so lowering one more bitwidth level hurts quality more once few bits remain. We check this on DeepSeek-V2-Lite by following random descent paths and recording, one block at a time, the resulting loss increase δ l \delta_{l} from a single bitwidth level ( Figure   2 ). This δ l \delta_{l} , which the heap caches, grows near-monotonically as the model is compressed, so a value cached at a higher budget typically underestimates its current one, the condition the lazy descent relies on. Appendix   A gives the protocol.

 
 Cost.

 
 The outer search’s cost is its evaluations of ℒ \mathcal{L} , each a forward pass of the assembled model.
The descent lowers budgets monotonically and uses τ \tau only as a stopping point, so a single top-to-bottom sweep yields the assignment for every target budget along the way.
Writing R R for the average number of evaluations per committed move, the lazy descent issues 𝒪 ⁡ ( R ​ L ​ K ) \mathcal{O}(RLK) , smaller than the eager 𝒪 ⁡ ( L 2 ​ K ) \mathcal{O}(L^{2}K) by a factor L / R L/R .
In practice, R R stays near 2.5 2.5 , far below the 24 24 to 32 32 blocks ( Table   6 in Appendix   A ).

 
 4 Experiments

 
 4.1 Setup

 
 We mainly evaluate three MoE LLMs of different scales, Mixtral-8 × \times 7B-Instruct (46.7B - A12.9B), Qwen1.5-MoE (14.3B - A2.7B), and DeepSeek-V2-Lite (15.7B - A2.4B) ( Jiang et al., 2024 ; Qwen Team, 2024 ; DeepSeek-AI et al., 2024 ) , and scale the comparison up to Qwen3-30B-A3B  ( Yang et al., 2025 ) in Section   D.6 .
All compared methods quantize weights with GPTQ  ( Frantar et al., 2023 ) and share one quantizer set, the group-128 asymmetric formats at 1 1 , 2 2 , 3 3 , and 4 4 bits.
The budget grid spans 1.25 1.25 to 4.25 4.25 bits in steps of 0.125 0.125 , giving K = 25 K=25 levels.
We report WikiText2 perplexity and the average accuracy over six zero-shot tasks, PIQA, BoolQ, WinoGrande, ARC-easy, ARC-challenge, and HellaSwag ( Bisk et al., 2020 ; Clark et al., 2019 ; Sakaguchi et al., 2019 ; Clark et al., 2018 ; Zellers et al., 2019 ) .

 
 The baselines are uniform GPTQ, MxMoE, and GEMQ.
Uniform GPTQ gives every linear layer the same bitwidth ( Frantar et al., 2023 ) .
MxMoE shares our inner stage but assigns every MoE block the same budget τ \tau rather than optimizing per-block budgets, making it the counterpart that omits our outer stage ( Duanmu et al., 2025 ) .
GEMQ allocates bitwidth globally at expert granularity, solving an ILP that minimizes a gradient-based additive proxy for model quality ( Deng et al., 2026 ) .
Uniform GPTQ, MxMoE, and Q-Strata apply the quantizers after a random Hadamard rotation without online rotation, following the protocol of Duanmu et al. (2025) ( Ashkboos et al., 2024 ) , so differences among these three reflect bit allocation alone.
GEMQ is reproduced under a shared protocol, labeled GEMQ (shared) in Table   1 , by taking its allocation from its own gradient-proxy ILP and deploying it with the shared quantizer set, keeping its original rotation-free setting and omitting its router fine-tuning (RFT) step, an additional training stage. 2 2

 GEMQ’s original pipeline further differs from the shared protocol in its GPTQ variant (MSE range search vs. min-max), its 1-bit format (symmetric, 1.125 effective bits, vs. asymmetric, 1.25), and its evaluation context length (2048 vs. our 4096). 
 Section   4.4 additionally compares Q-Strata against GEMQ with the RFT of its official recipe restored, with and without rotation.

 5 Related Work

 
 MPQ is often cast as a multiple-choice knapsack problem  ( Chen et al., 2021 ; Sinha and Zoltners, 1979 ; Malinovskii et al., 2025 ; Lee and Song, 2025 ) . A line of work approximates the loss with an additive proxy, a sum of per-layer importance scores estimated either from the backward signal  ( Dong et al., 2019 ; Dong et al., 2020 ; Yao et al., 2021 ; Kim et al., 2026 ; Deng et al., 2026 ) or from the loss change under full forward passes  ( Malinovskii et al., 2025 ; Lee and Song, 2025 ) , and solves the allocation with dynamic programming or integer programming  ( Pferschy and Scatamacchia, 2017 ; Gurobi Optimization, LLC, 2026 ) . For dense models, AMQ runs an AutoML-style search with search-space pruning and a model-based evolutionary method, scoring candidates with full forward passes  ( Lee et al., 2025 ) .
On the activation side, TWLA’s inter-layer-aware ILA-AMP allocates activation bitwidths across layers  ( Zhao et al., 2026 ) , a complementary axis to the weight-only allocation we study.

 
 For MoE models, prior methods score within-block assignments with block-local proxies. MC-MoE assigns a bitwidth to every expert with a linear program over a hand-designed score combining reconstruction error and routing statistics  ( Huang et al., 2025 ) , and MxMoE works at the finer linear-layer granularity, minimizing a sum of block-wise reconstruction-error proxies  ( Duanmu et al., 2025 ) , but both keep every block’s budget uniform. GEMQ allocates across blocks through an additive proxy resting on a first-order approximation of the loss  ( Deng et al., 2026 ) . Our inner stage keeps MxMoE’s linear-layer granularity, while our outer stage optimizes the model-level objective directly across blocks.

 
 In concurrent work, ScaleBits  ( Li et al., 2026 ) quantizes dense models at the tile level. It overlaps with us in a diminishing-returns property over its search space and a greedy allocator, but its space is far larger and finer than our per-block budgets, so it ranks moves by sensitivity-based estimates rather than by measured marginals of its objective. Our bi-level hierarchy shrinks the outer space enough to run the greedy on the objective’s marginals directly, and we use lazy evaluation only to cut redundant queries.

 
 6 Conclusion

 
 We introduced Q-Strata , a bi-level allocator for mixed-precision quantization of MoE LLMs.
Its inner stage ranks within-block assignments with a cheap proxy and caches one candidate per budget level, so the outer stage only needs to choose one budget per block.
This reduction lets the outer stage optimize the model-level objective directly with a lazy greedy descent, capturing the coupling between blocks that additive proxies miss.
On three MoE LLMs, Q-Strata achieves lower WikiText2 perplexity than uniform GPTQ, MxMoE, and GEMQ in the low-bit regime, and the gains over MxMoE persist on the larger Qwen3-30B-A3B.
The outer search also works as a standalone allocator on dense models, matching the black-box search of AMQ with far fewer objective queries.

 
 Limitations

 
 The outer stage evaluates the model-level objective directly, so each move is a forward pass of the assembled model.
Even with lazy evaluation, this issues more such evaluations than allocators that fit an additive proxy once and then solve it in closed form.
The outer search’s evaluation count grows with the block count L L rather than with the total number of linear layers 3 ​ L ​ E 3LE , since the evaluations per move stay near 2.5 and far below L L ( Table   6 ).
Because it runs once before deployment and adds nothing to inference latency, its cost is amortized over all subsequent inferences.
It nonetheless remains heavier than proxy-only allocation at search time, and lowering it further is left to future work.

 Appendix A Empirical diminishing returns

 
 The lazy descent ( Remark   3.2 ) is exact when the per-block marginal loss increase δ l ​ ( 𝜷 ) = ℒ ⁡ ( 𝐱 ⁡ ( 𝜷 − l ) ) − ℒ ⁡ ( 𝐱 ⁡ ( 𝜷 ) ) \delta_{l}(\boldsymbol{\beta})=\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{-l}))-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}(\boldsymbol{\beta})) does not shrink as the model is compressed, equivalently when the marginal gain of G = − ℒ G=-\mathcal{L} from restoring one level diminishes as the model grows more precise, which is the condition of Definition   3.1 .
We probe this on DeepSeek-V2-Lite, following ScaleBits  ( Li et al., 2026 ) , which verifies the same property for MPQ at a different granularity.

 
 For each of five trials we sample one descent chain and one block l l .
A chain starts at the top corner 𝜷 = ( β ( K ) , … , β ( K ) ) \boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta^{(K)},\dots,\beta^{(K)}) and lowers a uniformly random block by one level at a time down to the bottom corner, a monotone path along which the effective bitwidth b ¯ ​ ( 𝜷 ) \bar{b}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) decreases.
At each configuration along the chain where block l l is below the top level, we raise β l \beta_{l} by one level and record the marginal loss increase ℒ ⁡ ( 𝐱 ⁡ ( 𝜷 ) ) − ℒ ⁡ ( 𝐱 ⁡ ( 𝜷 + l ) ) \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}(\boldsymbol{\beta}))-\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{+l})) that its current level carries relative to one level up, on the calibration set used by the outer stage.
We min-max normalize this quantity within each chain and plot it against b ¯ ​ ( 𝜷 ) \bar{b}(\boldsymbol{\beta}) in Figure   2 .

 
 All five curves rise near-monotonically as the bitwidth falls, so this marginal grows as the model is compressed.
The loss increase δ l \delta_{l} the lazy heap caches therefore grows along the descent, so a value cached earlier at a higher budget underestimates its value later, exactly what the heap needs.
 ℒ \mathcal{L} is not strictly DR-submodular and a few small reversals appear, but the trend is monotone enough for the cached values to serve as the underestimates the lazy heap relies on, at the per-move cost reported in Table   6 .

 Appendix C Outer-stage baselines

 
 The baselines of Table   2 share the inner caches { 𝒮 l } \{\mathcal{S}_{l}\} and differ only in how they pick a budget β l ∈ ℬ \beta_{l}\in\mathcal{B} per block.

 
 Uniform.

 
 Allocate the same bitwidth for all MoE blocks.

 
 One-shot ILP.

 
 This baseline modularizes ℒ \mathcal{L} once.
From the top assignment 𝜷 top = ( β ( K ) , … , β ( K ) ) \boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{top}}=(\beta^{(K)},\dots,\beta^{(K)}) , for each block l l and lower level β ( k ) \beta^{(k)} it measures the marginal m l , k = ℒ ⁡ ( x ′ ) − ℒ ⁡ ( x ⁡ ( 𝜷 top ) ) m_{l,k}=\mathcal{L}(x^{\prime})-\mathcal{L}(x(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\mathrm{top}})) , where x ′ x^{\prime} lowers only block l l to β ( k ) \beta^{(k)} .
Treating ∑ l m l , k l \sum_{l}m_{l,k_{l}} as a separable surrogate for ℒ \mathcal{L} , it selects one level per block by an MCKP under the budget constraint, solved exactly with an ILP.
The surrogate needs only ( K − 1 ) ​ L + 1 (K-1)L+1 evaluations of ℒ \mathcal{L} , all at the top model, but it assumes the per-block reductions do not interact, an assumption that breaks as several blocks are compressed together.
This is the search used by Lee and Song (2025) .

 
 Ascending greedy.

 
 This is Algorithm   2 run in the opposite direction.
It starts from the cheapest corner ( β ( 1 ) , … , β ( 1 ) ) (\beta^{(1)},\dots,\beta^{(1)}) and repeatedly raises the block whose one-level increase lowers ℒ \mathcal{L} the most, until the average bitwidth reaches τ \tau .
It uses the same lazy heap and staleness check as our descent, and differs only in its starting corner and the direction of each move.

 
 Direct cell-level ILP ( Table   3 ).

 
 This pruning-free baseline measures the change in ℒ \mathcal{L} when each (expert, linear layer, bitwidth) cell alone is lowered from the top assignment, and solves the global assignment over raw cells as a single MCKP with an ILP.
The one-shot ILP variant of Table   3 applies the same one-shot measurement and ILP on top of our inner-stage caches, so comparing the two isolates the effect of the frontier pruning and the block decomposition.

 Appendix E Budgeted descent for dense models

 
 The outer descent needs one adjustment on dense models.
In the MoE setting every block holds the same number of parameters, so every one-level move frees the same amount of budget, and Algorithm   2 ranks moves by the loss increase alone.
On a dense model the choice groups are individual layers, and layers differ in parameter count.
Writing w i w_{i} for the parameter count of layer i i , the average bitwidth becomes the size-weighted mean b ¯ = ∑ i w i ​ b i / ∑ i w i \bar{b}=\sum_{i}w_{i}b_{i}\,/\,\sum_{i}w_{i} , and lowering layer i i by one level frees a budget proportional to w i w_{i} .
A move on a large layer therefore saves more budget than one on a small layer, and the descent ranks moves by the loss increase per unit of budget freed, Δ ​ ℒ i / ( w i ​ Δ ) \Delta\mathcal{L}_{i}/(w_{i}\Delta) , where Δ \Delta is the spacing of the budget grid  ( Khuller et al., 1999 ) .
 Algorithm   2 changes in two places only.
The min-heap is keyed by this ratio instead of Δ ​ ℒ i \Delta\mathcal{L}_{i} alone, and the stopping test compares b ¯ \bar{b} with τ \tau .
The lazy refresh and the staleness check are unchanged.
