 Abstract

 
 The Key-Value (KV) cache constitutes a dominant source of memory consumption during large language model (LLM) inference, particularly in long-context and complex reasoning scenarios.
Existing KV cache quantization methods have explored different compression granularities, including layer-wise precision adjustment and token-level information preservation.
In practical deployment, however, the key challenge is not merely compression, but how to allocate limited quantization resources among heterogeneous precision preservation strategies under a fixed KV cache memory budget.
We propose MultiAlloc , a profiling-guided precision allocation framework for KV cache quantization that adaptively distributes limited quantization resources between layer-wise precision enhancement and token-wise preservation.
MultiAlloc employs lightweight offline profiling to characterize layer sensitivity and token importance, enabling adaptive precision allocation under a fixed memory constraint.
Guided by the profiled memory–accuracy trade-off, it automatically derives an effective precision allocation strategy without online allocation search, and applies the resulting policy during inference.
Extensive experiments on reasoning and long-context benchmarks demonstrate that MultiAlloc consistently improves over layer-wise, token-wise, and recent multi-granularity KV cache quantization methods under matched memory budgets across diverse models, including Mistral, Llama, DeepSeek-Qwen, and QwQ.

 

 
 

 
 1 Introduction

 
 Figure 1: 
Motivation for budget-aware precision allocation under a fixed KV cache budget.
Different layers exhibit heterogeneous demands for layer precision enhancement
and token preservation. Existing layer-wise and token-wise methods optimize
individual preservation strategies, while MultiAlloc profiles these
demands and allocates the shared memory budget across preservation strategies.
 
 
 
 As large language models (LLMs) become increasingly integral to long-context reasoning, complex question answering, and multi-step planning, their growing inference-time memory footprint has emerged as a critical bottleneck to practical deployment  Xiao et al. (2024) ; Kwon et al. (2023) ; Liu et al. (2026) .
In long-sequence generation, the
Key–Value (KV) cache constitutes a dominant source of GPU memory consumption,
making efficient KV cache compression a central challenge for scalable and
cost-effective LLM inference  Zhang et al. (2026) ; Wang et al. (2026) .

 
 Recent studies on KV cache quantization have developed increasingly targeted strategies to reduce memory overhead while preserving model accuracy.
KIVI  Liu et al. (2024) introduces asymmetric low-bit quantization by applying channel-level quantization to keys and token-level quantization to values, substantially reducing KV cache memory consumption.
Subsequent token-oriented methods further improve this direction, with ZipCache  He et al. (2024) identifying salient tokens and OTT  Su et al. (2025) tracing and preserving outlier tokens at higher precision to reduce token quantization errors.
However, these methods largely overlook the heterogeneous sensitivity of different layers.
In contrast, KVTuner  Li et al. (2025) and PM-KVQ  Liu et al. (2026) allocate mixed precision according to layer or block sensitivity, thereby reducing quantization errors caused by layer heterogeneity.
Nevertheless, they generally apply uniform token treatment within each layer and do not explicitly preserve token-level heterogeneity.
Recent multi-granularity methods, such as KVmix  Li et al. (2026) , further explore the joint adaptation of layer-wise precision and token-level preservation by incorporating layer importance and pivotal context selection.
However, their token-level adaptation mainly focuses on context selection rather than precision allocation based on heterogeneous token importance.

 
 Motivated by these observations, we formulate KV cache quantization as a precision allocation problem under a fixed memory budget.
We propose MultiAlloc , a profiling-guided precision allocation framework that characterizes heterogeneous precision demands across layers and tokens.
Specifically, MultiAlloc employs layer-wise sensitivity profiling to estimate the precision requirements of different layers, while token-wise importance profiling identifies tokens that benefit from high-precision preservation.
Based on these complementary profiling signals, MultiAlloc adaptively determines the allocation of limited quantization resources between layer-wise precision enhancement and token-wise high-precision preservation.
All allocation decisions are derived offline and applied as a static inference-time policy, eliminating online allocation search during inference.

 
 We conduct extensive experiments on reasoning and long-context benchmarks, including GSM8K, GPQA, and LongBench-v2, using models ranging from 7B to 32B parameters, including Llama-3-8B-Instruct  Grattafiori et al. (2024) , Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3  Jiang et al. (2023) , DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B/14B  DeepSeek-AI (2025) , and QwQ-32B  Qwen Team (2025) .
The results demonstrate that MultiAlloc consistently outperforms representative layer-wise, token-wise, and importance-based KV cache quantization methods under matched KV cache memory budgets, with robust improvements across different model scales and both reasoning and long-context tasks.

 
 Our contributions are summarized as follows:

 
 • 
 
 We reveal heterogeneous precision demands across layers and tokens in KV cache quantization, motivating precision allocation under a fixed KV cache memory budget.

 • 
 
 We propose MultiAlloc , a profiling-guided precision allocation framework that models layer-wise sensitivity and token importance to adaptively allocate limited quantization resources between layer precision enhancement and token preservation.

 • 
 
 Extensive experiments across diverse models and reasoning and long-context benchmarks demonstrate that MultiAlloc consistently outperforms representative layer-wise, token-wise, and importance-based KV cache quantization methods under matched KV cache memory budgets.

 

 
 2 Related Work

 
 KV Cache Quantization and Precision Allocation. 
Recent KV cache quantization methods explore different strategies to reduce quantization errors while preserving inference quality.
KIVI  Liu et al. (2024) introduces asymmetric low-bit quantization by applying channel-level quantization to keys and token-level quantization to values.
Token-aware approaches, such as ZipCache  He et al. (2024) and OTT  Su et al. (2025) , preserve salient tokens at higher precision to mitigate token quantization errors.
Layer-wise methods, including KVTuner  Li et al. (2025) and PM-KVQ  Liu et al. (2026) , allocate mixed precision according to layer sensitivity.
KVmix  Li et al. (2026) further explores layer- and token-level adaptation, while KV-COBRA  Ha et al. (2026) studies budget-aware allocation of compression configurations under fixed memory constraints.
MoE-nD  Sun et al. (2026) extends KV compression to multiple dimensions by jointly considering quantization and token reduction.
However, existing approaches mainly optimize individual compression strategies or configurations, without explicitly modeling heterogeneous precision demands across granularities or allocating a shared budget between different precision preservation strategies.

 
 KV Cache Memory Management. 
Beyond quantization, another line of work focuses on managing KV cache memory usage during LLM inference.
PagedAttention  Kwon et al. (2023) improves memory efficiency through virtual memory paging, while subsequent approaches such as H2O  Zhang et al. (2023) , StreamingLLM  Xiao et al. (2024) , SqueezeAttention  Wang et al. (2024) , XKV  Li et al. (2024) , and KVPR  Jiang et al. (2025) reduce memory pressure through token eviction, retention, or offloading strategies.
These methods primarily optimize which KV entries to retain or where to store them, whereas MultiAlloc focuses on allocating limited quantization resources among different precision preservation strategies within the KV cache representation.

 
 Differentiation of Our Work. 
Existing KV cache quantization methods have demonstrated the effectiveness of preserving important layers or tokens at higher precision.
However, they mainly focus on identifying important components or adapting individual precision strategies, without explicitly modeling heterogeneous precision demands across different granularities.
In contrast, MultiAlloc introduces a profiling-guided precision allocation framework that determines how limited quantization resources should be distributed between layer-wise precision enhancement and token-wise high-precision preservation under a fixed KV cache memory budget.

 
 3 Preliminary

 
 3.1 Generative Inference and KV Cache

 
 Transformer-based Large Language Models (LLMs) generate text autoregressively in two phases: prefilling and decoding .
Given a prompt sequence of length T i ​ n T_{in} , prefilling processes all tokens in parallel to generate the initial Key-Value (KV) cache, while decoding generates new tokens token-by-token.
To avoid recomputing historical representations, the Key and Value vectors of past tokens are cached in GPU memory.
For an L L -layer Transformer, let 𝐡 l , t ∈ ℝ d \mathbf{h}_{l,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} denote the hidden state of the t t -th token at layer l l . The query, key, and value vectors are computed via linear projections:

 (1) 
 
 
 where 𝐖 l Q , 𝐖 l K , 𝐖 l V ∈ ℝ d × d \mathbf{W}^{Q}_{l},\mathbf{W}^{K}_{l},\mathbf{W}^{V}_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d} are learnable weight matrices.
At decoding step t > T i ​ n t>T_{in} , the query 𝐪 l , t \mathbf{q}_{l,t} attends to all cached keys and values. The KV cache is updated by concatenating the new vectors along the sequence dimension:

\mathbf{V}_{l,\leq t}&=\text{Concat}(\mathbf{V}_{l,<t},\mathbf{v}_{l,t}).\end{split} 
 
 (2) 
 
 
 The attention output 𝐨 l , t \mathbf{o}_{l,t} is:

 

 
 
 𝐨 l , t = softmax ​ ( 𝐪 l , t ​ 𝐊 l , ≤ t ⊤ d ) ​ 𝐕 l , ≤ t . \mathbf{o}_{l,t}=\text{softmax}\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}_{l,t}\mathbf{K}_{l,\leq t}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\mathbf{V}_{l,\leq t}. 
 
 (3) 
 
 
 As the sequence length T T grows, the KV cache footprint scales linearly as O ⁡ ( L ⋅ T ⋅ d ) O(L\cdot T\cdot d) , often exceeding model-weight memory in long-context scenarios and becoming a primary inference bottleneck.

 
 3.2 KV Cache Quantization

 
 To alleviate this bottleneck, Post-Training Quantization (PTQ) compresses the KV cache into low-bit representations.
For a tensor 𝐗 \mathbf{X} (representing either 𝐊 \mathbf{K} or 𝐕 \mathbf{V} ), uniform affine quantization maps floating-point values to b b -bit integers:

 

 
 
 𝐗 ¯ = clamp ​ ( ⌊ 𝐗 s + z ⌉ , 0 , 2 b − 1 ) , \bar{\mathbf{X}}=\text{clamp}\left(\left\lfloor\frac{\mathbf{X}}{s}+z\right\rceil,0,2^{b}-1\right), 
 
 (4) 
 
 
 where s s and z z denote the scale factor and zero-point, respectively. The dequantized approximation is reconstructed as 𝐗 ^ = ( 𝐗 ¯ − z ) ⋅ s \hat{\mathbf{X}}=(\bar{\mathbf{X}}-z)\cdot s .
The quantization error ℰ = ‖ 𝐗 − 𝐗 ^ ‖ F 2 \mathcal{E}=\|\mathbf{X}-\hat{\mathbf{X}}\|_{F}^{2} stems primarily from rounding and outlier clamping.
Conventional quantization approaches often apply uniform bit-widths across layers and tokens, overlooking the heterogeneous precision requirements within KV cache representations.

 
 However, this overlooks the heterogeneous sensitivity of KV cache representations: (1) certain layers are more robust to quantization noise and can tolerate lower bit-widths, while (2) specific tokens contribute disproportionately to quantization errors due to their distinct value distributions.

 4.1 Problem Formulation and Overview

 
 We formulate KV cache quantization as a precision allocation problem under
a fixed KV cache memory budget.
Given a Transformer model with L L layers, we aim to determine a static
quantization policy that allocates limited quantization resources between
layer-wise precision enhancement and token-wise high-precision preservation.

 
 Specifically, let
 𝐪 = { q l } l = 1 L \mathbf{q}=\{q_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L} denote the layer-wise bit-width
configuration, where q l q_{l} represents the quantization precision of the KV
cache at layer l l .
Meanwhile, let
 𝐤 = { k l } l = 1 L \mathbf{k}=\{k_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L} denote the token preservation configuration,
where k l k_{l} represents the number of high-precision tokens preserved per
group at layer l l .
The resulting policy is represented as
 π = ( 𝐪 , 𝐤 ) \pi=(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}) .

 
 Given a fixed KV cache memory budget B B , the objective is to minimize the
quantization-induced degradation while satisfying the memory budget:

 

 
 
 min 𝐪 , 𝐤 ⁡ ℒ ⁡ ( 𝐪 , 𝐤 ) s . t . ℳ ⁡ ( 𝐪 , 𝐤 ) ≤ B . \min_{\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k})\quad\mathrm{s.t.}\quad\mathcal{M}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k})\leq B. 
 
 (5) 
 
 
 where ℒ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \mathcal{L}(\cdot) denotes the estimated degradation caused by KV
cache compression and ℳ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) \mathcal{M}(\cdot) denotes the corresponding memory
consumption.

 
 Directly optimizing this objective requires searching over a large number of
layer-wise precision and token preservation configurations.
To address this challenge, MultiAlloc adopts an offline profiling and
allocation pipeline, as illustrated in Figure  2 .
First, the decoupled profiling stage estimates layer-wise precision demands
and token-wise preservation demands from calibration data.
Then, the budget-coupled precision allocation module evaluates candidate
policies and determines the allocation of quantization resources between
different preservation strategies.
Finally, the selected static policy is directly applied during online
inference without additional allocation search.

 
 4.2 Layer-wise Precision Demand Estimation

 
 Different Transformer layers exhibit different sensitivity to KV cache
quantization, resulting in heterogeneous requirements for quantization
precision.
To estimate layer-wise precision demands, MultiAlloc profiles the
quantization-induced degradation of each layer under different bit-widths.

 
 Let 𝒬 b ​ ( ⋅ ) \mathcal{Q}_{b}(\cdot) denote the quantization operator with bit-width
 b b .
For the KV cache of layer l l , the quantization errors of keys and values are
defined as:

 

 
 
 Δ ​ 𝐊 l ( b ) = 𝒬 b ​ ( 𝐊 l ) − 𝐊 l , Δ ​ 𝐕 l ( b ) = 𝒬 b ​ ( 𝐕 l ) − 𝐕 l . \Delta\mathbf{K}_{l}^{(b)}=\mathcal{Q}_{b}(\mathbf{K}_{l})-\mathbf{K}_{l},\quad\Delta\mathbf{V}_{l}^{(b)}=\mathcal{Q}_{b}(\mathbf{V}_{l})-\mathbf{V}_{l}. 
 
 (6)

 (7)

and

 4.3 Token-wise Preservation Demand Estimation

 
 Besides layer-wise precision differences, KV cache quantization also exhibits
token-level heterogeneity, where a small subset of tokens can contribute
disproportionately to inference quality.
 MultiAlloc therefore estimates token-wise preservation demands to
determine how much high-precision storage should be allocated to different
layers.

 
 For a token representation 𝐊 l , t \mathbf{K}_{l,t} at layer l l , we measure its
importance using the activation magnitude:

 

 
 
 s l , t = ‖ 𝐊 l , t ‖ 1 , s_{l,t}=\|\mathbf{K}_{l,t}\|_{1},

 which provides a lightweight proxy for the contribution of token t t to
attention computation.

 
 During inference, tokens are organized into groups of size G G .
For each group, MultiAlloc preserves the top- k l k_{l} tokens with
higher precision according to their importance scores, while the remaining
tokens are quantized using the layer-wise assigned precision.
To estimate the preservation demand, we measure the importance mass lost after
preserving k l k_{l} tokens in each group:

Together with the layer-wise precision profiles, these estimates provide

 4.4 Budget-Coupled Precision Allocation

 
 The layer-wise and token-wise profiling stages provide complementary
precision demand signals for allocation.
However, both precision enhancement strategies compete for the same KV cache
memory budget.
Therefore, MultiAlloc performs budget-coupled precision allocation
to determine how limited quantization resources should be distributed between
layer precision enhancement and token preservation.

 By coupling token preservation decisions with layer-wise precision allocation,
 MultiAlloc avoids manually specified budget splits and adaptively
allocates quantization resources according to heterogeneous precision
demands.

 
 4.5 Static Policy Execution

 
 After the offline allocation stage, MultiAlloc obtains a static
precision policy π ∗ \pi^{*} , which specifies the layer-wise precision
configuration and token preservation configuration.
During inference, the policy is directly applied to incoming KV caches
without additional allocation search.

 
 Specifically, for each layer l l , newly generated KV states are quantized
according to the assigned bit-width q l ∗ q_{l}^{*} .
Meanwhile, tokens are processed in groups according to the selected
preservation configuration k ∗ k^{*} .
Within each group, the top- k l ∗ k_{l}^{*} tokens with higher importance scores are
stored in high precision, while the remaining tokens are quantized using the
corresponding layer-wise precision.

 
 The resulting KV cache combines quantized representations with preserved
high-precision tokens, enabling efficient long-context inference
under the fixed KV cache memory budget.

 
 5 Experiments

 
 5.1 Experimental Setup

 
 We evaluate reasoning and long-context performance on five instruction-tuned
models spanning architectures and scales:
Llama-3-8B-Instruct  Grattafiori et al. (2024) ,
Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3  Jiang et al. (2023) ,
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B and DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B  DeepSeek-AI (2025) ,
and QwQ-32B  Qwen Team (2025) .
We report accuracy on GSM8K  Cobbe et al. (2021) and
GPQA  Rein et al. (2024) , together with macro-averaged accuracy across the six
task categories of LongBench-v2  Bai et al. (2025) .
The overall score averages the GPQA, GSM8K, and LongBench-v2 macro-average
scores.
Dataset statistics and evaluation details are provided in Appendix A.
All methods use identical prompts, tokenization, stopping criteria, and greedy
decoding. All experiments are conducted on NVIDIA A100 GPUs.

 
 For each model, we specify a model-dependent KV cache budget and evaluate
all compressed methods under the same KV cache memory budget, with budgets
reported in Table  1 .
We compare OTT  Su et al. (2025) , a representative token-wise
preservation method, PM-KVQ  Liu et al. (2026) , a representative layer-wise
mixed-precision method, KVmix  Li et al. (2026) , an importance-guided hybrid
KV cache quantization method, and our profiling-guided precision allocation
framework MultiAlloc .
All methods are implemented within the same inference framework and follow a
unified KV cache memory-accounting protocol.

 MultiAlloc are evaluated under the same memory budget, while FP16

 MultiAlloc achieves the highest overall accuracy among compressed

 6 Conclusion

 
 In this work, we formulate KV cache quantization under a fixed KV cache memory
budget as a precision allocation problem, where limited quantization resources
are adaptively distributed between layer-wise precision enhancement and
token-wise preservation.
We propose MultiAlloc , a profiling-guided framework that characterizes
heterogeneous precision demands across layers and tokens, and derives a static
precision policy through budget-coupled allocation.
Extensive experiments on reasoning and long-context benchmarks demonstrate that
 MultiAlloc consistently outperforms representative layer-wise,
token-wise, and importance-based KV cache quantization methods under matched
KV cache memory budgets.

 
 7 Limitations

 
 This work focuses on profiling-guided precision allocation for KV cache
quantization under fixed memory budgets. While MultiAlloc 
eliminates online allocation search through offline policy derivation,
the profiling stage introduces preprocessing overhead and requires
representative calibration data.
Future work will explore lightweight profiling and improved policy
generalization.

 Appendix A Experimental Setup and Calibration Details

 
 This appendix provides additional details on the evaluated
models and benchmarks, the evaluation protocol, and the
task-specific calibration procedure used by MultiAlloc.
Unless otherwise specified, all compressed methods follow the
same evaluation settings as the FP16 baseline.

 
 A.1 Models and Benchmarks

 
 Models.

 
 We evaluate MultiAlloc on five instruction-tuned language
models covering different model families and parameter scales:
Llama-3-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3,
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B,
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B, and QwQ-32B.
These models range from 7B to 32B parameters and include both
general-purpose instruction-tuned models and reasoning-oriented
models.

 
 GPQA.

 
 GPQA is a multiple-choice benchmark designed to evaluate
expert-level scientific reasoning. We use the GPQA-Diamond
subset, which contains 198 four-option questions.
Accuracy is reported over the evaluated questions.

 
 GSM8K.

 
 GSM8K evaluates multi-step mathematical reasoning using
grade-school word problems. The dataset contains 7,473
training examples and 1,319 test examples.
We report accuracy on the test set based on the extracted
final numerical answer.

 
 LongBench-v2.

 
 LongBench-v2 evaluates long-context understanding and
reasoning in realistic scenarios. It contains 503
multiple-choice questions spanning six task categories:
single-document question answering, multi-document question
answering, long in-context learning, code repository
understanding, long dialogue history understanding, and
long structured-data understanding.
Following the main paper, we report the unweighted
macro-averaged accuracy across the six task categories.

 
 A.2 Evaluation Protocol

 
 For each model, the FP16 baseline and all compressed methods
use identical evaluation examples, prompt templates,
tokenization, stopping criteria, answer-scoring procedures,
and greedy decoding. Therefore, the compared methods differ
only in their KV-cache compression strategies while following
the same benchmark-specific evaluation protocol.

 
 GPQA.

 
 We evaluate GPQA-Diamond on all 198 questions using the
zero-shot direct-choice protocol with option permutation,
following lm-evaluation-harness . We use a fixed
random seed of 0 and greedy decoding, with a maximum
generation length of 1,024 tokens. The generated response is
mapped to the corresponding multiple-choice option for
accuracy evaluation.

 
 GSM8K.

 
 We evaluate all 1,319 examples from the GSM8K test set.
The final numerical answer is extracted from the generated
response and evaluated using exact match. Generation is
performed with greedy decoding and is capped at 1,024 tokens.
FP16 and all compressed methods use the same generation and
answer-extraction procedure.

 
 LongBench-v2.

 
 We evaluate all 503 examples in LongBench-v2 and report the
unweighted macro-average accuracy over its six task
categories. All methods use greedy decoding. For
Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, the maximum
generation length is 128 tokens. For
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B,
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-14B, and QwQ-32B, the maximum
generation length is 64 tokens, with explicit thinking
disabled. QwQ-32B additionally uses constrained-choice
decoding. Invalid, truncated, or failed generations are
counted as incorrect.

 
 A.3 Task-Specific Calibration Protocol

 
 MultiAlloc uses a task-specific calibration set for each
evaluated benchmark. In other words, the profiling statistics
used for GSM8K, GPQA, and LongBench-v2 are collected from
separate calibration sets corresponding to the target
workload. For a given model and task, the calibration data are
used to estimate the layer-wise precision demand and
token-wise preservation demand required by the offline
allocator. The resulting allocation policy is then fixed and
applied throughout inference for that workload.

 
 Why task-specific calibration?

 
 MultiAlloc adopts workload-aware offline profiling because the
precision demand of KV cache quantization is inherently dependent on
the workload characteristics. Rather than deriving a single universal
allocation policy, MultiAlloc estimates the precision requirements
under the target inference workload, where different tasks may induce
distinct activation distributions and token importance patterns. These
variations affect both the relative quantization sensitivity of
Transformer layers and the benefit of preserving selected tokens in
higher precision.

 
 This design is analogous to the calibration process commonly used in
post-training quantization, where representative samples are used to
characterize model behavior before deployment. The calibration data
are only used to collect offline profiling statistics, including
layer-wise quantization sensitivity and token-level preservation
demand, rather than to optimize task-specific model parameters or
access inference labels. As shown in the allocation analysis in the
main paper, different workloads produce a stable layer-precision
backbone with task-dependent variations in both layer-wise precision
assignment and token-wise high-precision preservation.

 
 Therefore, workload-specific calibration enables MultiAlloc to
allocate the limited KV cache budget according to the precision demands
of the target deployment scenario instead of relying on a manually
designed fixed allocation strategy. Importantly, all profiling and
allocation decisions are completed offline. Once the precision policy
is generated, inference directly applies the static policy without
online profiling, search, or dynamic reallocation.

 
 A.4 Implementation Details

 
 All experiments are conducted on NVIDIA A100 GPUs with
80 GB of memory. Our implementation uses Python 3.10,
PyTorch 2.5.1, Transformers 4.49.0, and
Datasets 3.3.2. All methods use the same model checkpoints
and software environment, with a batch size of one.

 
 For each benchmark, FP16 and all compressed methods share
identical preprocessing, prompting, tokenization, decoding,
and stopping criteria. The KV-cache budgets used for the main
comparison are reported in Table 1 of the main paper.

 
 For MultiAlloc, profiling and budget-aware allocation are
performed offline. Each model–dataset pair is profiled for
the corresponding target KV-cache budget, and the resulting
allocation policy is saved and directly loaded during
inference. Consequently, online inference involves no
profiling, candidate-policy enumeration, dynamic programming,
budget search, or adaptive reallocation.

 
 Runtime measurements exclude the one-time offline profiling
cost and are averaged over five runs after warm-up. For
reproducibility, each run records the complete experimental
configuration, software environment, GPU information, and
source-code fingerprint. Model and dataset versions, memory
budgets, decoding settings, and evaluation protocols are
specified in the corresponding experiment configuration
files.

 
 Appendix B Profiling-based Precision Demand Estimation

 
 Directly evaluating the downstream performance of every
candidate precision configuration would require repeated
end-to-end inference and is prohibitively expensive.
Instead, MultiAlloc constructs lightweight profiling
surrogates to characterize the relative precision demand at
different granularities. These surrogates are not intended to
predict the absolute downstream accuracy of a compressed
model. Rather, they provide relative preference signals among
candidate precision-preservation choices during offline
allocation.

 
 Specifically, we separately estimate (i) the sensitivity of
different Transformer layers to KV cache quantization under
different bit-widths and (ii) the benefit of selectively
preserving important tokens in higher precision.

 B.3 Complementary Precision Demands

 
 The layer-wise and token-wise profiling stages characterize different
dimensions of precision heterogeneity in KV cache quantization. Although
both signals reflect the impact of quantization, they capture
complementary precision demands at different granularities.

 
 The layer-wise precision demand describes the quantization sensitivity of
each Transformer layer under different bit-width configurations. It
determines where increasing the base precision of the KV cache can
provide larger benefits. In contrast, the token-wise preservation demand
focuses on the additional benefit of retaining selected tokens in higher
precision within each layer. It identifies where extra memory allocation
can better preserve important token representations.

 
 These two precision demands correspond to different allocation choices.
Layer-wise precision enhancement provides broad protection by improving
the base representation quality of sensitive layers, while token-wise
preservation provides selective protection for a sparse subset of tokens
that remain vulnerable under the assigned layer precision.

 
 Under a fixed KV cache memory budget, these two strategies compete for
the same limited quantization resources. Allocating more memory to
increase layer precision reduces the available budget for token
preservation, while preserving more high-precision tokens limits the
memory available for layer-wise enhancement. Therefore, optimizing either
precision dimension independently cannot achieve the best allocation
under a constrained memory budget.

 
 The two profiling signals are consequently combined in the subsequent
budget-coupled allocation stage, where MultiAlloc determines how the
available KV cache budget should be distributed between layer-wise
precision enhancement and token-wise preservation.

 
 Appendix C Budget-Coupled Allocation Optimization

 
 C.1 Memory Accounting under Fixed KV Cache Budget

 
 MultiAlloc formulates KV cache quantization as a precision allocation
problem under a fixed memory constraint. Unlike approaches that optimize
layer-wise precision assignment or token preservation separately, the
two precision preservation strategies share the same KV cache budget and
must be considered within a unified allocation framework.

 
 Given a total KV cache budget B B , a precision allocation policy is
defined by a layer-wise bit-width configuration

 
 

 
 
 q = { q l } l = 1 L , q=\{q_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}, 
 
 (30) 
 
 

 
 and a token preservation configuration

 
 

 
 
 k = { k l } l = 1 L , k=\{k_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}, 
 
 (31) 
 
 

 
 where q l q_{l} denotes the base KV precision assigned to layer l l , and
 k l k_{l} represents the number of high-precision tokens preserved within
each token group of layer l l .

 
 The allocation objective is formulated as minimizing the quantization
degradation under the memory constraint:

 
 

 
 
 min q , k ⁡ ℒ ⁡ ( q , k ) s.t. M ⁡ ( q , k ) ≤ B . \min_{q,k}\mathcal{L}(q,k)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad M(q,k)\leq B. 
 
 (32) 
 
 

 
 The memory consumption of a policy is decomposed into two components:

 (33) 
 
 

 
 where M layer ​ ( q ) M_{\mathrm{layer}}(q) denotes the memory consumption of the
layer-wise quantized KV cache, and M token ​ ( k ) M_{\mathrm{token}}(k) represents
the additional memory required for preserving selected tokens in higher
precision.

 
 For a given token preservation configuration k k , the remaining budget
available for layer-wise precision allocation is:

 
 

 
 
 B layer = B − M token ​ ( k ) . B_{\mathrm{layer}}=B-M_{\mathrm{token}}(k). 
 
 (34) 
 
 

 
 Therefore, increasing token preservation capacity inevitably reduces
the memory available for layer-wise precision enhancement, while
allocating higher layer precision limits the budget for additional token
preservation. This budget coupling motivates the subsequent allocation
procedure, which determines how the limited KV cache memory should be
distributed between the two precision dimensions.

 D.3 Static Inference Deployment

 
 Once the allocation policy π ∗ \pi^{*} is obtained, it is directly applied
during inference. For each Transformer layer l l , the incoming KV states
are quantized using the assigned bit-width q l ∗ q_{l}^{*} . Meanwhile, the token
preservation configuration k ∗ k^{*} determines the number of high-precision
tokens retained within each token group.

 
 Although the identities of preserved tokens depend on the input
sequence, the selection rule itself is fixed by the offline policy.
Specifically, tokens are ranked using the predefined importance metric
and preserved according to the assigned top- k k capacity.

 
 During inference, the execution only involves deterministic KV
quantization, token scoring, high-precision token preservation, and
cache maintenance. No profiling, gradient computation, candidate policy
enumeration, budget optimization, or dynamic precision reallocation is
performed online.

 
 By separating allocation decisions from latency-critical inference,
MultiAlloc applies a fixed precision policy during deployment and avoids
any additional allocation search under the target KV cache memory
constraint.

constructed offline and remain small in practice. Therefore, the
allocation overhead is incurred only once for each target model,
calibration configuration, and KV cache budget.

 
 F.2 Inference-Time Complexity

 
 After offline optimization, inference directly applies the precomputed
static policy

 During decoding, each Transformer layer quantizes the incoming KV states
using the assigned bit-width q l ∗ q_{l}^{*} , while token preservation follows
the fixed capacity configuration specified by k ∗ k^{*} .

 
 The online execution only performs deterministic cache operations,
including KV cache quantization, token scoring, high-precision token
preservation according to the fixed policy, and cache maintenance. No
profiling, candidate enumeration, dynamic programming, budget search, or
precision reallocation is performed during inference.

 
 When the token group size and preservation capacity are bounded, the
additional computation introduced by the allocation policy is
 O ⁡ ( L ​ D K ​ V ) O(LD_{KV}) per generated token. This cost is independent of the size of
the offline candidate space and the target KV cache budget.

 
 Therefore, MultiAlloc separates the allocation overhead from
latency-critical inference by resolving all precision decisions offline,
enabling efficient deployment under fixed KV cache memory constraints.
