 QuantiBias: Benchmarking Quantization-Induced Bias in LLMs

 
 
 Emilio Ferrara
 
 Affiliation:  Thomas Lord Department of Computer Science
 
 Affiliation:  University of Southern California
 
 Email:  emiliofe@usc.edu 
 

 Abstract

 
 Almost every large language model that reaches a broad audience is quantized: trained in full
precision, then compressed for efficiency. This step is assumed harmless and its safety is
rarely re-checked. We find its principal side effect is increased bias that standard safety
evaluation misses. Holding the model, its training, and the prompts fixed, a quantized model still
refuses harmful requests, still avoids over-refusing benign prompts, and still selects the unbiased
multiple-choice answer. Yet asked an open-ended question, the same model volunteers stereotypes in
all eight languages we probe, in roughly one in four open-ended answers under an independent judge
(~24% to ~27% across the compression ladder): it passes every standard
check and still reaches users measurably more biased. The selective gap is a robust finding; whether open-ended bias further increases with
compression is less certain, sensitive to the judge that scores it. We address both with
 QuantiBias , a benchmark that pairs a generative, multilingual stereotype probe with the
refusal and multiple-choice controls that isolate open-ended generation, contrasts each build with
and without reasoning, and rates the content severity of what it generates. Across two backbone
models (Qwen and Gemma), a five-family screen, and eight benchmarks, quantizers allocate their extra
precision by capability data that carries no bias-prevention signal, and reasoning before answering
roughly halves the effect on some families while doing nothing on others. A quantized build must be
re-evaluated for open-ended bias, not only on the short-form safeguards it already passes. Code and
baselines: https://huggingface.co/datasets/emilioferrara/quantibias .

 

 
 

 
 1 Introduction

 
 A language model is evaluated at full precision and typically deployed compressed. Post-training
quantization shrinks the weights before release, often by a factor of 3 to 14, so the
build a user interacts with is usually not the one the evaluation measured. Quantization is
expected to preserve behavior, and capability benchmarks largely bear this out; the safety and
bias side has been probed less systematically. Reports on aggressively quantized builds tend to
document capability retention across benchmarks while saying less about safety or
bias.

 
 Figure 1: The selective safety gap under compression. Quantization compresses an aligned model
from 16 to about one bit per weight. The short-form checks a release is screened on, refusing
harmful requests and avoiding biased multiple-choice answers, still pass. Yet in open-ended
generation the same model volunteers stereotypes the checklist never registers, a gap at every
precision. QuantiBias measures it: a generative stereotype probe scored against effective bits per
weight, with and without reasoning, and rated for severity. 
 
 
 Two prior results suggest this gap is consequential. The butterfly-effect account of AI bias
 ( Ferrara, 2024 ) shows that small perturbations to a model or its training pipeline
can produce disproportionate and hard-to-predict changes in bias. Quantization is an
unusually clean perturbation of this kind: it alters no training data, no objective, and no
prompt, only the numerical precision of the weights. Separately, a multi-dimensional audit of
targeted bias-reduction methods ( Chand et al., 2026 ) found that reducing bias on one
dimension often shifted it on dimensions the method did not target, with smaller models
affected more, which the authors attribute to entangled representations in which no adjustment
stays local. Together these results predict that quantization moves behavior on several axes at
once: it perturbs every weight simultaneously, is directed at no particular behavior, and, if
representations are entangled, does not confine its effects to the capabilities it preserves.

 
 Prior studies of quantization and safety often disagree, and much of the disagreement follows
from what the standard safety metric examines. Safety is usually summarized as the rate at which a
short harmful prompt succeeds. That measure averages over the brief exchanges in which quantization
does the least damage, and never examines the sustained, open-ended generation in which it does the
most; separating the two shows the short-form safeguards holding while open-ended generation carries
bias the checks never sample. Two measurement details compound the confusion, and we control both: a
build’s nominal label understates how much it is quantized, so grouping builds by label compares
models compressed to different degrees as though they were the same; and the precision at which a
model runs its matrix multiplications depends on the serving kernel as well as the stored weights, so
two byte-identical checkpoints can compute at different precision. We therefore index every result to
effective bits per weight measured from the artifact (Section  3 ).

 
 This paper makes three contributions. First, we document selective blindness: across two anchor
backbones, a five-family screen, and eight benchmarks, quantization leaves the safeguards standard
evaluations measure, refusal and multiple-choice bias avoidance, unchanged, while the bias a model
volunteers in open-ended generation goes unmeasured by them. Second, we give the mechanism
(Section  7 ): quantizers allocate precision to calibration data that carries no bias
signal, leaving the bias-relevant weights fragile, and we show when reasoning before answering
suppresses the effect and when it does not: reasoning halves the open-ended bias on Qwen but leaves it unchanged on Gemma. Third, we build the
instrument that detects the gap. We introduce QuantiBias , a benchmark that pairs a
generative, multilingual stereotype probe with the refusal and multiple-choice controls that
establish the effect is specific to open-ended generation, contrasts each build with and without
reasoning, and rates the content severity of what a model generates (Section  4 ).

 
 2 Related Work

 
 Quantization is known to change model behavior beyond the capability benchmarks used to validate
it, and its effect on safety and bias has been studied unevenly. Xu et al. (2024) evaluate
pruning and quantization along degeneration harm, representational harm, and dialect bias, and show
that generation-side safety can erode while task accuracy holds. Closest to our setting,
 Marcuzzi et al. (2026) extend this across 13 benchmarks and report that aggressive
quantization slightly raises stereotyping and bias in generative tasks while lowering toxicity
and leaving sentiment flat. Rath and Maliakkal (2026) find that three-bit quantization turns 6 to
21% of previously unbiased BBQ items stereotypical, and Hua et al. (2026) 
show that such flips often cancel in aggregate, masking large per-group swings. On the safety side,
 Kharinaev et al. (2025) document alignment degradation across 66 quantized
variants, Egashira et al. (2024) show that quantization can be weaponized adversarially so
the artifact a user evaluates is not the one they deploy, and Al Hakim et al. (2026) protect
bias-critical weights and find the damage worst in non-English settings.

 
 We build on these findings and depart from them in three ways. First, we isolate a selectivity effect
that aggregate reporting obscures: the short-form safeguards these evaluations rely on, refusal and
multiple-choice bias avoidance, stay flat while the bias a model volunteers in open-ended generation
rises ( Jin et al., 2025 ) , so a build passes the checks and reaches users more biased. Second, we test whether reasoning
acts as a safeguard and find that it does not hold universally: on the Gemma backbone, unlike Qwen, reasoning does not reduce the bias.
Third, we add a content-severity axis that rates the harmfulness of what a model actually generates,
which no prior compression study measures. Throughout, we index results to effective bits per weight
measured from the artifact rather than nominal quantizer labels, which vary in what they deliver
(Section  3 ). Where critical-weight protection changes how a model is quantized,
QuantiBias changes how it is evaluated.

 
 3 Measuring True Quantization

 
 A build’s name states a nominal bits-per-weight, but conventional
low-bit builds retain some components, the embeddings, attention, and output head, at
substantially higher precision, and the size of that hidden margin varies from build to build
and vendor to vendor. We therefore measure every model directly, reading the size and
quantization type of each stored weight tensor and dividing total weight bytes by parameter
count to obtain the effective bits per weight (bpw). Table  1 gives the two anchor
ladders; nominal labels overstate the degree of quantization by 11 to 40%
(Figure  A3 ).

 Two consequences follow. First, from here on we plot every result against measured bits per
weight, and the label serves only to identify the artifact. Second, weight precision does not
fully specify the treatment: the checkpoint does not determine whether the model’s matrix
multiplications run in 16-bit or 4-bit, so we record the resolved backend for every run and
treat the weight measurement as a partial description. The label understates quantization
because the high-precision components are hidden behind it. In the “2-bit” UD-IQ2_XXS build
the multilayer-perceptron weights sit at 2.14 bits while the output head and attention tensors
carry substantially more (4.53 and 3.55 bits), and the build mixes nine distinct quantization
types; normalization tensors stay at full 32-bit precision. This mixing is inherent to the
llama.cpp k-quant schemes these builds use ( Gerganov and others, 2023 ) , which, like activation-aware and
error-compensating post-training quantizers ( Frantar et al., 2023 ; Lin et al., 2024 ; Xiao et al., 2023 ) , allocate more
bits to weights they judge sensitive; that allocation is tuned on capability data and has no reason
to track bias. The end-to-end ternary and one-bit builds, following the trend toward natively
low-bit models ( Ma et al., 2024 ) , are the exception, holding perceptron, attention, and output head
at a single flat precision, which is why their measured value tracks their label far more closely.

 
 4 QuantiBias

 
 QuantiBias packages the open-ended bias measurement into a reusable protocol with
 four components .

 
 1. 
 
 A generative, paired, multilingual probe. Each item is an open-ended request
that gives a model room to volunteer or decline a stereotype, paired with a
stereotype-endorsing claim and its group-swapped counterpart that fix the axis a judge scores
(Section  6 ). Open generation surfaces bias that multiple-choice instruments
miss ( Jin et al., 2025 ; Liu et al., 2024 ) . A parallel-translated variant holds content fixed while varying only the language.
Multilingual bias suites exist, but each drops one of these axes: MBBQ is multiple-choice
 ( Neplenbroek et al., 2024 ) , the Multilingual Holistic Bias set scores template sentences rather
than free generation ( Costa-jussà et al., 2023 ) , and SHADES supplies parallel stereotypes without
a compression axis ( Mitchell et al., 2025 ) . Our probe is, to our knowledge, the only one that
is open-ended, parallel across eight languages, and rated for the harmfulness of what a model
volunteers.

 2. 
 
 Scoring against measured effective bpw. QuantiBias reports every result against
the measured precision of a build (Section  3 ), never its nominal label, so two
builds sharing a label but differing in real precision are not conflated.

 3. 
 
 A reasoning-load contrast. Each build is evaluated with deliberation enabled
and disabled, because the effect is concentrated on the non-deliberative path and its
remediability is family-dependent; a single mode hides both.

 4. 
 
 Controls that show the effect is selective. QuantiBias pairs the generative
probe with refusal, over-refusal, and multiple-choice bias measures. Their flatness separates
selective bias amplification from a general behavioral change.

 
 A build passes QuantiBias when its generative-bias rate does not rise across its own precision
ladder while the controls stay flat, and fails when generative bias rises even though the
controls do not, the signature this paper documents. We release the probe, measurement
code, and scoring harness. 1 1

 5 Experiments

 
 Instrumentation.

 
 Each evaluation cell is one (artifact, benchmark, arm, prompt-pool,
decoding) tuple, executed by a checkpointed, idempotent harness that writes verbatim generations
to an append-only store; every reported number traces to a job identifier and a content hash.
Decoding is held constant across precisions (temperature 0.7, top- p p 0.95, fixed token
budgets, the model’s own chat template). We score the full-ladder generative probe with an in-family Qwen3-8B lean judge, which is fast
enough to run at scale, and validate that scoring with two independent judges drawn from different
vendors and from families outside the evaluation set: Claude Sonnet-5 (Anthropic) and
Gemini-2.5-pro (Google), both fixed frontier models never themselves quantized, so the quantization
under test cannot affect the instrument that measures them. The lean judge’s validated quantity is
the binary neutral-versus-endorsing distinction, which it recovers at about 94% against
MBTP’s own labeled reference responses; its absolute endorsement level is judge-family-dependent and
far below what the independent judges assign. 2 2 
 2 
 
 
 
 “In-family” means the judge, Qwen3-8B, is from the same model family as the anchor under
test, so it cannot independently measure the bias level ( Wataoka et al., 2024 ) ; the two out-of-family judges (Claude
Sonnet-5 and Gemini-2.5-pro) supply that. On the open generations the in-family judge scores well
below them, so its rates understate the level, and the selective gap rests on the independent judges. What we claim is the
selective gap: the short-form safeguards stay flat while open-ended generation carries a high
stereotype rate under every judge we ran. The compression slope of that open-ended rate is a
within-judge comparison and is not established: re-scoring the reasoning-off ladder with an
independent judge returns a high but roughly flat profile (0.238, 0.267, 0.266 from Q4 to one bit),
against the rising 0.042, 0.053, 0.078 the lean judge reports, so we treat the dose-response as
provisional (Limitations). On a controlled parallel probe (two middle rungs, n = 160 n=160 ) the two
independent judges agree on 85% of items (Krippendorff’s α = 0.69 \alpha=0.69 ), and a full-ladder
independent-ensemble re-scoring is the reference measurement, whose levels supersede the lean
judge’s.

 
 Benchmarks.

 
 The first group tests explicit safeguards: refusal of directly harmful
requests and their jailbreak-wrapped versions (StrongREJECT ( Souly et al., 2024 ) ),
over-refusal of benign-but-alarming prompts (XSTest ( Röttger et al., 2024 ) ), and
multiple-choice bias avoidance under ambiguity (BBQ ( Parrish et al., 2022 ) ). The second measures
implicit behavior with a multilingual generative stereotype probe, MBTP ( Kaneko et al., 2026 ) ,
spanning 8 languages (Arabic, Chinese, English, French, German, Japanese, Russian, Spanish) and
13 social categories (Table  A1 ). Each of its items pairs an open-ended request with a matched pair
of short claims, one stereotype-endorsing and one its group-swapped counterpart, that fix the
axis the judge scores. The probe items are due to ( Kaneko et al., 2026 ) ; QuantiBias contributes the
frozen 320-item balanced subset, the measurement of results against effective bpw, the
reasoning-load contrast, and the independent-judge scoring harness. An independent generative benchmark (CEB ( Wang et al., 2024 ) ) provides a
second instrument over disjoint items; a next-token benchmark (StereoSet
 ( Nadeem et al., 2021 ) ) serves as a short-form negative control; and 2 capability anchors
(MMLU-Redux ( Gema et al., 2025 ) , GSM8K ( Cobbe et al., 2021 ) )
separate safety-specific degradation from general capability loss.

 
 Multilingual construction.

 
 MBTP is built parallel across the eight languages by
machine translation with back-translation quality control. Forty English source items are
canonical; each is rendered into the other seven languages by a frontier model (Gemini 2.5 Pro),
holding the content fixed so that only the language of presentation varies. Every translated
field is independently back-translated (Gemini 2.5 Flash) and scored against its source for gross
drift, with low-agreement rows flagged rather than dropped and reserved for native-speaker
validation. This yields the 320-item parallel probe that isolates the
language of presentation from the national target and the content, the confound the original
diagonal design carries (cf. Limitations).

 
 6 Results

 
 We establish the effect and its structure on the Qwen3.6-27B anchor, on a second backbone, and
across a five-family screen (build details in Table  A2 ). Every rate carries a Wilson 95% interval.

 
 The safety checks hold flat.

 
 Table  A3 reports the safeguard, capability, and MBTP
benchmarks across the full Qwen3.6-27B ladder, from 16 to 1.13 measured bpw. The safeguards standard
evaluations probe do not move: refusal of directly harmful requests stays at 0.98, over-refusal
on XSTest near 0.33, and BBQ accuracy under ambiguity near 0.97, at every quantized rung. The capability
anchors hold through IQ2 and lose only a little at the one-bit extreme (MMLU-Redux 0.952 to
0.877), which separates safety-specific degradation from general capability loss: from Q4 to IQ2
capability is flat while generative bias already rises. A short-form next-token bias control
(StereoSet stereotype score, 50 ideal) is flat as well, 58 to 60 across the ladder. A study measuring
only these outcomes would conclude that quantization is safety-neutral. The stable multiple-choice
scores may also be flattered by contamination: if BBQ-style items sit in the training data, a model
can recognize the evaluation format and answer it well while its open-ended behavior diverges ( Balloccu et al., 2024 ) , a
defeat device in the sense of Ferrara (2026) .

 
 Figure 2: The selective safety gap, measured. Safeguard, capability, and MBTP benchmarks across the Qwen3.6-27B ladder
(more compression rightward). The upper panel holds: refusal, multiple-choice bias avoidance
(BBQ), and capability (GSM8K, MMLU-Redux, declining only at one bit) stay near full precision, so a
standard audit reports it unchanged. The lower panel is what it misses: scoring the same reasoning-off
generations two ways, the in-family lean judge reports low endorsement rising modestly with compression,
while an independent judge (Claude Sonnet-5) flags a stereotype in nearly one open-ended answer in four,
roughly flat and far above the lean judge. 
 

 
 Open-ended bias is high where the safety checks stay flat.

 
 The safeguards hold; open-ended
generation does not. On the Qwen3.6-27B anchor the model volunteers a stereotype in a substantial
fraction of open-ended answers while its refusal, over-refusal, and multiple-choice bias controls sit
at their near-ideal values (Table  A3 , Figure  2 ). Under an independent judge that fraction is
0.238, 0.267, and 0.266 across the reasoning-off ladder (per language in Figure  A1 ),
close to one open-ended answer in four, while
the same model’s multiple-choice bias control stays near 0.97 and its refusal near 0.98
(Limitations), so a build passes every standard check and still reaches users markedly more biased. This selective gap is the robust finding: it is present at every
precision and under every judge we ran. Whether the open-ended rate also rises with compression
is a second, separable question, and the answer is not universal. Under the in-family lean judge the
rate rises at every step on this anchor and every language ends higher at one bit than at 4-bit
(Table  A3 , Figure  A5 ); under the independent judge the same cells are higher and roughly flat. Across the five-family screen the per-family compression slope is not resolved at that
sample size: under both judges every Q8-to-Q2 change lies within its 95% interval
(Figure  6 ). We therefore document a selective gap that is robust across judges and
families, and a compression-driven increase that appears on the anchor under the lean judge but
flattens under the independent one, which we report as provisional. The eight most severe one-bit instances appear in
Table  2 , one endorsing sentence each; the high-precision build declines each
stereotype while the one-bit build asserts it. Appendix  D gives the full cards.

 Content severity of the endorsements.

 
 Whether a model endorses a stereotype and how
harmful the endorsement is are separate questions, and a binary lean answers only the first. We score
every one-bit instance the lean judge marked as an endorsement on a 0–4 content-severity rubric
(0, no biased content; 4, a biased generalization asserted as fact) with a judge independent of the
evaluation set. Of the 48 instances, mean severity is 1.83 and the harm is real but bounded: 33 score
2 or higher (a stated group generalization) while none reach the severity-4 ceiling, so the failure
mode is a confident stereotype rather than the most extreme assertion. The rubric also exposes the
limits of the binary lean: about a fifth of the instances (10 of 48) score 0, complying with a loaded
prompt without asserting the stereotype, a distinction the binary lean cannot make. Table  2 shows the eight most severe instances verbatim and
Figure  3 the full distribution; severity is measured at the one-bit rung here, and
its slope against compression is a prediction of Section  7 that a higher-powered study
could test.

 
 The reasoning contrast, and its limit.

 
 Turning reasoning on roughly halves the rate
at every rung and flattens the compression slope from + 3.6 +3.6 to + 0.7 +0.7 points
(Table  A3 , Figure  A4 ): compression damages the fast, no-deliberation path. This remedy is not
universal ( Wu et al., 2025 ) . On the Gemma-4-31B backbone the compression effect replicates (MBTP reasoning off,
0.038 at Q4 rising to 0.069 at IQ2, n = 480 n{=}480 ), but reasoning barely moves it: the two modes are
identical at Q4 (18 of 480 either way) and close at IQ2 (0.069 off, 0.064 on), against a halving on
Qwen (Figure  4 ). The manipulation did occur, verified by the multi-thousand-character reasoning traces the
reasoning-on cells carry. Deliberation
is thus a family-dependent safeguard, not a universal one: it helps only where the added reasoning
actually revisits the stereotype decision, a mechanism we develop in Section  7 .

 Widening to five families.

 
 Screening five model families
(Figure  6 ) resolves the level of open-ended bias but not its
per-family compression slope. We hold Qwen3-8B out of the screen because it is the lean judge, which
would make the judge and the subject the same model; measured by the independent judge, which has no
such conflict, it sits within the retained families’ level band with a slope within noise, so it is
excluded for cleanliness, not because it stands out. Baselines differ about twofold across families, with the 8B class
above the 27B anchor, consistent with the size fragility reported by Chand et al. (2026) .
The level is also strongly judge-dependent: an independent re-scoring (Claude Sonnet-5) puts every
family at 20 to 30%, three to four times the in-family lean judge, echoing the anchor gap. The
per-family slope, by contrast, is within noise at this sample size, under both judges (every
Q8-to-Q2 change lies inside its interval), so the screen names which families differ in level
without settling whether any one family’s bias rises with compression, which a higher-powered study
would resolve.

 
 7 Why the Effect Is Selective

 
 The pattern documented above, refusal intact while open-ended bias worsens as precision falls, is
not specific to these models. It follows from how quantization perturbs a network and how
generation reads out its state. The account is mechanistic, not a proof; it is consistent with the measurements above.

 
 A bounded, semantically empty perturbation.

 
 Let f W f_{W} denote the model with weights

bit width. This is why we plot against measured bpw.

 Each token is selected by an argmax or top- p p draw over
the logits, so continuous drift in the hidden state produces no visible change until it crosses
the boundary between one token and the next. Behaviors decided by a wide margin appear
unaffected; behaviors decided by a narrow margin flip first. Let m m be the logit margin
favoring the non-endorsing continuation, so the clean model declines when m > 0 m>0 . Linearizing

 What follows.

 
 Under a margin distribution skewed toward declining, three properties
hold, each of which the results above bear out. (i) The rate is non-decreasing as precision falls,
 d ​ R / d ​ σ ≥ 0 dR/d\sigma\geq 0 . (ii) A behavior whose margins all exceed some m min ≫ σ m_{\min}\gg\sigma has

selectivity is a property of where a behavior’s margins lie, not of the size of the
perturbation, which is common to all behaviors. (iii) At the high-precision end the excess over
the clean rate grows as R ⁡ ( b ) − R 0 ∝ 2 − 2 ​ b R(b)-R_{0}\propto 2^{-2b} , matching the dose (Figure  A6 ). Refusing an overtly

a stereotype in open-ended generation was never a direct training target; it sits behind a
narrow margin and degrades ( Tran et al., 2022 ; Dam et al., 2023 ) . Current quantizers can only widen this asymmetry, since they score
each layer on coding and chat data that carries no bias signal and so have no reason to
protect the fragile weights. Reasoning helps because each committed token is written to the
transcript and conditioned on thereafter ( Guan et al., 2024 ) , letting later steps correct an error the drift
introduced; it fails when the deliberation never revisits the stereotype decision, which nothing
guarantees.

 
 Frequency and severity are separate axes.

 
 The rate R ⁡ ( b ) R(b) counts how often the model
crosses into endorsement, not how far past the boundary it goes. Per-generation harm is the product
of this frequency and the mean severity s ¯ ​ ( b ) \bar{s}(b) of an endorsement, H ⁡ ( b ) = f ⁡ ( b ) ​ s ¯ ​ ( b ) H(b)=f(b)\,\bar{s}(b) , and
our results estimate only the first factor. The same margin model predicts the second also rises with
compression: conditioned on a crossing, the expected depth grows with the noise scale σ ⁡ ( b ) \sigma(b) , so
a model that endorses more often is predicted to endorse more decisively, with total harm
non-decreasing and its sensitivity concentrated on the near-boundary behaviors alignment never
reinforced (Appendix  C ). This severity channel is a prediction, not a result: our
current instrument scores frequency alone. Measuring s ¯ ​ ( b ) \bar{s}(b) calls for a graded severity rubric
rather than the binary lean, in the spirit of continuous stereotype-strength measures
 ( Liu, 2024 ; Sheng et al., 2019 ) ; no standard toxicity classifier captures a politely worded
stereotype endorsement ( Hartvigsen et al., 2022 ) , so the rubric is scored by an independent judge
 ( Souly et al., 2024 ) and controlled for generation coherence ( Xu et al., 2024 ) . QuantiBias
reserves this as its severity extension.

 
 8 Conclusions

 
 Quantization leaves a model’s short-form safety behavior almost untouched while its open-ended
generation grows markedly more biased. Across the Qwen3.6-27B ladder the refusal, over-refusal, and
multiple-choice bias controls hold near their full-precision values at every rung, yet an independent
judge flags a stereotype in close to one open-ended answer in four. This selective gap is robust
across judges and families; whether the open-ended rate also rises with compression is a
separate question we leave open. A build that passes every safety check can still reach users markedly more biased; a quantized
model needs re-evaluation on open-ended generation, not short-form scores alone.

 

 
 Limitations

 
 The effect is clear; the ways it could be sharpened are specific and named, in order of how much
they matter. (1) The full-sample bias results rest on a single in-family judge; on the
controlled parallel probe we ran a three-family panel (Section  5 ), and the two
independent judges agree closely ( α = 0.69 \alpha=0.69 ) while the in-family judge is uncorrelated with
both, which shows absolute bias levels are judge-family-dependent, consistent with known LLM-as-judge
pitfalls of position and self-preference bias ( Zheng et al., 2023 ; Panickssery et al., 2024 ; Wataoka et al., 2024 ) . Label
instability of this kind is not confined to model judges: human harm annotations are themselves inconsistent
enough that filtering high-variance annotators flips majority harm classifications for 18.6 % 18.6\% of prompts
 ( Ghafouri et al., 2026 ) . The full-sample diagonal cells must therefore be re-scored with the independent
ensemble before any single level is relied on. That pilot spans two middle rungs at 80 items each and does not yet reach the one-bit
rung where the diagonal slope concentrates, so it does not by itself resolve the slope on the
controlled instrument, which a full-ladder parallel run would settle. (2) The
full-sample evidence rests on one backbone; the second-backbone replication and the independent
CEB instrument point the same way but are underpowered. (3) Power analysis against the observed
effect size (Cohen’s h ≈ 0.15 h\approx 0.15 ) indicates several hundred to above one thousand paired
prompts per cell are required to resolve the per-family slope; the prompt pools are being expanded
accordingly. (4) Activation precision is
held constant here and is a second treatment dimension this stage does not vary; it requires
hardware unavailable on the current cluster. (5) The screening probe is sensitive to the fast
path by design (reasoning off); families whose degradation lives elsewhere would be missed. (6)
The multilingual probe is a diagonal design in which the language of presentation ( Yong et al., 2023 ) , national
target, and content are entangled, so any between-language comparison is confounded; the
within-language quantization slopes reported here do not rest on that comparison, which a
parallel-translated instrument that holds content fixed would remove.

 
 Ethics Statement

 
 This work studies a safety and bias failure mode in deployed models and introduces an
evaluation, not an attack. QuantiBias quotes model-produced stereotype text for measurement; we
mark such content with warnings and select it deterministically rather than by hand.
Harmful-prompt benchmarks (StrongREJECT) are used only to measure refusal, under standard
responsible-disclosure practice. The study involves no human subjects; all data are model
generations over public benchmarks. The intended use of QuantiBias is to re-evaluate quantized
builds for bias before release, closing a gap that current short-form safety testing leaves
open.

 The flagship probe.

 
 MBTP is a multilingual generative stereotype benchmark. Each item is
an open-ended request paired with a stereotype-endorsing claim and its group-swapped counterpart,
which fix the axis a judge scores without constraining the model’s answer. We freeze a 320-item
subset, balanced across categories, spanning eight languages (Arabic, Chinese, English, French,
German, Japanese, Russian, Spanish) and 13 social categories, with a fixed seed and a frozen
prompt order. The reasoning-load contrast is applied at run time (deliberation on versus off), not
baked into the prompt, so the same frozen item serves both conditions. The parallel-translated
variant is built by rendering 40 canonical English items into the other seven languages with a
frontier model and back-translating every field for a gross-drift check, holding content fixed so

 C.1 Rarely-reinforced behaviors collapse first

 
 A rate-constrained code spends its bits where the signal is strong. Behaviors reinforced rarely in
training carry a low-amplitude weight signal and are dropped first as precision falls. This is

compression result of Isik et al. (2022) and the information-bottleneck view that a
rate-constrained representation discards the least task-relevant information first
 ( Tishby et al., 1999 ; Tishby and Zaslavsky, 2015 ) . It is the formal counterpart of the empirical finding
that compression forgets the long tail ( Hooker et al., 2019 ; Hooker et al., 2020 ) .
