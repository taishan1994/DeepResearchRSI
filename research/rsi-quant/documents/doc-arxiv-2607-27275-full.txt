 Flat Score, Amplified Failures:
 How the Error Budget Masks Damage in Quantized LLM Agents

 
 
 Jiwon Jang
 
    
 Kisu Yang
 
    
 Heuiseok Lim \corresponding 
 
    
 Hyunwoo Park \corresponding 
 

 Abstract

 
 Post-training quantization to 4-bit weights is widely reported to be nearly lossless. We test this claim for multi-turn, tool-calling agents, where it now matters most. On τ 2 \tau^{2} -bench, across two open-weight model families in dense and MoE variants and two domains (eight cells, 456 episodes each, at 16-, 8-, and 4-bit weights), quantization indeed looks free on the standard metric. No cell shows a score change that survives multiple-comparison correction, and in the cell that carries the largest process damage, equivalence testing bounds the change within ± 7.5 \pm 7.5 points. The process tells a different story. Quantization amplifies the failure the model already exhibits at full precision (tool-name hallucination in telecom, with the same directional trend in retail entity errors) by up to 2.5 × 2.5\times in volume ( + 17.6 +17.6 points per task), while creating essentially no new failures. The failure set is the same at every precision (rank correlation ≥ 0.94 \geq 0.94 , 0.18 % 0.18\% novel events). The score stays flat because the benchmark’s ten-error budget absorbs the extra failures. Shrinking the budget to two errors re-exposes a score gap of 17 17 points, and it does so only in the one cell where quantization added error volume, exactly as the masking account predicts. A targeted error-repair prompt, run for five telecom models at every precision, removes the damage exactly and only where it lives. Both diagnostics, the per-channel error rate and success under a shrinking budget, come from logs benchmarks already collect; we suggest reporting them alongside task reward.

 Weight quantization is the default mechanism for serving large language
models (LLMs) at scale. Reducing weights from 16 to 4 bits cuts memory
and latency several-fold, and systematic evaluations report 4-bit
quantization as nearly lossless, degrading standard benchmark accuracy
by only a point or two  ( Frantar et al. 2023 ; Lin et al. 2024 ; Li et al. 2024b ; Jin et al. 2024 ) . That verdict, however, rests
almost entirely on single-turn evaluations, while deployed LLMs
increasingly act as agents , multi-turn tool-calling
controllers that interact with an environment, observe feedback, and
recover from their own mistakes.

 
 RQ1: Does “nearly
lossless” survive the shift to multi-turn tool-calling agents, and
can the benchmark’s final score tell? The natural way to ask turns
out to be misleading.
The obvious experiment is to run quantized and full-precision agents
on an agentic benchmark and compare task success. We do exactly this
on τ 2 \tau^{2} -bench  ( Barres et al. 2026 ) , a tool-calling benchmark with
a simulated user, across two open-weight model families
 × \times {dense, MoE} × \times two domains (the full
eight-cell grid, 456 simulations per arm), plus two earlier Qwen-3.5
checkpoints in telecom. The result is a flat line
(Figure  1 a). No score change survives
multiple-comparison correction, and the largest paired decline,
 2.0 2.0 points, sits within 1.3 × 1.3\times the run-to-run noise floor.
Taken at face value, quantization is free in the agentic setting too.
Compression studies of agents have so far stopped at this aggregate
verdict  ( Dong et al. 2025 ) ; the rest of the paper asks what it
conceals.

 
 We argue this conclusion is an artifact of what task reward
measures . Task reward is a binary, end-of-trajectory outcome. It is
swamped by task-difficulty variance and, more importantly, the
agentic loop actively repairs the errors quantization
introduces before they reach the outcome. Decomposed at the level of
individual tool calls, trajectories instead reveal a large effect
with two parts. First, universally, quantization does not
change what the model gets wrong . In telecom, where the
channel is tool-name hallucination (calls outside the agent’s tool
list, nearly always real tools on the user’s side of the
dual-control interface), the BF16 and INT4 distributions over
hallucinated names are nearly identical, and the invariance holds at
8 bits. The failure set is a property of the model , not of
any precision.
Second, in a cell-dependent way, quantization multiplies how
often that set fires . In the highest-amplification cell
(Gemma-4 dense, telecom), 4-bit weights produce 2.5 × 2.5\times more
out-of-list calls ( → 1,646 649\!\to\!1{,}646 ); across cells the multiplier
ranges from ≈ 1 × {\approx}1\times to 4.6 × 4.6\times , and in retail the
channel that moves is entity/argument errors instead ( 1.45 × 1.45\times ,
Gemma-4 MoE, a trend short of significance). Quantization rescales
the model’s existing error set; the scale factor is a property of the
cell.

 
 RQ2: Why does the final score stay flat while the
agent’s per-step failures multiply? Because the loop hides the
damage. τ 2 \tau^{2} -bench, like other agentic
benchmarks, allows ten failed tool calls per episode, and per-error
recovery is equally reliable at both precisions, so the budget
absorbs the extra failures quantization produces. Tightening the
tolerance from ten errors to two re-exposes the deficit, growing the
INT4 score gap from 1.3 1.3 to 16.7 16.7 points, and it re-exposes damage
only in the one cell where quantization added error volume ( 15.4 15.4 
points there, at most 0.2 0.2 elsewhere). The score is flat because the
environment is forgiving, not because the model is unharmed. The same
localization predicts the remedy, and a reflexive error-repair
prompt indeed collapses the channel wherever it runs but moves score
only in the damaged cell.

 
 RQ3: Why are some models damaged by quantization
while others are unaffected? Not size or dense-versus-MoE architecture but
the model’s full-precision failure propensity . A model that
rarely hallucinates tools at 16 bits (Qwen-3.6) has nothing to
amplify, a model with a standing
tendency (Gemma-4) has that tendency magnified, and an older Qwen-3.5
checkpoint whose gate is slightly open is slightly, but
significantly, amplified. The open gate is necessary rather than
sufficient, since a cell with an equally open gate (Gemma-4 MoE
telecom, 19.3 % 19.3\% ) multiplies by only 1.05 × 1.05\times . A logit-margin account is
consistent with this pattern. Quantization noise flips only
thin-margin argmax decisions, precisely those the model already got
wrong occasionally, making baseline propensity the observable shadow
of the thin-margin mass.

 
 Our contributions are:

 
 • 
 
 The final score cannot detect 4-bit damage (RQ1). 
End-of-episode task reward is not merely low-powered but
 actively masked by the environment’s error budget; stopping
at task success yields a false “all-clear.”

 • 
 
 Quantization amplifies the existing failure set
(RQ1). (i) The failure set is precision-invariant across 16,
8, and 4 bits ( 0.97 0.97 correlation, 0.18 % 0.18\% novel events), ruling
out the “some models are just worse” confound; (ii) the failure
 volume multiplier is cell-dependent, 1 × 1\times to
 4.6 × 4.6\times , so damage claims must be made per cell.

 • 
 
 The error budget masks the damage, falsifiably
(RQ2). A counterfactual budget analysis shows the recovery slack
hides the damage; its advance prediction, that tightening
re-exposes damage only in cells with added error volume, holds
across all cells (one adds volume, nine check the converse),
widening 15.4 15.4 
points there versus at most 0.2 0.2 elsewhere; and a reflexive
error-repair system, run for five telecom models at all three
precisions, recovers score only in the damaged cell.

 • 
 
 Full-precision propensity gates the risk (RQ3). 
Neither architecture nor size does. A standing full-precision failure
channel is the precondition , and the added error volume the
observable signature , of maskable damage. Both come from logs
benchmarks already collect; we suggest reporting them alongside task
reward, and low-slack deployments face more 4-bit risk than
single-turn benchmarks show.

 

 
 Related Work

 
 Post-training quantization and its evaluation.

 
 Post-training weight quantization  ( Frantar et al. 2023 ; Lin et al. 2024 ; Xiao et al. 2023 ; Dettmers et al. 2022 ) has become the default
mechanism for serving large language models, and systematic
evaluations consistently report that 4-bit quantization leaves
aggregate benchmark accuracy largely
intact  ( Li et al. 2024b ; Jin et al. 2024 ) . A separate
line of work, however, shows that this aggregate view conceals
non-uniform damage. Compression disproportionately affects long-tail
and underrepresented inputs  ( Hooker et al. 2019 ; Ahia et al. 2021 ) , increases hallucination rates in
generation  ( Li et al. 2024a ; Haque et al. 2025 ) , and can erode
alignment- and safety-related behaviors  ( Rath and Maliakkal 2026 ) .
These studies establish that quantization damage hides below
aggregate metrics, but in single-turn, static generation; none
characterize where it goes in a closed-loop agentic
trajectory, nor why end-to-end metrics stay flat while per-step
reliability degrades.

 
 Evaluating tool-calling agents.

 
 Benchmarks for tool use have progressed from single-turn function
calling  ( Patil et al. 2024 ) , function-calling
leaderboards  ( Patil et al. 2025 ) , and stepwise capability
probes  ( Chen et al. 2024 ; Qin et al. 2024 ) to
long-horizon interactive environments with simulated users, most
notably τ \tau -bench  ( Yao et al. 2025 ) and its dual-control
successor τ 2 \tau^{2} -bench  ( Barres et al. 2026 ) . These environments
score agents by end-of-episode task reward
(pass^ k k ), and prior analyses note that tool use is
fragile  ( Cuadron et al. 2026 ) , yet the benchmarks ship with
generous per-episode error tolerances allowing retries. All of this work evaluates full-precision models; the
interaction of error tolerance with per-step error rate has not been
examined as a measurement problem.

 
 Compression under agentic workloads.

 
 Closest to our setting, Dong et al. (2025) evaluate quantized
and pruned models on agentic capability suites and report
capability-level degradation patterns, and
 Paramanayakam et al. (2025) document performance drops when
quantized models serve as on-device function-calling agents. Both
efforts score isolated steps or aggregate accuracy on a fixed
pipeline. We instead evaluate quantized agents closed-loop across
single- and dual-control domains, decompose failures into
domain-specific channels, and trace individual failure events across
precisions within a single model, yielding two findings unavailable
to step-level or aggregate evaluation. Quantization amplifies the model’s pre-existing failure
set rather than creating new failures, and the final score is blind to
the amplification because the environment’s error budget absorbs the
extra failures until that budget is tightened.

 
 Experimental Setup

 
 Benchmark.

 
 We evaluate on τ 2 \tau^{2} -bench  ( Barres et al. 2026 ) , a tool-calling
benchmark in which an agent resolves a customer request over a
multi-turn conversation with a simulated user, issuing tool calls
against a stateful environment. We use its two domains.
 Retail is a single-control setting in which only the agent
acts on the environment; telecom is a dual-control setting
in which the user also executes actions on their own device and the
agent must reason about state it cannot manipulate directly. The domains differ
in horizon and tool inventory (telecom trajectories are longer,
median ∼ \sim 16 versus ∼ \sim 11 agent turns, with a larger, less
standard tool set) and, as we show, expose different failure
channels. Each domain
contains 114 tasks; an episode ends when the user closes the
conversation, when the agent exhausts max_errors = 10 {=}10 
failed tool calls, or at max_steps = 300 {=}300 . Task reward is the standard
 τ 2 \tau^{2} -bench outcome (a final database-state check plus per-task
assertions). In practice it is binary in both domains, so we report
its mean as the final score in percent.

 
 Models and quantization.

 
 We study two open-weight LLM families, each in a dense and a
mixture-of-experts (MoE) variant, namely Gemma-4-31B (dense) and
 Gemma-4-26B-A4B (MoE, 4B active parameters), and
 Qwen-3.6-27B (dense) and Qwen-3.6-35B-A3B (MoE, 3B
active parameters), plus Qwen-3.5-27B and
 Qwen-3.5-9B (both dense) as family-version and size
extensions. Each model runs at three precision arms:
 BF16 , the original 16-bit weights and full-precision
reference; FP8 ; and INT4 , quantized with AWQ. All agents
are served with vLLM on NVIDIA A100 GPUs; the A100 has no native FP8
compute, so the FP8 weights run weight-only via the Marlin
kernel (W8A16). Activations and the KV cache are 16-bit in every
arm; all three arms are weight-only. Recipes differ by family
(AWQ-style with g = 32 g{=}32 for Gemma INT4, zero-point AWQ with
 g = 128 g{=}128 for Qwen INT4).
The full cross-product (two families × \times {dense, MoE}
 × \times two domains, eight cells) is complete at all three
precisions, with both Qwen-3.5 telecom extensions on top. Episodes terminated by infrastructure_error ( ≤ 3 \leq 3 
per arm) carry no reward record and are excluded (one retail cell mixed max_steps of 200
and 300; no episode reaches either cap).

 Protocol.

 
 To isolate the effect of agent quantization, the simulated user
is held fixed at full precision (Qwen-3.6-27B, BF16) across all
conditions; only the agent’s precision varies. In the Qwen-3.6 dense
cells the agent thus shares a base model with the user simulator; the
Qwen-3.5 and Qwen-3.6 MoE cells, which do not, behave identically, so
this does not drive the family contrast. We run num_trials = 4 {=}4 trials
per task, giving 114 × 4 = 456 114\times 4=456 simulations per cell. vLLM serving
is not bit-deterministic (continuous batching perturbs reduction
order); a repeated 456-episode run shifted the mean score by about
 1.5 1.5 points, our coarse single-repeat estimate of the run-to-run
noise floor. Unless noted, comparisons are BF16 versus INT4 within a
fixed model and domain, holding family, architecture, task
distribution, and user simulator constant.

 
 Process metrics and failure channels.

 
 Beyond task reward, we log every tool call and its outcome. Our primary
process metric is the channel rate , the fraction of the agent’s
own tool calls (user-simulator calls excluded) that trigger a given
failure channel, pooled over a cell; paired tests use the same rate
computed per task. We decompose failures into two
channels by matching the environment’s error string. A
 tool-name hallucination is a call to a tool that does not exist
in the domain’s inventory (error Tool ’ X X ’ not found ); this is
the dominant channel in telecom. An entity/argument error is a
well-formed call whose arguments reference a record that cannot be found
(e.g. a wrong customer or order identifier); this is the channel in
retail. A raw “not found” count would conflate inventing a tool
with passing a wrong identifier, and the two behave differently. We additionally track trajectory length
(agent turns, tool calls), termination reasons, and, for the mechanism
analysis, the per-task and per-tool-name distributions of hallucinations.

 
 Statistics.

 
 For each metric we report the change from BF16 to INT4 with a 95%
confidence interval from 5,000 5{,}000 bootstrap resamples. Because the
four trials of a task share its difficulty, we resample tasks 
with replacement, keeping all trials of each sampled task (a cluster
bootstrap over the 114 tasks); an interval excluding zero denotes
significance. Episode-level resampling gives narrower intervals; we
report clustered intervals throughout.
Paired-by-task comparisons (removing task-difficulty variance) and
equivalence tests (two one-sided tests, TOST) at ± 7.5 \pm 7.5 points
supplement these where noted. For the mechanism analysis we compute,
within one model × \times domain, the BF16/INT4 correlation of
hallucinated-name distributions, the fraction of INT4 events unseen at BF16, and the per-task BF16/INT4 rate relationship.

 
 Repair arm.

 
 For the mitigation study, five telecom models (the grid’s four plus
Qwen-3.5-27B) are additionally run at every precision with a
 reflexive repair loop:
on a failed tool call, the agent is prompted to diagnose the error
message and re-issue a corrected call, up to three attempts. Agent
decoding is greedy in all arms (verified from the logs:
identical prefixes always yield identical outputs); repair arms
differ from base only in user-simulator temperature, 0.6 0.6 versus
 0.7 0.7 , so we read contrasts against the run-to-run noise floor.

 
 Counterfactual error budget.

 
 To ask whether the environment’s error tolerance hides the damage, we
treat max_errors as a knob. For a budget K K , the
 surviving success rate S ⁡ ( K ) S(K) is the fraction of episodes that
succeeded using at most K K failed tool calls. The projection is
conservative (an episode exceeding K K counts as a failure even if it
finished earlier) but applied identically to both
precisions, so the gap S BF16 ​ ( K ) − S INT4 ​ ( K ) S_{\text{BF16}}(K)-S_{\text{INT4}}(K) is a fair
comparison; too_many_errors terminations occur at 10–11
counted errors, confirming K K matches max_errors .

 
 Results

 
 Final Task Reward Is Blind to Quantization

 
 Table  1 (left) and Figure  1 a
report the change in the final score from 16- to 4-bit weights. In
all ten cells the 95% interval includes zero; the largest point
estimate is 4.6 4.6 points and five cells nominally improve . The
absence of a difference is not merely low power. In seven cells,
including the highest-signal one below, the change is statistically
 equivalent to zero by TOST at ± 7.5 \pm 7.5 points, paired by task. In the three remaining Gemma-4 cells
the intervals are wider than the margin, so flatness there means no
detectable change, not equivalence. The largest paired decline, 2.0 2.0 points in Qwen-3.6 MoE telecom at
 p = .049 p{=}.049 , survives neither multiple-comparison correction nor the
 1.5 1.5 -point noise floor and lies well inside the equivalence margin. Paired by task in the highest-signal cell, 4-bit
is worse on 33 tasks, better on 26, tied on 55.
Aggregate or paired, task success cannot distinguish a
4-bit agent from a full-precision one ; the rest of this section
shows why that all-clear is wrong.

 
 The Damage Is Real, in the Process, and Channel-Specific

 
 When we decompose trajectories into the two failure channels
(Table  1 , right, and Figure  1 b),
a large and systematic effect emerges. In telecom ,
whose active channel is tool-name hallucination, 4-bit quantization
raises the Gemma-4 dense hallucination rate from 19.5 % 19.5\% to 38.3 % 38.3\% 
of the agent’s tool calls ( + 18.8 {+}18.8 points, CI [ 14.0 , 23.6 ] [14.0,23.6] ), a
 2.0 × 2.0\times increase, and drives a 9 × 9\times rise in
error-budget-exhausting terminations ( → 46 5\!\to\!46 of 456 episodes).
Because INT4 trajectories also contain more calls per episode
( → 9.4 7.3\!\to\!9.4 ), the 2.0 × 2.0\times rate ratio is a 2.5 × 2.5\times growth
in event volume ( → 1,646 649\!\to\!1{,}646 ), the multiplier we quote. In retail , whose active channel is entity/argument
errors, the same family moves in that channel instead
(Gemma-4 MoE, 4.2 % → 5.7 % 4.2\%\!\rightarrow\!5.7\% , + 1.5 {+}1.5 , CI
 [ − 0.1 , + 3.1 ] [-0.1,+3.1] , a trend short of significance under clustered
intervals). Whichever failure the domain makes available,
quantization pushes on it in Gemma-4. The channel structure is
sharp. Retail produces zero tool-name events and telecom
entity rates stay ≤ 0.1 % {\leq}0.1\% . Each domain opens exactly one
failure mode, and quantization can only amplify an open channel.

 
 Three observations sharpen the picture. First, Qwen-3.6 is not 
amplified in any of its four cells. Telecom tool-name stays at
 0.38 % → 0.47 % 0.38\%\!\to\!0.47\% , not significant, with 9 events at BF16,
and retail entity rates are flat. Second, architecture is not a stand-alone
predictor . The one strongly amplified cell is dense, yet the dense
Qwen-3.6 is flat everywhere, and the Gemma-4 MoE shows only
non-significant trends ( + 3.8 {+}3.8 points in telecom, + 1.5 {+}1.5 in
retail), so dense-versus-MoE by itself does not sort damaged from
undamaged cells. Third, susceptibility is
 checkpoint-specific . The previous-generation Qwen-3.5,
whose gate is slightly open ( 0.26 % 0.26\% ), is significantly amplified
( → 1.21 % \rightarrow 1.21\% , + 0.95 {+}0.95 [ 0.5 , 1.5 ] [0.5,1.5] ), Gemma-4’s pattern
at a twentieth of the magnitude, while its successor and its smaller
9B sibling (gate 0.61 % 0.61\% ) are both flat. The
discriminating variable is the model’s own baseline behavior, not
size or architecture (§ Analysis).

 
 The Cliff Is (Mostly) at Four Bits

 
 Sweeping the intermediate precision shows that 8 bits, weight-only
in our serving setup, is
close to free. No cell degrades significantly in score (one nominally
 improves by 9.7 9.7 points; with ten cells tested, one
excursion is unsurprising) and no telecom channel moves at FP8; the
jump to 38.3 % 38.3\% happens entirely between 8 and 4 bits. One trend
is instructive. Gemma-4 MoE retail already shows its full INT4-sized
entity/argument movement at FP8 ( + 1.40 {+}1.40 , CI [ − 0.2 , 3.0 ] [-0.2,3.0] , not
significant under clustered intervals). If that trend is real, the
safe-precision boundary, too, is a property of the
model × \times channel pair, not a universal constant.

 Quantization Amplifies the Existing Failure Set, Not New Ones

 
 Is the damage a new pathology or a scaling-up of what the model
already did? We answer within a single model and domain ,
Gemma-4 dense telecom, removing any family or architecture confound.

 
 At the level of individual tool names, quantization is almost purely
multiplicative (Figure  2 a). The set of hallucinated
tool names is essentially unchanged. The per-tool frequency
distributions at BF16 and INT4 correlate at 0.97 0.97 , only 3 of the

 Why the Score Stays Flat: the Error Budget Masks It

 
 Sections above leave a puzzle. If per-step failures rise
 2.5 × 2.5\times , why is the final score unchanged? Because the agentic loop recovers the
extra failures, and the benchmark’s error budget pays for the recovery
(Table  2 ). Two facts establish this. First, quantization
raises failed tool calls per episode from 1.45 1.45 to 3.64 3.64 
( 2.5 × 2.5\times ), swelling the ≥ 5 {\geq}5 -failure tail from 51 to 173 of
456 episodes (Figure  4 ) and stretching
trajectories ( → 17.9 16.4\!\to\!17.9 turns, → 9.4 7.3\!\to\!9.4 calls;
errors–turns r = 0.48 r{=}0.48 ), error-driven recovery that quietly
refunds part of the compute quantization saved. Second, the model recovers
from each individual failure about equally well at both
precisions. The recovery rate after at least one failed call is
 55 % 55\% at both BF16 and INT4. Quantization does not
weaken recovery; it simply produces more failures to recover from,
and the 10 10 -error budget absorbs them. 

 
 Treating that budget as a knob makes the masking explicit and
falsifiable (Figure  3 ). Recomputing the surviving
success rate S ⁡ ( K ) S(K) under tighter budgets, the score gap between BF16
and INT4 grows as K K shrinks. It is 1.3 1.3 points at the default
 K = 10 K{=}10 , then 7.5 7.5 at K = 5 K{=}5 and 16.7 16.7 at K = 2 K{=}2 , roughly a
 13 × 13\times widening; the same tightening costs BF16 10 10 points but
INT4 25 25 . The Qwen-3.6-27B cell is a negative control. With almost
no baseline failures, masking predicts no hidden gap there, and
indeed it is flat at every K K , nothing for a tighter budget to
expose.

 
 The budget response doubles as a falsification test. If masking
explains the flat score, tightening should re-expose damage only
where quantization added error volume. Across all cells this
is exactly what happens. The gap widens by 15.4 15.4 points in Gemma-4
dense telecom, the one cell with a large added volume, while no other
cell widens by more than 0.2 0.2 points. The largest movement elsewhere
is a 2.2 2.2 -point narrowing in Gemma-4 MoE telecom, whose event
volume grows only about 5 % 5\% . Where nothing was hidden, nothing is
revealed. The score is flat because the environment is forgiving,
and the damage re-emerges precisely where, and only where, it
exists.
The test is not vacuous, since extra failures landing in episodes
already lost would re-expose nothing, and this partly happens. All
46 INT4 exhaustions score zero yet the default-budget gap is only
 1.3 1.3 points, so part of the damage lands in episodes lost anyway. With one
volume-adding cell the positive arm has a single member; the eight
flat cells check the converse, and a rerun at a tightened budget is
the decisive version of the test.

 Why Quantization Amplifies Existing Failures

 
 A logit-margin account.

 
 The amplify-but-do-not-invent pattern of Figure  2 
follows from a simple property of quantized decoding. At any generation
step, let the model choose among tokens by their logits, and consider the
correct token c c and its strongest competitor w w , with margin
 m = z c − z w m=z_{c}-z_{w} . Weight quantization perturbs the logits by approximately
zero-mean noise η \eta , so the post-quantization margin is m ′ = m + η m^{\prime}=m+\eta .
The probability that the argmax flips (that the model now errs)
changes by
 Δ p ( m ) = 𝔼 η [ σ ( − ( m + η ) / τ ) ] − σ ( − m / τ ) \Delta p(m)=\mathbb{E}_{\eta}[\sigma(-(m+\eta)/\tau)]-\sigma(-m/\tau) 
for a temperature-like scale τ \tau . It is near zero for large
positive m m (confident decisions) and large negative m m (already
lost), and maximized near m ≈ 0 m\approx 0 . Quantization damage
therefore concentrates on thin-margin decisions.

 
 Two predictions follow, both borne out. First, a thin-margin flip
promotes the already -closest competitor, the failure the
model occasionally made anyway, so quantization reuses the existing
failure set ( 0.97 0.97 correlation, 0.18 % 0.18\% novel).
Second, baseline error rate and quantization-induced increase are
governed by the same latent quantity, the mass of thin-margin
decisions, so a model’s full-precision failure rate is the
 observable shadow of its quantization sensitivity; the
prediction is not circular, being tested at a finer grain, the
per-tool-name distribution, than the aggregate rate it predicts.
A positional test separates this account from a natural
alternative in which quantization erodes instruction-following as the
context grows. That account predicts damage concentrated late, but
the INT4-to-BF16 ratio stays within
 1.3 × 1.3\times – 2.0 × 2.0\times in every turn bin and the hallucination rate
 falls in the longest-context quartile at both precisions.

 
 What governs susceptibility.

 
 The account explains the family split. Qwen-3.6 adheres almost
perfectly to the tool schema at full precision ( 0.38 % 0.38\% , 9
events). It has essentially no thin-margin mass here and nothing to
amplify, genuinely robust rather than merely saturated, as the
process metric confirms. Gemma-4 carries a standing
tendency to invent plausible tools (649 events), and quantization
magnifies exactly that tendency. The claim is not “Gemma is worse”
but “quantization scales up whatever a model is already prone to.”
Architecture does not set this propensity; the checkpoint does. The
Qwen releases have near-zero baselines ( 0.26 % 0.26\% versus 0.38 % 0.38\% ) yet
land on opposite sides of significance ( + 0.95 {+}0.95 versus + 0.09 {+}0.09 
points), so near the closed end the gate pins the ceiling ,
damage near 1 % 1\% versus 38 % 38\% where it is wide open, rather than
the exact magnitude. The converse is sharper than raw counts
suggest. Gemma-4 MoE telecom opens the gate as wide per call as the
dense variant ( 19.3 % 19.3\% versus 19.5 % 19.5\% at BF16, 841 events) yet
multiplies by only 1.05 × 1.05\times , per-tool factors scattering
 0.5 × 0.5\times – 1.5 × 1.5\times instead of the dense 2.5 × 2.5\times . The gate is necessary for
large damage, not sufficient; the aggregate rate cannot see the
thin-margin mass, which varies by cell. The domain contrast follows the same
logic. Telecom’s dual-control split places plausible device actions
just across the agent/user role boundary, creating thin-margin
choices between near-synonymous agent- and user-side names;
single-control retail has no boundary and a structurally zero
tool-name channel.

 
 Discussion

 
 Agentic benchmarks need process-level reporting.

 
 End-of-episode reward is blind to quantization for a structural
reason. It is a saturating outcome buffered by the environment’s
error tolerance, so a benchmark reporting only task
reward certifies a compressed agent whose per-step reliability has
halved. Benchmarks should report per-channel error rates 
alongside task reward and, where an error budget exists, success as
a function of that budget;
both come from existing logs and are far more sensitive.

 
 Full-precision propensity is a cheap risk screen.

 
 Susceptibility is governed by full-precision propensity, so risk can
be screened before quantizing by measuring the per-channel
error rate on the target domain. A near-zero rate, as for Qwen-3.6,
signals 4-bit is likely safe there. The
screen is one-directional, an elevated rate flagging risk without
pinning the multiplier, since an equally open gate can stay flat
(Gemma-4 MoE telecom); it still beats size and architecture.

 
 Deployment slack is not always benchmark slack.

 
 The masking depends on a forgiving environment. Many real
deployments are not forgiving, with single-shot interactions, strict
error limits, irreversible actions, and no silent retries. With the slack gone,
amplified per-step failures become outcome failures, as the
budget-tightening analysis projects. “Lossless on the benchmark”
need not be lossless where deployed.

 
 A mechanism-targeted mitigation.

 
 The damage is amplification of a small, enumerable set the
model already produces at full precision, with 99.8 % 99.8\% of INT4
hallucinations repeating BF16 tool names, so the remedy is
forbidding that set at the source rather than general robustness.
Constrained decoding, native in vLLM, eliminates the dominant
channel by restricting the tool-name field to the inventory. We run the lighter reflexive repair system of Setup for five
telecom models at all precisions; the one significant gain is the damaged
cell, Gemma-4 dense at INT4, + 5.9 {+}5.9 points (CI [ + 1.1 , + 10.8 ] [+1.1,+10.8] ,
 p = .017 p{=}.017 ) to 71.3 % 71.3\% , above unmitigated BF16 ( 66.7 % 66.7\% ), while
every other interval includes zero. The mechanism is prevention
rather than cure. The in-context inventory collapses the out-of-list
volume from 1,646 1{,}646 events to 82 82 and shortens 
trajectories ( → 15.9 17.9\!\to\!15.9 turns). Both negative controls behave. Qwen
cells, with nothing to repair, move within or near the
 ± 2.0 \pm 2.0 -point repeat-run band, and repair trends negative only
where nothing needs fixing (Gemma-4 MoE, whose score dip is
 not channel-mediated, − 2.6 -2.6 at BF16 and − 4.2 -4.2 at INT4;
Qwen-3.6 − 2.4 -2.4 at FP8; none significant). The same
prompt suppresses its errors only 4 × 4\times ( → 217 882\!\to\!217 )
versus 20 × 20\times dense, and its trajectories lengthen. Help only where the channel damage lives,
nothing where nothing needs fixing, mild harm elsewhere, a third
confirmation of the localization. The
fix is per-channel. Retail’s entity/argument errors instead call for
validate-and-retry or argument grounding, the propensity screen
deciding when either is needed.

 
 Beyond quantization.

 
 Nothing in the logit-margin account is specific to quantization.
Any weight perturbation adding near-zero-mean logit noise (pruning,
low-rank compression, distillation drift) should likewise
amplify existing thin-margin failures, not create new ones.
The agentic loop accumulates and hides such damage, making
multi-turn tool use a probe for perturbations single-turn accuracy
misses.

 
 Conclusion

 
 Standard evaluations call 4-bit quantization nearly lossless, and
our ten agentic cells agree, with no final-score change surviving
correction. The agreement is an artifact of what the score can see.
Underneath, quantization amplifies the failures the model already
exhibits, the same tools invented more often, up to 2.5 × 2.5\times 
while creating almost nothing new. The benchmark’s error budget absorbs the extra failures, and
tightening it re-exposes the damage exactly where, and only where,
it was added, and a channel-targeted repair recovers the score
there. Susceptibility follows the model’s own full-precision
propensity, not its size or architecture. Measure process, not just
outcome.

also falls in the longest-context quartile at both precisions (BF16
 34.5 % → 27.2 % 34.5\%\to 27.2\% , INT4 61.8 % → 41.9 % 61.8\%\to 41.9\% ), and the INT4-to-BF16
ratio stays within 1.3 × 1.3\times – 2.0 × 2.0\times in every bin. An account
in which quantization erodes instruction-following as context grows
predicts the ratio to rise with position; the observed uniformity is
what a per-decision account predicts. 
 

 
 Appendix B Limitations

 
 The paper’s strongest claims are within-model, robust to the
family confound; four limitations bound the generalization. (i) Two
families plus two further Qwen checkpoints support the propensity
claim as a gate, open versus closed, not a fitted curve; a curve
needs more checkpoints. (ii) The budget-masking result is a
projection from existing trajectories, conservative by construction;
a rerun at tightened budgets would confirm it directly, and the
margin account is likewise indirect, since token log-probabilities
are not in our logs, though Figure  6 rules out the
leading alternative. (iii) The INT4 recipes differ between families
(Table  6 ), partly conflating checkpoint with recipe;
the confound is bounded, since the finer-grouped, normally more
accurate recipe belongs to the amplified family and the
recipe-matched Qwen-3.5/3.6 pair is separated by baseline propensity
alone, but a same-recipe requantization is the missing control. (iv)
Our evidence spans one benchmark, two domains, and weight-only
recipes on A100 GPUs with a 16-bit KV cache, a fixed full-precision
user simulator, and a telecom-only repair arm; low-bit KV caches,
activation-quantized FP8, other benchmarks, and compressed user
simulators are open.

 
 Appendix C Reproducibility Details

 
 Benchmark and protocol.

 
 All runs use a single τ 2 \tau^{2} -bench commit with its standard
 retail and telecom domains (114 tasks each),
 num_trials = 4 {=}4 , seed 300 300 ,
 max_steps = 300 {=}300 , max_errors = 10 {=}10 , agent
sampling temperature 0.0 0.0 , and user-simulator temperature 0.7 0.7 
( 0.6 0.6 in the repair arms, which also set an explicit agent seed). One
nonuniformity remains. The Gemma-4-31B retail arms ran at
 max_steps = 200 {=}200 (BF16, INT4) and 300 300 (FP8); no episode
in any arm reaches either cap, so the difference is immaterial. The
user simulator is Qwen-3.6-27B at BF16 in every arm, served as a
separate vLLM instance; only the agent model and its precision vary
across cells.

 
 Serving.

 
 Agents are served with vLLM on NVIDIA A100 GPUs with a 16-bit KV
cache. Because the A100 has no native FP8 compute, vLLM executes the
FP8 checkpoints as weight-only 8-bit via the Marlin kernel (W8A16);
the dynamic per-token activation quantization specified in the FP8
configurations is therefore inactive, and activations are 16-bit in
all three arms.

 
 Quantization configurations.

 
 Table  6 lists each arm’s weight-quantization recipe as
read from the served checkpoints’ quantization_config .

 
 Coincidence note.

 
 The identical score triples of the Qwen-3.6-35B-A3B and Qwen-3.5-27B
telecom rows in Table  1 are a counting
coincidence. Both cells succeed in 448 of 456 episodes at BF16 and
439 at INT4, while the underlying episode outcomes differ in 16 and
32 episodes respectively.

 
 Analysis.

 
 Every number in the paper is computed from the raw simulation logs by
a single analysis script (channel regexes, per-cell bootstrap and
paired tests, S ⁡ ( K ) S(K) projection, and figure/table generation); the
script and per-episode logs will be released upon publication.
