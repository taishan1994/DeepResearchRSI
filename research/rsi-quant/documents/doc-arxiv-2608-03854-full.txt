 When Calibration Depends on the Scoring Rule:
 Quantized Biomedical LLM Classification

 
 
 Anton Rasmussen
 
    
 Hong Qin
 

 Abstract

 
 Quantized large language models can run on consumer hardware, which motivates interest in on-premises processing of sensitive data.
Before such models can support evidence synthesis, however, their confidence estimates must be trustworthy.
Their reliability depends on implementation choices (prompt template, label wording, and scoring normalization) that are seldom treated as experimental variables.
We evaluate three 7-billion-parameter Mistral variants (a base model, BioMistral, and an instruction-tuned checkpoint evaluated without its chat template) at FP16, INT8, and INT4 on five-class sentence classification in medical abstracts.
We test two primary templates on n = 2,000 n=2{,}000 test sentences and two auxiliary templates on n = 200 n=200 validation sentences.
Our central observation, made without post-hoc calibration, is that switching from summed to mean-token log-likelihood reverses which model appears better calibrated: BioMistral’s mean calibration error across matched conditions nearly triples, while the instruction-tuned model’s drops by more than half.
Accuracy changes by at most 1.4 percentage points for these two checkpoints.
Negative log-likelihood and Brier score show the same reversal, so it is not specific to ECE binning.
The two primary templates were selected using test-derived examples, so absolute performance with them is exploratory.
Between them, prompt choice changes mean accuracy across precisions by 2.9 to 17.8 percentage points, depending on the model.
Eight-bit quantization changes accuracy by at most 1.1 percentage points for the adapted checkpoints; four-bit quantization shows mixed but non-catastrophic effects.
Post-hoc temperature scaling reduces calibration error under summed scoring but was not fitted under mean-token scoring, so whether the reversal survives per-scorer calibration is unknown.
These exploratory results suggest that calibration comparisons of decoder-based classifiers should treat scoring normalization and prompt design as first-order experimental decisions.

 Introduction

 
 Systematic reviews are central to evidence-based medicine, yet the manual screening and classification of research abstracts remains a major bottleneck.
Automated sentence classification, which assigns functional roles such as Background , Objective , Methods , Results , and Conclusions to sentences within medical abstracts, can accelerate evidence synthesis by structuring unstructured literature at scale  ( Dernoncourt and Lee 2017 ) .

 
 Large language models (LLMs) are natural candidates for this task, and BioMistral-7B  ( Labrak et al. 2024 ) exemplifies efforts to adapt general-purpose decoders to biomedical and medical NLP.
However, for evidence synthesis to be trustworthy, models must provide not only accurate classifications but also well-calibrated confidence estimates .

 
 Model compression makes local deployment feasible.
Techniques such as LLM.int8()  ( Dettmers et al. 2022 ) roughly halve inference-time weight memory relative to FP16, making single-GPU inference practical for many 7-billion-parameter models.
Local deployment can keep model inputs within institutional infrastructure rather than sending them to third-party inference services.
Whether compression preserves calibration quality is poorly understood, particularly for biomedical LLMs where the stakes of miscalibration are high.
Local deployment motivates our study but is not what we measure: all experiments use public PubMed abstracts, and we do not evaluate privacy protection or clinical deployment safety.

 
 Instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback can improve instruction following, human preference, and selected behavioral measures  ( Ouyang et al. 2022 ) , but recent studies find that post-training and the chat templates that accompany it both degrade probability calibration  ( Huang et al. 2026 ; Sanz-Guerrero, Mager, and von der
Wense 2026 ) .
Apparent calibration effects are also entangled with the probability-extraction protocol: the prompt template, verbalizer, scoring rule, and chat format all influence the induced class distribution.
Our exploratory results suggest that these measurement choices can dominate the apparent model comparison.

 
 This paper makes three contributions within this exploratory design:

 
 
 1. 
 
 Scoring-normalization sensitivity. We observe that switching from summed to mean-token log-likelihood scoring reverses which of two checkpoints, BioMistral-7B and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, appears better calibrated on PubMed RCT before any post-hoc calibration, while accuracy remains largely unchanged.
This observation motivates specifying and varying the scoring protocol in calibration comparisons.

 2. 
 
 Template variation. Between the two primary templates, evaluated on the same test sample, mean accuracy over the three precisions differs by ∼ {\sim} 3pp for Instruct, ∼ {\sim} 7pp for BioMistral, and up to ∼ {\sim} 18pp for the base model, and the template determines which model achieves the highest accuracy.

 3. 
 
 Quantization deltas. For the adapted checkpoints (BioMistral, Instruct), INT8 quantization changes accuracy by at most 1.1pp relative to FP16.
The base model shows larger INT8 effects on some templates (up to + + 4.2pp on Struct-Delim).
INT4 (NF4) shows heterogeneous effects that vary by model and template, without catastrophic degradation in the configurations tested.

 

 
 Related Work

 
 Calibration of neural networks.

 
 Guo et al. (2017) demonstrated that temperature scaling is a simple and effective post-hoc calibration method.
For pretrained transformers, calibration varies across tasks and distribution shifts, and temperature scaling can improve in-domain calibration  ( Desai and Durrett 2020 ) .
For RLHF-tuned LLMs, Tian et al. (2023) find that verbalized confidences are typically better calibrated than conditional token probabilities.

 
 Instruction tuning and calibration.

 
 Huang et al. (2026) show that instruction tuning can raise confidence without commensurate accuracy gains, worsening calibration across multiple languages.
 Sanz-Guerrero, Mager, and von der
Wense (2026) decouple post-training from chat templates, finding that templates independently aggravate miscalibration via an “ownership bias” (up to 26% higher confidence on self-generated answers).
These findings motivate our analysis: apparent calibration effects may reflect measurement artifacts.

 
 Verbalizer sensitivity and surface form competition.

 
 When decoder LMs are used as classifiers, predicted probabilities depend on the verbalizer , the mapping from task labels to model-facing output tokens.
 Holtzman et al. (2021) introduced the concept of surface form competition, showing that a model’s probability mass is split among surface-equivalent continuations, so the highest-probability answer is not always the semantically correct one.
 Cho, So, and Lee (2026) demonstrate that raw and length-normalized likelihood scoring remain sensitive to superficial answer-choice content and input format, calling into question standard LLM evaluation protocols.
 Zhao et al. (2021) show that few-shot prompting introduces strong recency, majority-label, and common-token biases into LLM predictions, proposing content-free calibration to mitigate them.
These results bear on our work: the five PubMed RCT label names have unequal token lengths (3 to 5 tokens), creating a surface-form length prior that interacts with the scoring policy (summed vs. mean log-likelihood).

 
 Biomedical LLMs.

 
 BioMistral-7B  ( Labrak et al. 2024 ) continues pretraining of Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.1 on PubMed Central articles, achieving competitive results on biomedical benchmarks.
PubMedBERT  ( Gu et al. 2021 ) demonstrated that domain-specific pretraining from scratch outperforms general-domain models on biomedical NLP tasks.
 Labrak et al. (2024) include quantized variant evaluation and some calibration analysis, but do not vary the scoring rule or verbalizer.

 
 Model quantization.

 
 LLM.int8()  ( Dettmers et al. 2022 ) introduced mixed-precision INT8 decomposition that preserves outlier features in FP16, achieving near-lossless compression.
QLoRA  ( Dettmers et al. 2023 ) introduced NF4 quantization with double quantization for efficient fine-tuning.
While predictive performance under quantization has been studied  ( Dettmers et al. 2022 ) , calibration effects have received less attention.
 Zhong et al. (2025) study calibration in quantized LLMs across multiple models and datasets, finding that quantization generally worsens calibration and proposing a soft-prompt recovery framework.
Our work complements theirs by examining answer-text scoring sensitivity within the quantized biomedical setting.

 
 Automated prompt optimization.

 
 EvoPrompting  ( Chen, Dohan, and So 2023 ) used language models as mutation and crossover operators in evolutionary neural architecture search.
AlphaEvolve  ( Novikov et al. 2025 ) extends this paradigm to general program optimization, using LLM-proposed code changes and automated evaluation metrics.
We use AlphaEvolve with our own evaluator for a small prompt search in biomedical classification template design.

 
 Methods

 
 Task and Dataset

 
 We evaluate on PubMed RCT sentence classification  ( Dernoncourt and Lee 2017 ) , a 5-class task assigning functional roles ( Background , Objective , Methods , Results , Conclusions ) to sentences from medical abstracts.
We use a stratified sample of n = 2,000 n=2{,}000 sentences from 1,362 1{,}362 abstracts in the test set for the main evaluation and, for temperature scaling, n = 200 n=200 validation-set examples for fitting plus a separate n = 200 n=200 test sample for evaluation.
Paired confidence intervals use abstract-cluster bootstrap resampling ( 5,000 5{,}000 resamples) to account for within-abstract sentence correlation.

 Scoring Protocols

 
 Answer-text log-likelihood (decoders).

 
 For each example and candidate label, we compute the sum of the token log probabilities of the label text (e.g., “BACKGROUND”) conditioned on the prompt:

 

 
 
 score ​ ( ℓ ) = ∑ t = 1 T ℓ log ⁡ P ⁡ ( w t ( ℓ ) ∣ prompt , w 1 ( ℓ ) , … , w t − 1 ( ℓ ) ) , \text{score}(\ell)=\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\ell}}\log P(w_{t}^{(\ell)}\mid\text{prompt},w_{1}^{(\ell)},\ldots,w_{t-1}^{(\ell)}), 
 
 
 
 where w 1 ( ℓ ) , … , w T ℓ ( ℓ ) w_{1}^{(\ell)},\ldots,w_{T_{\ell}}^{(\ell)} are the tokens of label ℓ \ell .
Because label names have unequal token counts ( T ℓ = 3 T_{\ell}=3 to 5 5 ), we also evaluate a length-normalized variant:

 

 
 
 score mean ( ℓ ) = 1 T ℓ ∑ t = 1 T ℓ log P ( w t ( ℓ ) | prompt , w < t ( ℓ ) ) . \text{score}_{\text{mean}}(\ell)=\frac{1}{T_{\ell}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{\ell}}\log P\!\left(w_{t}^{(\ell)}\,\middle|\,\text{prompt},w_{<t}^{(\ell)}\right). 
 
 
 
 We softmax-normalize the candidate scores (sum or mean) across the five labels to obtain a probability distribution.
The two scorers are not symmetric alternatives: the softmax over summed log-likelihoods is the model’s conditional distribution over the five candidate label strings, whereas the length-normalized variant is a widely used heuristic without that probabilistic interpretation.
To preserve the tokenizer boundary, for each label we form a leading-space continuation and jointly tokenize the canonical prompt-plus-continuation string with add_special_tokens=False .
We locate the candidate suffix after the longest stable prefix shared with the standalone prompt tokenization, then verify that decoding the reconstructed prompt and candidate token IDs reproduces the canonical string after trimming surrounding whitespace.
For each candidate, the saved artifacts retain its token IDs, decoded token pieces, token count, and summed and mean log-likelihoods.

 
 Encoder softmax (PubMedBERT).

 
 The fine-tuned classification head produces logits over five classes, which are softmax-normalized to obtain probabilities.

 
 Prompt Templates

 
 We evaluate four few-shot answer-text prompt templates for the decoder models.
All four templates use the same verbalizer (the literal class names Background , Objective , etc.) as continuation targets, so they share one label-to-token mapping.
They differ in instructions, examples, delimiters, and continuation framing:

 
 
 • 
 
 Struct-Delim ( structured-delimiter ): Wraps the input sentence in triple-quote delimiters with explicit category definitions.
Selected by the AlphaEvolve search.

 • 
 
 Bare-Cont ( bare-continuation ): Uses bare text without delimiters and the same category definitions.
Selected by the AlphaEvolve search.

 • 
 
 Min-Inst ( minimal-instruction ): Concise task instruction with domain-shifted few-shot exemplars (diabetes/GLP-1 domain).
LLM-designed to test robustness to instruction verbosity and exemplar domain.

 • 
 
 Alt-Frame ( alternate-frame ): Changes the continuation frame (“Input:”/“Role:” instead of “Sentence:”/“Rhetorical Role:”) with distinct exemplars (chronic kidney disease domain).
LLM-designed to test sensitivity to the lexical frame.

 

 
 All four templates share a common answer-text scoring interface: (1) a task preamble, (2) five few-shot demonstrations (one per class), and (3) a natural continuation point for answer-text log-likelihood scoring.
Struct-Delim and Bare-Cont were selected by the AlphaEvolve search described next, and results using them are exploratory.
Min-Inst and Alt-Frame were designed independently of that search, with different exemplar domains, to probe prompt-template sensitivity.
PubMedBERT does not use prompts (direct text-to-label classification).

 
 Evolutionary Prompt Optimization

 
 A zero-shot baseline prompt collapsed to a single predicted label on a 100-example selection subset, which motivated automated prompt search.
The answer-text scorer used at that stage was preliminary: it could omit the first label token when prompt and candidate tokenization differed at their junction.
We used AlphaEvolve  ( Novikov et al. 2025 ) , an LLM-driven program-evolution framework, to run a small evolutionary search that started from this baseline prompt.
At each step, AlphaEvolve proposed a complete candidate prompt by expanding or modifying an earlier one, including its instructions, category definitions, continuation frame, and embedded few-shot demonstrations.
We scored each candidate with BioMistral-7B at FP16 on the 100 labeled PubMed RCT examples using our own multi-objective evaluator, and these scores guided selection.
The search evaluated 29 candidates over ∼ {\sim} 3 hours on one H100 GPU.

 
 AlphaEvolve generated the five demonstrations embedded in each selected prompt as part of the candidate text (generic trial sentences, e.g., “drug X” versus placebo); the 100 labeled examples served only to score candidates and never appear inside a prompt.
However, those 100 examples came from the test split, and selection relied on the preliminary scorer and a single model.
We later switched the development subset to validation data, but this does not restore held-out status for prompts already selected.
Two template families emerged at the Pareto frontier: Struct-Delim (calibration-optimized) and Bare-Cont (accuracy-optimized).
The selected templates combined few-shot examples, category descriptions, and an answer-text-compatible continuation, features that were not present together in any hand-designed template.

 
 Quantization Conditions

 
 We evaluate three quantization conditions. FP16 serves as the half-precision baseline, requiring approximately 14 GB of weight storage for a 7B model, excluding runtime overhead. INT8 uses LLM.int8() mixed-precision decomposition  ( Dettmers et al. 2022 ) via bitsandbytes ; outlier features remain in FP16, while the remaining weights are quantized to 8-bit integers, roughly halving weight storage relative to FP16. INT4 uses NF4 (4-bit NormalFloat) quantization with double quantization  ( Dettmers et al. 2023 ) via bitsandbytes , reducing weight storage by roughly 75% relative to FP16.

 
 Calibration

 
 We apply post-hoc temperature scaling  ( Guo et al. 2017 ) , fitting a scalar temperature T T on n = 200 n=200 validation-set examples (seed 42) to minimize NLL, then applying the fitted temperature to n = 200 n=200 held-out test examples:

 

 
 
 P cal ​ ( ℓ ) = softmax ​ ( score ​ ( ℓ ) T ) P_{\text{cal}}(\ell)=\text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\text{score}(\ell)}{T}\right) 
 
 
 
 A fitted T > 1 T>1 indicates that the induced distribution is more peaked than warranted by accuracy under the selected scorer; T ≈ 1 T\approx 1 indicates the scores are already well-scaled.

 
 Metrics

 
 We evaluate classification performance using accuracy and macro-F1, and calibration using Expected Calibration Error (ECE; 15 equal-width bins), the Brier score, and negative log-likelihood (NLL). Majority-class and class-prior-matched random classifiers provide lower-bound baselines; the latter values are analytical expectations under independent draws from the empirical class distribution.
We computed bootstrap confidence intervals only for the paired accuracy and macro-F1 differences reported in the text.
We report ECE, Brier score, and NLL as point estimates without an uncertainty analysis, so small differences in these metrics should not be read as statistically reliable.

 The Accuracy-Calibration Tradeoff

 
 Across six matched BioMistral-Instruct pairs (two templates × \times three precisions), Instruct achieves modestly higher mean accuracy ( + + 1.4pp) but 2.4 × 2.4{\times} higher mean ECE (0.237 vs. 0.097) under sum scoring.
The accuracy advantage is template-dependent: Instruct leads on Struct-Delim by + + 5.9 to 6.2pp (paired cluster-bootstrap 95% CIs exclude zero) but BioMistral leads on Bare-Cont by + + 2.8 to 3.9pp (CIs: [ 1.1 , 5.5 ] [1.1,5.5] pp).
This template-dependent sign change shows that the comparison between these models is not a simple instruction-tuning effect (see Table  1 ).

 Quantization Stability

 
 Table  2 reports FP16, INT8, and INT4 point estimates for each configuration; selected paired bootstrap intervals are reported below.
For BioMistral and Instruct, INT8 accuracy deltas are ≤ 1.1 {\leq}1.1 percentage points (the largest being BioMistral Bare-Cont at + + 1.05pp).
Where the 95% CI excludes zero, the direction uniformly favors INT8 (e.g., BioMistral Bare-Cont: Δ ​ F1 = + .015 \Delta\text{F1}=+.015 [ + .006 , + .024 ] [+.006,+.024] ; Instruct Struct-Delim: Δ ​ Acc = + .007 \Delta\text{Acc}=+.007 [ + .001 , + .013 ] [+.001,+.013] ).

 
 The base model exhibits a larger INT8 effect on Struct-Delim ( + + 4.2pp).
INT4 (NF4) shows mixed effects: for BioMistral, the INT4 ECE point estimate is lower than at FP16 (Bare-Cont: 0.063 vs. 0.104) with similar accuracy; for Instruct Bare-Cont, INT4 shows a modest accuracy drop ( − - 0.7pp) and a slightly higher ECE point estimate.
Because the calibration metrics have no interval estimates, we do not interpret these ECE differences as reliable improvements or degradations.
INT4 does not show the catastrophic degradation sometimes reported for aggressive quantization, though the heterogeneous pattern warrants per-configuration validation before deployment.

 Failure of Single-Token Class-Code Scoring

 
 Before adopting answer-text scoring, we evaluated four class-code templates in nine interpretable BioMistral template-precision configurations ( n = 2,000 n=2{,}000 each) using restricted softmax over single-letter logits (A to E): FP16 and INT8 for all four templates, plus INT4 for one template.
We excluded an additional label-permutation condition because its outputs could not be interpreted under the intended mapping.
Among the nine retained configurations, five predicted a single class for all examples, and macro-F1 ranged from 0.038 to 0.095.
These results document severe collapse under the evaluated class-code prompts but do not identify whether prompt design, code-position bias, or another factor caused it.
This failure motivated the full-label answer-text protocol used in the main experiments; because the prompts and scoring protocol changed together, the preliminary and main results are not a controlled comparison of scoring methods alone.

 
 Discussion

 
 Scoring Protocol and Calibration

 
 Within this exploratory design, our central observation is that, for the evaluated configurations, templates, and samples, the calibration ranking of BioMistral and Instruct, measured without post-hoc calibration, reverses between summed and mean-token log-likelihood.
Under summed scoring, Instruct appears less well calibrated than BioMistral (average ECE 0.237 vs. 0.097); under mean-token scoring, the order flips.

 
 Temperature is an unresolved alternative explanation for the reversal.
Mean-token normalization divides each candidate’s summed log-likelihood by its token count T ℓ T_{\ell} , which compresses score differences before the softmax.
If all labels had the same length T T , this would equal temperature scaling of the summed scores with temperature T T ; with label lengths of 3 to 5 tokens the correspondence is only approximate, because a label-specific divisor can also reorder candidates.
The reversal may therefore partly reflect a difference in probability sharpness between the two scorers.
The temperatures fitted under summed scoring (Instruct 2.2 2.2 to 4.6 4.6 , near the label-length range; BioMistral 1.3 1.3 to 1.7 1.7 , below it) are compatible with this account but, being fitted on 200 examples under one scorer, do not test it.
We did not fit a temperature for each scorer, so we cannot say whether the reversal would persist, shrink, or disappear after per-scorer calibration.

 
 This observation has a methodological implication: a calibration comparison made under a single scoring protocol may not hold under an alternative protocol that is equally common in practice.
We do not claim that calibration is intrinsically or universally unstable: the primary templates were selected using test-derived data, and the reversal was not tested after per-scorer calibration.

 
 Sources of Variation

 
 Our results suggest a qualitative ordering of effect sizes on this task, although the ranking is itself sensitive to the scoring rule. Among the factors we varied, the scoring rule produced the largest change in apparent calibration: switching from sum to mean log-likelihood reverses the BioMistral-Instruct ranking while barely affecting their accuracy. We include PubMedBERT only as a contextual reference; its supervision, architecture, and evaluation sample all differ from the decoder runs, so we do not attribute its higher accuracy to any single factor.

 
 Across the two primary templates, template choice changes mean accuracy across precisions by ∼ {\sim} 3pp for Instruct, ∼ {\sim} 7pp for BioMistral, and up to ∼ {\sim} 18pp for Base. The Instruct-vs-BioMistral accuracy difference is smaller ( + + 1.4pp on average under sum scoring) and changes sign on Bare-Cont. Quantization generally has the smallest effect for the adapted checkpoints (INT8: ≤ {\leq} 1.1pp), whereas base-model accuracy shifts by up to + + 4.2pp at INT8 and by approximately − - 3.5pp to + + 3.3pp at INT4, depending on the template.

 
 Implications for Evaluation Practice

 
 For biomedical NLP evaluation, calibration comparisons between decoder models should report results under multiple candidate-scoring normalizations (at minimum, summed and mean-token log-likelihood) and identify which conclusions are robust to that choice. Evaluations should likewise treat template design as an experimental variable, not an implementation detail, given the within-pair accuracy swings observed here. INT8 quantization produces small accuracy changes ( ≤ {\leq} 1.1pp) for the adapted checkpoints studied, although the base model shows larger shifts; therefore, equivalence testing with predeclared margins is needed before claiming deployment safety.

 
 Limitations and Future Work

 
 Our evaluation is limited to a single task, PubMed RCT. Generalization to other biomedical classification tasks (e.g., relation extraction and clinical NLI) remains to be validated.
The range of compression methods is also limited: we evaluate only bitsandbytes -based INT8 and NF4 (INT4) quantization. Other approaches, including GPTQ  ( Frantar et al. 2023 ) , AWQ  ( Lin et al. 2024 ) , and knowledge distillation, may exhibit different calibration-accuracy tradeoffs.

 
 The results are sensitive to the verbalizer and scoring rule. Answer-text scoring uses label names of unequal token length (e.g., RESULTS = 3 tokens, CONCLUSIONS = 5), creating a surface-form length prior.
The low Conclusions F1 under sum scoring (Table  6 ) is consistent with, but does not by itself establish, a surface-form length penalty.
Because raw and length-normalized likelihoods can both remain choice-sensitive, robust evaluation should compare multiple scoring normalizations  ( Cho, So, and Lee 2026 ) .
Equal-length verbalizers and label-permutation controls are additional checks for future work.

 
 Figure 1: Scoring-rule sensitivity across three calibration metrics. Each thin line connects a single model-template-precision condition under summed vs. mean-token scoring; diamonds show per-model aggregates. All values are uncalibrated. The BioMistral-Instruct ranking reversal appears in ECE, NLL, and Brier, so it is not specific to ECE binning. 
 
 
 Prompt selection introduces a provenance caveat: we selected Struct-Delim and Bare-Cont in July 2026 on examples from the PubMed RCT test split with a flawed preliminary scorer (see Methods) and did not re-select them afterwards.
Results using these templates are therefore exploratory rather than the product of a clean held-out selection procedure.

 
 The temperature-scaling analysis is further constrained by its sample size (200 validation examples for fitting and 200 test examples for evaluation), which yields noisier ECE estimates and fitted temperatures than the n = 2,000 n=2{,}000 main evaluation.
The fitted temperatures come from a 64-point geometric grid over [ 0.01,100 ] [0.01,100] and reflect that resolution rather than continuous optima.

 
 Finally, the model comparisons are confounded: BioMistral and Instruct differ in more than one training variable (Table  1 ), Instruct was evaluated without its chat template, which may independently affect calibration  ( Sanz-Guerrero, Mager, and von der
Wense 2026 ) , and PubMedBERT differs from the decoders in supervision and evaluation sample.
We evaluate 7B-parameter decoders only; larger models may exhibit different calibration dynamics.

 
 Conclusion

 
 Motivated by resource-constrained local deployment, we evaluated confidence reliability for quantized biomedical language models on public PubMed abstracts. Within an exploratory design affected by test-derived prompt selection and a token-boundary error in the preliminary selection scorer, we observed that scoring normalization reversed the apparent calibration ranking of BioMistral and Instruct, measured without post-hoc calibration, while leaving accuracy largely unchanged; we did not test whether this reversal persists after per-scorer calibration. We also observed that prompt-template choice can substantially alter performance. Quantization effects are model- and template-dependent, with lower-bit quantization producing less consistent behavior. These observations motivate treating prompt design and scoring normalization as first-order experimental decisions and validating compression choices for each deployment setting in clean held-out studies.
