 Abstract

 
 Improving an agent through successive revisions requires balancing exploration and evaluation under a limited budget. A narrow search can miss useful sequences of edits, while fully evaluating every intermediate version often exhausts the budget before those sequences yield a verified improvement. Inspired by the draft–verify principle of speculative decoding, we propose Spec ulative R ecursive S elf- I mprovement ( SpecRSI ), a two-level framework coupling inner-loop agent search with outer-loop improver learning. SpecRSI drafts successors before their predecessors are fully evaluated, while adopting only fully evaluated, admissible versions. In the inner loop, Verification-Aware Tree Search (VATS) allocates the budget between further edits and outstanding checks using the predicted verified return of a continuation. In the outer loop, Group Verification Optimization (GVO) updates the improver from completed searches while keeping the real evaluator fixed. We evaluate SpecRSI on scientific optimization (including active queue management (AQM) for network infrastructure), coding, and general agent-system benchmarks. Under matched evaluation-call budgets, SpecRSI improves scientific terminal gain over Janus by 60.0%–96.3% and area under the budget curve by 32.1%–91.6%, while achieving higher means across all 50 scientific backbone–task pairs and outperforming MetaRSI-v1 by 1.30–2.70 percentage points (pp) in system score across twenty backbones. On instance-level comparisons, it further improves PyBaMM terminal gain by 142.6% and SWE-Pro success by 6.0 pp at 7.0% and 9.2% lower token cost, respectively. These results demonstrate the efficacy of the proposed approach.

 1 Introduction

 
 Agent systems can improve through successive revisions to their prompts, tools, and workflows, and recursive self-improvement (RSI) extends this goal to the mechanisms that generate subsequent improvements. Recent systems revise agent programs ( Zhang et al., 2026a ; Wang et al., 2026b ) , modify the meta-agent that proposes revisions ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) , adapt improvement operators and their scheduling in MetaRSI ( Tan et al., 2026 ) , or co-evolve target programs and proxy evaluators to allocate expensive real evaluations in Janus ( Liu et al., 2026 ) . Across these approaches, proposing changes and establishing their benefits both consume time and computation. This allocation problem is central to Web infrastructure and agentic systems: in active queue management (AQM), revising a controller to manage queue buildup and delay requires assessing traffic delivery, delay, loss, and fairness across diverse scenarios, consuming substantial simulator resources  ( Liu et al., 2026 ; de Almeida et al., 2024 ) . This raises a central question: how should an agent allocate its limited budget between exploring new revisions and verifying their benefits? Useful revisions often support later improvements, yet identifying them requires expensive evaluation; the challenge is turning exploration into verified progress before resources run out (Figure  1 ).

 
 Two limiting strategies expose this trade-off. Following one fully evaluated lineage gives every decision fresh feedback, but narrows the alternatives and places an evaluation barrier between dependent edits—for instance, when a coding-agent revision adds a file–test map that a successor uses for focused intermediate retesting while retaining the final full suite. Maintaining many lineages explores more broadly, but fully evaluating them all spends budget on branches that are never used. Extra workers cut elapsed time without making evaluation free, and under a fixed budget a wider search may not leave enough computation to finish the most useful branch. While prior systems partially relax this barrier by separating expansion from evaluation or validating in parallel ( Wang et al., 2026b ; Hu et al., 2026 ) , we focus on drafting successors ahead of their parents’ full evaluation and jointly selecting further revisions and bundles of outstanding checks under the remaining budget.

 
 Allowing successors to be drafted ahead changes where the budget should be spent next. A candidate that looks strong may still need several checks before it can be adopted, whereas a child that repairs an unhelpful parent may need only one remaining check. Evaluating a version solely by its apparent quality misses the cost of making it usable. Under a hard budget, what matters is a continuation —a sequence of further edits and checks from the current state—and the verified improvement it can still deliver with the remaining budget. Because this return must be estimated before paying for the checks that reveal it, scoring must shift from static candidate quality to the marginal verified return of further editing and checking.

 
 Extending speculation from tokens to agent revisions therefore changes the search space, the verification process, and the learning objective. Agent versions are revisable programs whose successors may repair or build on a parent that is not yet admissible. Verification requires paid, complementary external checks with heterogeneous costs and dependencies; several may be jointly necessary for adoption, so a single target-model pass cannot supply this evidence. Progress must consequently be measured by realized verified gains under resource limits, accounting for all incurred search costs, including failed and unfinished work.

 
 Contributions. We introduce SpecRSI , a framework for budgeted speculative agent improvement. 1   Two-Level Speculative Self-Improvement. We decouple speculative exploration from verified adoption: inner searches extend incompletely evaluated versions but adopt only fully evaluated, admissible ones, while the improver remains frozen within each search and learns from complete-search feedback between rounds. 2   Verification-Aware Tree Search (VATS). VATS allocates the remaining budget across drafting and verification: the Solver preserves affordable completion bundles, including individually low-value checks; the Scorer predicts verified gains attainable through further edits and checks within the remaining budget; and the Policy selects the next draft or verification plan. 3   Group Verification Optimization (GVO). GVO assigns credit across independent complete searches from the same start, using verified gains from searches under matched resource caps to provide delayed credit to sampled edits and work choices for the Drafter and Policy, while training the Scorer on realized suffix returns and paid same-state comparisons.

 3.1 Motivation

 Accurate benefit evaluation is central to guiding RSI toward better agents under a limited search budget. If benefit evaluation is inaccurate, search either overlooks useful multi-step edits or wastes resources on candidates that never become admissible. Effective allocation must therefore evaluate the verified improvement that continued work can deliver.

 
 Estimating this benefit is non-trivial because a candidate’s current quality does not determine the value of continuing from it, due to two challenges. 1   Benefit evaluation should reflect downstream verified improvement. Intermediate revisions may serve as stepping stones to later gains, but realizing those gains requires subsequent edits and real checks. 2   Benefit evaluation should account for completion costs. Complementary checks, planning, and failed attempts all draw from the same finite resources, so even promising gains may be too costly to verify. Together, these challenges induce a performance–cost trade-off: a worthwhile continuation must offer sufficient verified improvement while remaining affordable to complete.  3.3 Inner-loop Search with VATS

 
 Within each inner search, Verification-Aware Tree Search (VATS) iteratively selects bounded work ω \omega under expense and attempt caps: either DRAFT ​ ( P , h ) \textsc{DRAFT}(P,h) , where P = ( P a ) a = 1 | P | P=(P_{a})_{a=1}^{|P|} indexes existing parent versions and h ≥ 1 h\geq 1 bounds edit depth, or VERIFY ​ ( 𝒥 ) \textsc{VERIFY}(\mathcal{J}) , where 𝒥 \mathcal{J} specifies verification jobs on existing versions. Either action may repeat until ACCEPT terminates the search. Because complementary checks cannot be scheduled by token-confidence ranking alone, VATS coordinates three modules under the frozen improver: the Solver constructs feasible completion bundles, the Scorer refines verification scope, and the Policy selects the next action before execution updates the state for replanning (Appendix  D ).

 3.4 Outer-loop Learning with GVO

 
 Outer-loop training begins with supervised warm-up and then alternates between frozen-improver search collection and Group Verification Optimization (GVO) updates. Completed trajectories provide two supervision signals derived strictly from observed outcomes under the frozen improver rather than updated Scorer predictions: realized suffix returns train the Scorer (when return or pair records are present), and whole-search advantages train the Drafter and Policy over sampled choices (with empty empirical loss terms contributing zero).

 
 3.4.1 Supervised Warm-up

 
 Prior to the first collection round, supervised warm-up initializes Θ 0 \Theta_{0} by training Drafter θ \theta to imitate executed legal edits, Policy ψ \psi to imitate legal plan choices, and Scorer ϕ \phi to predict observed returns; unexecuted alternatives receive no labels. Throughout training, Solver feasibility rules and menu settings ν \nu remain fixed, and neither Solver outputs nor environment outcomes carry policy likelihoods.

 Evaluation Metrics. 
We report terminal gain ( Δ K \Delta_{K} ) and area under the budget curve (AUBC) on scientific tasks following Janus  ( Liu et al., 2026 ) , alongside task scores and mean inference cost ( C C ).
Token-accounted searches track initial and remaining budgets B 0 B_{0} and B t ≥ 0 B_{t}\geq 0 (in k tokens) separately from the evaluation-call limit K K , which is fixed across scientific methods at K = 140,130,160,135,120 K=140,130,160,135,120 for PyBaMM, AQM, FDTD, Reactor, and IRP, respectively. Each call executes one instance’s full real-evaluation suite (counting failed runs, but excluding cached and proxy evaluations). Let s b s_{b} denote the held-out score of the complete admissible incumbent selected on validation after b b calls (with held-out scores never used during search selection), and let Δ b = s b − s 0 \Delta_{b}=s_{b}-s_{0} . We compute

 

 
 
 Δ K = s K − s 0 , AUBC = 1 2 ​ ∑ b = 1 K ( Δ b − 1 + Δ b ) , C = ∑ i = 1 M T i 10 3 ​ M , \Delta_{K}=s_{K}-s_{0},\quad\mathrm{AUBC}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{b=1}^{K}(\Delta_{b-1}+\Delta_{b}),\quad C=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{M}T_{i}}{10^{3}M}, 
 
 (15) 
 
 
 where M M is the number of evaluated instances and T i T_{i} sums all input and output inference tokens over instance i i ’s full execution, including failed or unfinished attempts. Thus C C measures online inference cost in k tokens per task (whereas Figure  2 A counts tokens up to the first qualifying outcome or termination, and seed totals or training-token costs are listed separately; Appendix  C.1 ). Scientific gains use dimensionless task scores (Appendix  B ), and AUBC integrates gain over calls by carrying the final value forward to K K after early stopping. Task scores are reported as accuracy or success percentages (with absolute differences in percentage points, pp, and relative gains over the named baseline); higher gain, AUBC, and score and lower cost indicate better performance. Unless stated otherwise, we average three searches per training seed and report mean ± \pm SD across five seed means.

 4.3 VATS Ablations (RQ2)

 
 In this section, we dissect verification scope and planning in VATS on PyBaMM (Figure  6 ).
 1   Bundles improve accepted outcomes. 
With full-state inputs and draft-ahead disabled, bundled verification raises accepted gain from 4.8 to 6.3 pp and hit rate (the share of 125 searches returning a complete, admissible agent with positive gain) from 84% to 92%, while reducing search cost from 47.96 to 44.99 k tokens.
 2   Draft-ahead and bundles yield the best mean outcomes. 
In the 2 × 2 2\times 2 factorial comparison, combining draft-ahead with bundled checks achieves the highest accepted gain (6.02 pp) and hit rate (92%) alongside the lowest cost (47.64 k tokens).
 3   Planning improves the gain–cost trade-off. 
Full SpecRSI achieves the highest mean gain and lowest mean cost across all eight budget caps; at the 70% cap, it reaches 6.00 versus 4.73 pp for additive MILP planning while using 44.76 versus 49.29 k tokens (9.2% less).
Under matched inputs, scoring continuation return (CR) also improves gain by 1.32 pp over candidate-quality scoring (CQ) at 7.4% lower search cost (Figure  S2 A,B; Appendix  C.2 ).

 4.5 Resource Trade-offs (RQ4)

 
 In this section, we examine candidate-plan limits, lifecycle costs, and parallel execution (Table  2 ).
 1   More plans yield diminishing gains. 
Raising the candidate-plan limit from 8 to 32 increases gain from 5.2 to 6.3 pp, whereas doubling it to 64 adds only 0.2 pp while increasing planning cost from 3.9 to 5.6 k tokens (Table  2 A).
 2   SpecRSI uses fewer lifecycle tokens. 
SpecRSI achieves lower mean token usage across all seven lifecycle categories, reducing full-run expenditure (including training and serialized copies) from 112.7 k tokens for MetaRSI-v1 to 106.3 k tokens ( − 5.7 % -5.7\% ; Table  2 B).
 3   Parallel workers reduce latency but use more worker time. 
Scaling from one to eight concurrent workers cuts elapsed wall-clock time from 1,287 to 192 s ( 6.7 × 6.7\times ) at the cost of 19.0% more total worker time, while twenty workers further reduce latency to 100 s ( 12.9 × 12.9\times ) with 55.2% more worker time as mean gain rises from 6.3 to 6.7 pp (Table  2 C).

 
 5 Conclusion

 
 In this work, we present SpecRSI, whose two-level loop extends incompletely evaluated parents but adopts
only complete, admissible versions.
VATS allocates edits and complementary checks by predicted verified continuation
return; GVO updates the improver from completed searches under a fixed
verification contract.
SpecRSI improves terminal-gain and AUBC means over Janus in all 50 scientific backbone–task pairs
and exceeds MetaRSI-v1 by 1.30–2.70 pp in score across
twenty backbones.
Full planning outperforms six planning controls in mean held-out gain and
mean total token cost across all eight budget caps.
Starting-artifact controls support gains from improver updates beyond
retaining stronger task agents.
Instance-level comparisons show higher PyBaMM gain and SWE-Pro success with lower token cost.
These results show that planning verification and learning the improver jointly improve agent performance and token efficiency.

 Appendix A Extended Related Work

 
 Recursive self-improvement. 
Recent agent-optimization frameworks—including DGM, SICA, ADAS, AFlow, and GEPA—iteratively refine agent programs, workflows, or prompts  ( Zhang et al., 2026a ; Robeyns et al., 2025 ; Hu et al., 2025 ; Zhang et al., 2025 ; Agrawal et al., 2026 ) .
To guide multi-step evolution, HGM expands lineages using descendant outcomes while scheduling evaluations separately  ( Wang et al., 2026b ) , Hyperagents directly modifies the meta-agent  ( Zhang et al., 2026b ) , MetaRSI adapts improvement operators and their scheduling  ( Tan et al., 2026 ) , and Janus co-evolves target programs with screening proxies under real-validation gates  ( Liu et al., 2026 ) .
By contrast, SpecRSI learns both successor drafting and verification allocation directly from the net verified returns of complete searches.

 
 Speculative decoding. 
Standard speculative decoding accelerates generation by verifying cheap draft tokens in parallel with a target model  ( Leviathan et al., 2023 ) .
In agentic settings, FlashEvolve asynchronously overlaps evolution stages  ( Hu et al., 2026 ) .
SpecRSI instead brings speculation to persistent agent revisions: only adopted versions require complete verification , whereas intermediate ancestors may remain partially evaluated.

 
 Learning for agent improvement. 
Whereas GRPO estimates group-relative advantages across responses to the same prompt  ( Shao et al., 2024 ) and MAS 2 trains meta-agents via Collaborative Tree Optimization  ( Wang et al., 2026a ) , GVO assigns credit across independent complete searches launched from matched starting states.
Its search-level returns reward verified gains net of all incurred costs—including preparation as well as failed or unfinished branches—using realized suffix returns to train the Scorer and whole-search advantages to update the Drafter and Policy.
